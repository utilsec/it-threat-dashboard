Top Story

A leaked cloud key let attackers rewrite Brevo’s own scripts for four hours — and the newsletter sign-up forms and chat widgets on charity websites were the delivery route

Brevo, the email-marketing platform formerly called Sendinblue and a common choice for supporter newsletters and sign-up forms, has confirmed that attackers served malicious code through its content-delivery network on 14 September. According to Brevo and to e-commerce security firm Sansec, which disclosed the attack on 16 September, the attackers found a long-lived Cloudflare API key with full account permissions hardcoded in Brevo’s source code and used it to create a malicious Cloudflare Worker that rewrote responses at the network edge. For roughly four hours (Brevo gives 16:07–20:30 UTC), the sdk-loader.js and brevo-conversations.js scripts that websites embed pulled in code from attacker-controlled subdomains of sendibt1.com, across Brevo assets including sibforms.com, where its forms are hosted. Sansec puts the number of exposed sites above 100,000. Ordinary visitors were shown a fake “verify you are human” page telling them to paste and run a command — the ClickFix technique. Visitors logged in to WordPress as an administrator had the script try to install a plugin called “Web Media Optimizer” as a persistent backdoor. Brevo says the Worker stripped headers such as Content-Security-Policy at the edge, so its origin files were never modified and its integrity checks saw nothing. A linked incident on 10 September gave attackers access to 138 Brevo customer accounts through a mis-scoped single sign-on path. The lesson for this sector: a third-party script on your donation or sign-up page runs with your site’s authority, and the person most likely to browse it while logged in as administrator is your own web volunteer.

Medium Incident

Ransomware hit the vendor-run payroll system behind 48 Singapore mosques and four madrasahs — staff bank details were in it, and salaries still went out

The Islamic Religious Council of Singapore (MUIS) has confirmed a ransomware incident affecting SmartHRMS, a human-resources and payroll system operated by the vendor Avelogic, after threat activity was detected on 30–31 August. Those affected are 48 mosques, four madrasahs, the Islamic Learning Hub and Management Office, and Mosque-Madrasah-Wakaf Shared Services. The system held staff names, contact details, salaries and bank account numbers, and the data in it was encrypted. MUIS said on 17 September that there is no evidence a large amount of data was taken from the system, that no ransom was paid, that a police report has been made, and that alternative payroll arrangements mean salaries were not disrupted; the Cyber Security Agency of Singapore is aware and has offered assistance. Avelogic’s own incident notice, last updated 14 September, did not name its customers, and no ransomware group has been identified or has claimed the attack. The shape is the familiar one for faith organisations: congregations rarely run their own HR systems, so a shared-services body or a sector vendor runs one for dozens of them, and one intrusion at the vendor reaches all of them at once. What MUIS evidently had ready is what most small organisations have never tested — a way to pay people when the payroll system is gone. What remains is that bank details were in the system, and “no evidence of large exfiltration” is not the same as none, so payment-diversion and impersonation fraud aimed at those staff is the realistic follow-on risk.

Confirmed — MUIS confirmed the ransomware incident and the affected entities; large-scale data theft not confirmed (MUIS: no evidence); no group named or claiming No CVE — the vendor has not disclosed how the attackers got in
No patch applies — vendor assurance, payroll continuity and staff fraud warnings Action: Find out which vendor holds your payroll and HR data, and whether that system is shared with sister congregations, a diocese, a federation or a shared-services body. Ask the vendor in writing how and how quickly it would tell you about an incident, and whether it keeps offline backups it has actually restored from. Write down how you would pay staff for one payroll cycle if the system disappeared tomorrow: last month’s figures held offline, a bank payment template, and who is authorised to approve it. If a provider holding your staff’s bank details reports an incident, warn staff directly that fraudsters may contact them using those details, and make it a rule that any change to an employee’s bank account is confirmed by a call to a number already on file, never by email.

Autistici/Inventati, a volunteer-run Italian technology collective that provides privacy-focused infrastructure to civil-society groups, decided on 6 September to dissolve and close all of its services, and its press page gave 18 September as the scheduled shutdown date, with instructions for users to export their data. What disappears is working infrastructure: roughly 16,000 email addresses, 1,500 websites, 5,500 mailing lists and around 10,000 blogs. For the organisations relying on those services this is an availability event with a fixed deadline, no incident response available and no vendor to escalate to — plus a credential problem for anyone who reused a Noblogs password elsewhere. Status on 20 September: the scheduled date has passed, and we could not confirm whether the switch-off went ahead as planned, so this card does not say it has.

High Incident

A WordPress admin plugin was backdoored through its own update channel — sites that updated on 14 September may now have a hidden administrator

The developer of Admin Menu Editor Pro, a premium WordPress plugin, published an incident notice on 14 September after an attacker gained access to the server hosting adminmenueditor.com and pushed a malicious version 2.35 through the plugin’s normal update mechanism, roughly between 06:00 and 13:00 UTC. The replacement version 2.36, released later that day, was also compromised. The backdoor drops a web shell at includes/wp-user-consent.php, creates a /wp-content/object-cache/ directory and wp_ocache database options, and adds a hidden administrator account whose username begins “wp_”. The vendor counts about 230 customers affected and around 300 more possibly affected; version 2.34 is confirmed clean. Updated 19 September: a clean version 2.37, built from 2.34, has been released through a temporary download that checks your licence key, while adminmenueditor.com is offline and its servers are rebuilt on new infrastructure; the investigation is ongoing, and sites still on 2.34 do not need to update. There is no CVE because nothing in the plugin’s code was the weakness — the vendor’s update channel was. It belongs on this page because a WordPress site built by an agency or a volunteer and left to update itself is a common arrangement for small organisations, and it is the site that carries the donation page and the supporter sign-up form. An automatic update that lands overnight is exactly the change nobody inside the organisation sees.

Confirmed — the developer’s notice confirms the malicious 2.35 and 2.36 releases; customer counts from the vendor (~230 affected, ~300 possibly affected) No CVE — supply-chain compromise of the vendor’s update server, not a plugin vulnerability
Remediation — restore from a backup made before 14 Sep 2026, or remove the plugin and its artifacts; 2.34 is clean and a clean 2.37 shipped on 19 Sep 2026 Action: Ask whoever looks after your website — agency, freelancer or volunteer — whether Admin Menu Editor Pro is installed, which version, and when it last updated. If it received 2.35 or 2.36, treat the site as compromised: restore from a backup taken before 14 September, or remove the plugin and delete the includes/wp-user-consent.php web shell, the object-cache directory, the wp_ocache database options and any administrator account beginning “wp_” that nobody recognises; reinstall only the clean 2.37, checking its SHA-256 against the value in the vendor’s notice (3a39a83dda629328602dabdba92f6ea0225eb10ff584ccbce15482aa2a420e6e). Then change every WordPress administrator password and rotate the hosting, database and payment-integration credentials stored on the site, because a web shell can read them. Keep a note of what was found; if supporter data sits on that site, your data-protection obligations may apply.

Critical Incident

The Magento zero-day backdooring online stores now has a CVE, an emergency patch and a place in CISA’s KEV catalog

Sansec, the Dutch e-commerce forensics firm, reported on 5 September that a previously unknown flaw it names StyleSmuggler is being used to plant backdoors in Magento Open Source and Adobe Commerce stores. Sansec describes it as giving an unauthenticated attacker remote code execution with no user interaction, delivered in two stages: PHP code is smuggled in through the platform, then executed by triggering a built-in scheduled email job. The implant that follows disguises itself as a system process, re-establishes itself through cron, and reads session data. Sansec observed exploitation from 4 September. The position has since changed for the better: the flaw is now tracked as CVE-2026-75650 at CVSS 10.0, affecting all versions of Adobe Commerce and Magento Open Source, Adobe has shipped an out-of-band patch and confirmed exploitation in the wild against merchants, and CISA added it to the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog on 8 September. Attackers used it to deploy a Rust backdoor and a PHP web shell; the injection path abuses Magento’s template system through a “Payment Transaction Failed Reminder” email. The same Adobe release fixed over 170 vulnerabilities. CrowdSec telemetry published on 14 September counted 2,760 exploit signals between 9 and 13 September, peaking at 1,303 on 11 September, from some 500 unique attacking addresses; volume has since halved but still runs more than ten times the first day’s level, and there is still no published count of compromised stores. Nonprofits reach Magento by a predictable route — a charity shop moved online, merchandise, Christmas cards, event ticketing, membership fulfilment — usually built years ago by an agency that is no longer on retainer, which means nobody is watching for an advisory that has not been written yet. If your store takes card payments, the exposure is not only your own data.

Critical Incident

Attackers are taking over MikroTik routers through internet-facing SSH — the cheapest capable box in the sector

CERT Polska, Poland’s national CERT, published confirmation on 5 September that vulnerabilities it disclosed in MikroTik RouterOS are being exploited in the wild, chained into an attack it calls MikroTrick. The chain gives an unauthenticated attacker administrative control of a router whose SSH service is reachable from the internet: a flaw in how RouterOS verified the public key presented during SSH authentication provides the entry point, and improper handling of disallowed characters in the username then escalates that session to full administrator. A third issue affects the bandwidth-test service. CERT Polska reports attacks observed since at least 2 September. Updated: CISA added two of the flaws, CVE-2026-67277 (missing authentication for a critical function) and CVE-2026-86060 (argument injection), to the KEV catalog on 10 September with a 13 September federal deadline. MikroTik lists fixes in RouterOS 7.24.2, 7.23.4 and 6.49.21, with 7.24.3 and 7.23.6 also covering CVE-2026-67278 according to CERT Polska, and says the upgrade runs a compromise check that writes a critical log entry if the device has been tampered with. This matters to nonprofits not because MikroTik is glamorous but because of who owns one. It is the router that ends up in small charities, community organisations, faith groups, hospices and overseas field offices precisely because it does serious routing for very little money — frequently installed once by a volunteer, a trustee’s relative or a contractor who has long since moved on, and then never touched again. The exposure is not a misconfiguration you made; it is a device nobody has logged into for years with management left open to the internet. If the router falls, everything behind it is reachable and every DNS lookup on your network is the attacker’s to steer.

High Campaign

UK, US and Dutch agencies warn that Iranian spyware is being planted on dissidents’ and journalists’ computers through fake tech support and fake apps

The UK’s National Cyber Security Centre, the FBI and the Netherlands’ AIVD issued a joint alert on 15 September about CHOSEN BRICK, Windows spyware used by a Tehran-backed actor active since at least 2025 against dissidents and journalists. Delivery is social rather than technical: the operators approach targets on social media posing as a contact or as technical support, then persuade them to install what looks like Pictory, RunwayML, Norton, Telegram or Adobe Flash. Once installed, CHOSEN BRICK harvests contacts, email, social-media accounts and Telegram and WhatsApp data, takes screenshots, records through the microphone, deletes and downloads files, and can wipe the entire system. The agencies note that stolen data has appeared on pro-Iranian leak sites, which puts victims’ physical safety at risk. Separately, Group-IB reported on 17 September that the “Handala Hack” persona is tied to a Telegram-controlled backdoor it calls HEAVYGRAM, and attributes the activity to Void Manticore, a group linked to Iran’s Ministry of Intelligence, with targets including Iran International journalists and opposition groups; its tooling includes a utility that adds Microsoft Defender exclusions. For human-rights groups, diaspora organisations, exile media and the funders and NGOs that work alongside them, this is the threat model in plain terms: no exploit, just a believable message and an installer. The recommended mitigations are ordinary ones — phishing-resistant MFA, application allowlisting, automatic updates and endpoint monitoring — which is exactly why they are worth doing for the people most likely to be approached.

Active — joint government alert from NCSC, FBI and AIVD; the Handala/HEAVYGRAM link to Void Manticore is Group-IB’s assessment No CVE — social engineering and fake installers, not a software flaw

Critical Campaign

A nation-state zero-day chain is being aimed at NGOs directly — three Chrome and Windows zero-days, delivered through a hijacked university website

Volexity disclosed on 9 September, with a detailed public write-up following on 15 September, a spear-phishing campaign that began on 1 September against multiple non-governmental organisations, attributed with moderate confidence to the China-linked actor tracked as UTA0560. The targeting is the point: NGOs were the intended victims, not collateral damage from something aimed elsewhere. Recipients were sent links pointing at a legitimate US university website, which the actor had compromised through a reflected cross-site scripting flaw and used purely as a redirect — so the domain in the email read as academic and trustworthy, and the university itself was infrastructure rather than target. Anyone who followed the link was driven through a chain of three zero-days: CVE-2026-85046 in Chrome’s V8 JavaScript engine and CVE-2026-87491 in its WebAssembly handling to compromise the browser, then CVE-2026-85880, a Windows kernel flaw in ALPC, to take full control of the machine. The payload is GRIMWEDGE, a JScript backdoor that runs in memory inside the Windows Installer process and supports reconnaissance, file and process management and command execution; updated 16 September: a second China-linked actor, tracked as JungleBamboo and associated with APT31, used the same exploit chain to install LONGTALE (also known as GemStone), a credential-stealing Chrome extension. Researchers also note a patch gap — fixes for the browser bugs existed in the open-source Chromium tree before they reached stable Chrome, and the campaign ran inside that window. All three flaws are now patched, and all three are in CISA’s KEV catalog. No new activity or victims were reported between 15 and 20 September. No organisation defends against a browser-plus-kernel zero-day chain by patching faster than the attacker; what it can do is keep the window short, isolate the people most likely to be targeted, and know who in the organisation those people are.

Patched — current Chrome stable builds and the September 2026 Windows cumulative updates carry all three fixes Action: Start with the browser, because that is where the chain begins. Tell every member of staff, volunteer and trustee to fully close and reopen Chrome today — the update only applies on relaunch — and do the same for Edge, Brave, Opera and Vivaldi, which share the engine. Because one of the two groups installed a malicious Chrome extension, have higher-risk staff review their browser extensions and remove anything they do not recognise. Confirm Windows machines have installed the September updates and actually restarted. Then do the part that matters more than patching: write down who in your organisation is a plausible target for a state actor — people working on human rights, sanctions, diaspora communities, investigations or anything a government would rather you did not publish — and treat their devices differently. That means a managed, up-to-date laptop rather than a personal one, phishing-resistant multi-factor authentication, and somewhere to report a suspicious email that is a person, not a policy. Tell those staff, plainly, that they are targets; people who know they are targeted behave differently. If a device is suspected, take it off the network and get help rather than reimaging it, because the forensic evidence is the only way you will learn what was taken.

High Campaign

Attackers are phoning IT help desks to take over senior staff accounts — and a small charity’s “help desk” is usually one trusting colleague

Arctic Wolf published research on 7 September 2026 on a data-theft and extortion cluster it tracks as PREY-0058. The method is voice phishing aimed at IT help desks: the caller impersonates a director or vice-president and asks for a password reset or a multi-factor re-enrolment. Once that succeeds, the operators use adversary-in-the-middle infrastructure to steal the session token and sign in through residential proxies, so the login looks ordinary, then work through Microsoft 365 and connected SaaS applications taking data for extortion. Arctic Wolf notes tradecraft overlapping Mandiant’s UNC6671. Small organisations should not read the corporate framing and assume this misses them. In most charities there is no help desk — there is an operations manager, a part-time IT volunteer or an outsourced provider, and the same social pressure applies with more force, because refusing a trustee or a chief executive feels like obstruction. Two details are worth carrying away. Residential proxies defeat the country-blocking and impossible-travel rules many small tenants rely on, so geography will not flag it. And because the attacker steals a live session token, resetting the password afterwards does not evict them unless sessions are explicitly revoked.

Two exploited Chrome V8 zero-days in a fortnight — and both turn out to be links in the chain aimed at NGOs

Google shipped an emergency Chrome update on 4 September for CVE-2026-85046, a type-confusion flaw in the V8 JavaScript engine exploited in the wild, fixed in builds 152.0.7977.82 and .83. It followed on 9 September with a second emergency fix for CVE-2026-87491, an out-of-bounds write in V8 that allows a sandbox escape from a crafted web page, fixed in 153.0.8010.36 and .37 — Chrome’s seventh in-the-wild zero-day of 2026. Both are now known to be the browser half of the GRIMWEDGE exploit chain that a China-linked actor used against NGOs from 1 September, which makes this the rare Chrome advisory with a named victim profile that matches this page. Researchers described a patch gap: the underlying fixes landed in the open-source Chromium tree before they reached stable Chrome, giving anyone watching that tree a window. Google withholds technical detail until most users have updated. For nonprofits the browser is not one application among many, it is the operating estate — the CRM, the fundraising platform, the grant portal, the payroll bureau, the case-management system and the bank all live in tabs, often on a laptop that is shared, personally owned, or managed by nobody. Chrome only applies its update on relaunch, and the machines furthest behind belong to the volunteer whose browser has not been closed since spring.

Low Guidance

CISA publishes guidance on decoys and tripwires for small security teams — one of the few detection controls a charity can realistically run

CISA published guidance on 17 September encouraging critical-infrastructure operators, and small and medium organisations new to deception, to deploy cyber decoys: honeytokens and tripwires placed in high-value areas as fake files, fake accounts and fake credentials that no legitimate user should ever touch, so that any use of one is a reliable alarm. The guidance recommends mapping decoy coverage against MITRE ATT&CK and MITRE Engage and refining it through threat emulation, and it positions deception as a supplement to Zero Trust rather than a replacement for it. It is guidance, not regulation, and places no obligations on anyone. It belongs on the nonprofit page because this sector’s detection gap is structural: most organisations have nobody watching logs, and alerting that fires constantly is alerting that nobody reads. A decoy inverts that — it stays silent until the moment it matters. A document with a tempting name in the finance share, a dormant administrator account that should never sign in, or a fake credential in a password vault or code repository, each wired to alert a named person, costs little and is well suited to the quiet post-compromise browsing described in the help-desk vishing items on this page. The caveat is the same as for any control: a tripwire that alerts an inbox nobody monitors is decoration.

No patch — low-cost detection practice Action: Pick three decoys this month and keep them simple: a plausibly named document in your most sensitive shared folder, an unused administrator account with sign-in alerting switched on, and a fake credential stored where an intruder would look for real ones. Route every alert to a named person and a named deputy rather than a shared inbox, and trigger each decoy once to prove the alert arrives. Tell your IT provider the decoys exist, in writing, so nobody tidies them away or trips them during routine work.
