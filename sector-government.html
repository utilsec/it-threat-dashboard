Attackers are installing a Linux rootkit on F5 BIG-IP APM devices, hiding a web shell in memory where file-based checks will not find it

Reporting on 8 September describes compromises of F5 BIG-IP Access Policy Manager appliances in which a Linux rootkit hooks PHP file loading and injects a web shell directly into memory. Nothing malicious lands on disk, so integrity checking and scanning come back clean while the attacker keeps access. BIG-IP APM is remote-access and authentication infrastructure, and it sits at the edge of a great many government networks — frequently as the device staff and contractors authenticate through to reach internal systems. A compromised appliance therefore sees the sessions and credentials passing through it. Agencies should treat a confirmed case as a credential-exposure event with a rebuild and rotation task attached, not as a malware clean-up, and should check whether appliance logs are forwarded anywhere the appliance itself cannot reach.

High Ransomware

A Minnesota county paid a $128,000 ransom in January — and a different group hit it again three months later

Winona County, Minnesota has confirmed that it paid $128,539 to recover from a ransomware attack in January 2026, and that a second, separate ransomware attack struck the county in April. County Administrator Maureen Holte said roughly $50,000 of the payment came from insurance and about $78,000 from the county levy — that is, from local taxpayers — and described the decision as taken on advice, to restore services and protect residents’ personal information. County officials have said the two incidents were the work of different criminal groups. Emergency services continued to operate through both, but other county functions fell back to paper. The uncomfortable detail is the sequence. Paying a ransom buys a decryption key and, sometimes, a deletion promise; it does not remediate the access path, and it does not make an organisation a less attractive target. Nothing in the public record suggests the second group used the first group’s access, but the second attack landed inside the window in which the county was still recovering from the first — which is exactly when monitoring, change control and staff attention are weakest. For small counties and municipalities the transferable lesson is about the recovery period rather than the payment decision: the months after an incident are the months you are most likely to be hit again, and that is when hardening investment has to land.

Confirmed — two separate ransomware incidents, Jan and Apr 2026, attributed by the county to different groups No CVE disclosed — initial access vector not made public
No patch applicable — action is post-incident hardening and monitoring during recovery Action: Write a post-incident hardening plan that runs for at least six months after recovery, not six weeks, and fund it before the incident rather than after. Concretely: force a full credential and service-account rotation including anything cached on rebuilt hosts, re-enrol MFA rather than trusting existing enrolments, and keep enhanced logging and EDR alerting at a raised sensitivity for the whole window. Verify that restored systems were rebuilt rather than reverted — a restored image can carry the same weakness back. If your county is considering payment, get the legal, insurance and disclosure positions in writing in advance; several states now impose reporting or restriction requirements on public bodies. Rehearse paper fallback for benefits, permits, deeds and dispatch, and confirm emergency services can run on isolated systems.

A New Hampshire county spends a week on paper after a network security incident cuts dispatch access to records

Merrimack County, New Hampshire fell back to manual processes for around a week following a network security incident that began in late August 2026, with services still degraded into early September. Reporting by the Concord Monitor described disruption to county functions including the Register of Deeds and dispatch access to records systems. The county has not published an attribution, a ransomware brand or an initial access vector, and no CVE has been associated with the incident. It is a small case and deliberately included as one: the pattern of a mid-sized county losing records access, running deeds and dispatch on paper for days, and giving little public technical detail is the most common shape of a government cyber incident in the United States, and it very rarely produces a national headline. The operational lessons sit in the fallback rather than the intrusion. Deeds recording has statutory timing consequences, and dispatch access to criminal-history and warrant data has officer-safety consequences; both need a written, rehearsed manual procedure that does not assume any county network is available, and a documented catch-up process for the backlog once systems return. Counties that have never timed how long a paper day actually takes tend to discover the answer during the incident.

CISA advisory AA26-251A warns of industrial-scale AI model distillation aimed at US AI developers and federal agencies

CISA issued advisory AA26-251A on 8 September warning that China-based AI companies are running knowledge-distillation campaigns at industrial scale to extract proprietary functionality from models built by US AI companies. High Nation-State

A new APT28-linked backdoor hides its command channel inside a legitimate webhook service, targeting European government and diplomatic bodies

Recorded Future’s Insikt Group has documented a previously unreported Windows backdoor it calls HOOKEDGE, used against government and diplomatic organisations in Romania, Spain and Türkiye between September 2025 and April 2026. Insikt attributes the activity with moderate confidence to the Russian state-sponsored group it tracks as BlueDelta, more widely known as APT28 or Fancy Bear, and describes the implant as a direct evolutionary successor to the earlier HEADLACE backdoor, sharing code architecture and the same habit of abusing legitimate webhook services. Delivery is a macro-enabled Word document with a diplomatic-themed lure; the macro writes six files into the user profile directory and starts the installation chain, which establishes a scheduled task that runs every thirty minutes and then deletes the installer files. Command and control, payload staging and exfiltration all run through webhook[.]site. The backdoor pulls .cmd payloads using headless Microsoft Edge in hidden mode, executes them, and returns results inside HTML files. Deployment is two-stage: broad reconnaissance at a thirty-minute beacon, then a second-stage payload at a five-minute interval for hands-on operator control against selected targets. No CVE is involved — the entry point is a macro and a user. That is what makes it relevant beyond the three named countries: nothing here needs a vulnerability, and nothing here needs infrastructure a blocklist would already know.

Active espionage campaign — observed Sep 2025 to Apr 2026 against government and diplomatic targets; attribution to APT28/BlueDelta is moderate confidence No CVE — initial access is a macro-enabled document, not a software vulnerability
No patch applicable — mitigation is macro policy, scheduled-task monitoring and egress control on webhook services Action: Block macros in Office files that originate from the internet by policy rather than by prompt, and verify the policy actually applies to the diplomatic and ministerial mailboxes most likely to receive a themed lure — those users are often the ones with an exception. Two delivery routes matter for public-sector readers. The first is a public-sector and water-focused pilot with the Multi-State Information Sharing and Analysis Center, providing training and hands-on support for state, local, tribal and territorial cyber defenders; MS-ISAC is a channel that most US state and local bodies already have a relationship with, which lowers the barrier considerably. The second is a partner network of more than 35 enterprise products and services. This is a vendor programme rather than a government one, and it should be evaluated as such: the offer is real and the target population is genuinely under-resourced, but subsidised access has an end date, and the data-handling terms for anything a defender feeds into a model deserve scrutiny before rather than after adoption.
