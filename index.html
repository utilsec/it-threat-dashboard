Top Story

Brevo’s own embedded scripts were rewritten at the CDN edge to serve ClickFix lures and a WordPress backdoor — Sansec estimates more than 100,000 sites were exposed

Email-marketing platform Brevo, formerly Sendinblue, has confirmed a two-stage compromise that ended with its own customer-embedded scripts serving malware. On 14 September, between about 16:07 and 20:30 UTC, attackers used a long-lived Cloudflare API key with full account permissions that had been hardcoded in Brevo’s application source code to deploy a Cloudflare Worker that rewrote responses at the CDN edge. The Worker altered brevo.com, sendinblue.com, the login and account hosts and sibforms.com form hosting. It also altered the forms, Conversations chat widget and SDK loader scripts that customers embed in their own sites, and it stripped Content-Security-Policy headers along the way. Ordinary visitors to those sites were shown a fake “Cloudflare, verify you are human” ClickFix page telling them to paste and run a command. For visitors logged in as WordPress administrators, the script tried to install a malicious plugin, “Web Media Optimizer”, as a persistent backdoor with a hardcoded authentication bypass. Sansec, which first reported the attack, estimates that more than 100,000 websites were likely exposed; Brevo has not published its own count. Brevo says the key was first misused in late August, but it found no injection into customer-facing pages before 14 September. Its origin servers were never modified, and because the change happened at the edge, “standard integrity checks did not detect the change”. The first stage, on 10 September, was separate. An improperly scoped SAML single sign-on path gave the attacker access to 138 Brevo customer accounts; contacts were exported from 43 of them and phishing was sent from six. This story leads the edition ahead of Cisco’s exploited ISE zero-day because it is a confirmed compromise rather than a vulnerability, and because it reached every organisation that pasted a vendor’s script tag into its site: retailers, charities, schools and law firms alike. Brevo has revoked the key, removed the hardcoded credentials, deleted the attacker’s hostnames and purged its edge caches. It cannot clean the WordPress sites that received the plugin, or the computers of visitors who ran the command.

Revolut disclosed on 14 September that an unauthorised party impersonated a government agency and, using what the company describes as valid technical domain credentials, submitted fraudulent data requests that its process treated as legitimate. The exposed fields for the affected subset of customers are unusually complete: names, addresses, dates of birth, copies of driving licences and passports, identity-verification selfies, IBANs, account statements and full transaction history including cryptocurrency activity. Revolut says customer funds and core systems were not affected, has blocked the attacker’s email, and has notified law enforcement, data-protection authorities and financial regulators while contacting affected users directly. The number of people affected has not been disclosed. Update, 17–18 September: SecurityWeek and American Banker have reported the scale and the route in, drawing on Hudson Rock and other investigators rather than on Revolut. They put the affected group at about 680 high-value customers, none of them in the US. The forged requests ran for five to six months from an Italian Ministry of the Interior certified-email (PEC) account compromised by infostealer malware, and went to Revolut Bank UAB in Lithuania. What makes this worth the space on an all-IT page is that there is nothing to patch. The compromised control was an authorisation decision inside a legal-request workflow — a queue where the operating assumption is that the requester is who the letterhead says, and where refusing or delaying feels like obstructing an investigation. Any organisation with a channel through which police, regulators or government agencies can lawfully demand customer data has the same queue, usually staffed by a small team under time pressure, and usually without the verification step that would have stopped this.
