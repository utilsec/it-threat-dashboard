A Linux rootkit is being planted on F5 BIG-IP APM devices, injecting a fileless web shell that never touches disk

Reporting on 8 September describes compromises of F5 BIG-IP Access Policy Manager devices in which attackers install a Linux rootkit that hooks PHP file loading and injects a web shell directly into memory. Nothing malicious is written to the filesystem, so file-integrity checks and on-disk scanning return clean results while the attacker keeps interactive access. BIG-IP APM is authentication and remote-access infrastructure, which puts a compromised device between users and everything they reach, with sight of the sessions passing through it. Technology organisations tend to run these appliances at the front of both corporate and customer-facing estates, and to treat them as network plumbing rather than as servers requiring hunting and log forwarding. Treat any affected device as a credential-exposure event: rebuild it rather than clean it, and rotate what passed through it.

The autonomy is the novel part; the credential exposure it exploited is not.

Critical Incident

Dropbox says about 5,000 accounts were opened without a password — the flaw was in Lenovo’s email verification, and Dropbox’s decision to trust it

Dropbox disclosed on 2 September 2026 that an attacker reached roughly 5,000 customer accounts between 4 and 21 August without ever knowing a password. The weakness sat in a federated login path rather than in Dropbox’s own authentication: a flaw in Lenovo’s email verification let the attacker register Lenovo IDs against addresses they did not control, and Dropbox’s identity-linking logic accepted that assertion as proof of ownership without asking for the existing Dropbox password. Content in some affected accounts was viewed and downloaded. Dropbox expired every session established through a Lenovo ID and now requires the Dropbox password before a Lenovo ID can authenticate; Lenovo called it a legacy integration issue and said its own customers were unaffected. Accounts with two-factor authentication came through largely intact, because the second factor sat outside the trust that broke.

Critical Incident

Stolen Claude session cookies walk straight past two-factor — and can inherit connectors into corporate Gmail that no AI-platform admin can revoke

Anthropic began emailing affected users in early September 2026 after detecting a threat actor using commodity infostealer malware to lift Claude login sessions from users’ own computers and replay them to access accounts and burn through usage. The mechanism is deliberately boring and that is the point: a session cookie is evidence that a login already happened, so replaying one skips the password and the second factor entirely. Reporting attributes the harvesting to well-known stealer families including Vidar, LummaC2, StealC, RedLine and Acreed on Windows and Atomic Stealer on macOS — none of them specific to AI platforms. Anthropic signed affected users out of all sessions, removed saved payment methods, identified and refunded unauthorised charges, and told users the malware was not related to Claude or installed through it. The consequence enterprises should sit with is the second-order one. A hijacked session inherits every permission the account holds, and where an individual has connected a personal AI account to corporate Google Workspace or Microsoft 365, that includes read access to a work mailbox and Drive. Those OAuth grants live in an administrative gap: the AI vendor can kill the session, but a Workspace or Microsoft 365 administrator often does not know the grant exists, and killing the session does not revoke it.

JFrog patched CVE-2026-82329 on 28 August 2026, an improper-authentication flaw in self-hosted Artifactory carrying a CVSS score of 9.8. Artifactory sits in the middle of the build chain for a great many software vendors, holding registry credentials, signing material and the packages that ship to customers, which is why a token-minting bug there is a supply-chain problem rather than an application one.

More than 7,100 internet-exposed Rails instances were counted in early August.
