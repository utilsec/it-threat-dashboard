Top Story

Google’s threat group details BREEZE COMET — intruders who reach bank payment infrastructure and push hundreds of fraudulent transfers within 48 hours

The Google Threat Intelligence Group and Mandiant published a detailed profile of BREEZE COMET (previously UNC5669, also tracked as Plump Spider), a financially motivated crew active since 2024 that has moved from opportunistic retail-banking fraud to direct intrusion into core financial plumbing. The targets are Brazilian banks, payment processors, fintechs and banking-software providers, and the objective is explicit: reach the National Financial System Network, obtain the mutual-TLS credentials used for Pix, STR and Boleto transfers, learn how the victim’s own transfer approval and anti-fraud checks work, then move money. Initial access is voice phishing that impersonates IT support over phone and WhatsApp, followed by installation of legitimate RMM software such as AnyDesk; the group has also exploited vulnerable JBoss application servers, staged payloads on compromised Brazilian municipal government domains, attempted to recruit insiders, and physically connected rogue devices to retail networks. Once inside it runs a purpose-built toolkit — REALBREEZE for LDAP brute-forcing, COBALTSPIN for reverse SOCKS5 tunnelling across segmented financial networks, LIGHTPAINT for SoftEther VPN persistence, MILDFROST for DNS-tunnelled backdoor access, KICKPLATE masquerading as Windows Update Health Tools, and BOATBEAM posing as an IIS HTTPS listener on 443. It greps CI/CD systems and source repositories for hard-coded payment credentials using terms like boleto, cnab, remessa and instant-payment webhook patterns. Within 24 to 48 hours of establishing access the operators launch two waves of hundreds of fraudulent transactions, then clear event logs and delete their working directories. Google assesses the group is using large language models to generate reconnaissance scripts and automate deployment, and has observed infrastructure aimed at Nigeria, Paraguay, Ghana and Venezuela — so the model is being exported. For institutions outside Latin America the transferable lesson is the target selection: the crew is not after customer credentials, it is after the operator consoles, mTLS certificates and segmented payment networks that sit behind the retail banking layer, and it gets to them through a help desk and an RMM installer rather than an unpatched perimeter box.

High Breach

A dark-web service put 153 million driver licence scans on sale — and the evidence points at an identity-verification vendor banks rely on

A marketplace calling itself Nexus appeared on 31 August 2026 offering searchable digital scans of more than 153 million US and Canadian driver licences, roughly 1.1 million of them Canadian, alongside over 10 million identification cards, more than 3 million travel documents and about 580,000 medical cards. Records were not single photographs: each licence came with roughly six image files — front, back and infrared or ultraviolet captures — plus a capture timestamp, which is the format an automated document-verification scanner produces rather than what a consumer uploads. Brian Krebs traced the likely origin to IDScan.net, a Louisiana identity-verification firm that processes tens of millions of verifications a month across more than 20,000 locations, by correlating timestamps on his own and others’ records with the exact dates those people had their IDs scanned at businesses using IDScan technology. IDScan.net has not confirmed a breach; the attribution rests on that correlation, and the company said only that it was investigating. The FBI’s New Orleans field office opened an investigation, and the Nexus site went dark shortly after Krebs published. For banks, insurers and fintechs this is a direct hit on the evidence layer beneath onboarding. Remote account opening, step-up verification and contact-centre re-identification lean on a photographed government ID, and infrared and ultraviolet captures are precisely the security-feature images used to prove a document is genuine. A leaked corpus of authentic multi-spectrum scans lets an attacker satisfy a document check without ever holding the document.

High Breach

Apollo Global has completed its breach investigation: names, contact details and Social Security numbers taken in a July help-desk intrusion

Apollo Global Management, which manages roughly $1.05 trillion in assets, has finished investigating an intrusion into its cloud platforms that ran from 6 to 10 July 2026 and confirmed that names, contact information and Social Security numbers were exposed. The company says it has found no evidence the data was published or used for fraud, and is offering identity protection and credit monitoring. It has not stated how many individuals are affected. There is no CVE: the entry route was a human identity-verification process, not a software flaw. Two points still deserve care. And the pattern is the reusable part: private-equity, asset-management and insurance groups concentrate investor and limited-partner records carrying SSNs into a small number of cloud tenants, and the same service desk that resets a junior analyst’s MFA can reset a partner’s. A crew that has learned the sector’s job titles and vocabulary can repeat this at a peer firm without touching an unpatched server. BREEZE COMET, this week’s top story, opens the same way.

High Payments

Live Stripe merchant secret keys are circulating in bulk — enough to list customers, create charges and redirect refunds

A dataset published on a criminal forum on 18 August 2026 by an actor using the handle ‘Satanic’ contained live-mode Stripe secret keys for hundreds of merchant accounts together with roughly 33–35 GB of customer and payment data: names, email addresses, phone numbers, postal addresses, partial card details such as brand, expiry and last four digits, IP addresses, transaction identifiers, invoices and active discount codes. Reporting puts the leak at 1,033 sk_live_ keys across about 669 merchant folders and 688,363 customer records. Separately, researchers documented more than 50,000 exposed Stripe keys sitting in public GitHub repositories, unmasked GitHub Actions build logs and misconfigured web servers, and demonstrated that a single exposed key could be turned into a customer list, an attacker-created payment link and a live test charge within 17 hours. Stripe itself was not breached — every key belongs to a merchant that leaked its own credential. What makes this a financial-sector item rather than a merchant one is what a live secret key permits: enumerate customers and stored payment methods, create charges, issue refunds to an attacker-controlled destination, rewrite webhook endpoints so the merchant never sees the events, and in some configurations reach connected accounts through Stripe Connect. Acquirers, payment facilitators, banking-as-a-service providers and any institution with merchants or embedded-payments partners inherit the chargeback exposure and the fraud pattern, not the merchant alone.

A phishing operation across 46 countries persuades staff to install real remote-management software — banking and finance are among the top targets

Researchers have mapped a campaign spanning 46 countries in which fake documents talk recipients into installing legitimate remote monitoring and management tools. The United States accounts for about 45 per cent of observed activity, and 601 distinct cases have been linked to the operation. The delivery infrastructure is deliberately disposable: 425 phishing-kit URLs across 240 hosts, 94 per cent of which were seen for a single day, rotated through Vercel, GitHub Pages, Netlify, Amazon S3, Cloudflare R2 and compromised legitimate sites. Lures are the ordinary business kind — shipping and courier notices, Adobe PDF prompts, tax notices, Social Security Administration themes and invoices. Education, technology and government lead the target list, with banking, finance and manufacturing directly behind them. The technique matters more than the volume. Because the payload is signed, legitimate software that many organisations already use somewhere in the estate, endpoint controls keyed on file reputation stay quiet and the resulting session looks like sanctioned support. This is the same access pattern BREEZE COMET uses to reach Brazilian payment infrastructure and the same one the vishing crews behind the Apollo intrusion rely on: no exploit, no malware signature, just an authorised remote-control session opened by the user. The defensible control is an allowlist of approved RMM products enforced at execution, not detection of a malicious binary.

High Intel

Mirage2FA phishing-as-a-service has hit 4,532 organisations by stealing the session rather than the password

ANY.RUN published analysis of Mirage2FA, a commercial phishing-as-a-service kit aimed at Microsoft 365 that proxies the genuine Microsoft login flow and captures both credentials and the resulting session cookie. Because the victim completes a real multi-factor prompt against the real identity provider, conventional MFA does not stop the theft — the attacker simply replays the authenticated session. The researchers counted 4,532 unique organisation email domains targeted, assessed that around 48 per cent of targeted mailboxes were potentially compromised, and documented more than 9,000 potential session-theft events, with 63.7 per cent of victims in the United States and further activity in India, Singapore, the UK, Canada, Saudi Arabia and South Africa. Technology, manufacturing and education were the most-targeted industries in this dataset; finance is not called out, which is worth stating plainly. It belongs on this page anyway because of what a stolen Microsoft 365 session reaches in a bank or insurer: the mailbox used to approve payment-instruction changes, the SharePoint estate holding policy and client documents, and every downstream application federated behind the same SSO. It also pairs directly with the passkey-persistence tradecraft covered previously — a token-theft incident is an identity incident, and closing it means revoking tokens and enumerating registered authenticators, not resetting a password.

High Intel

Shai-Hulud now sweeps 469 credential locations across developer, CI/CD, cloud and AI tooling

GitGuardian reported that the Shai-Hulud npm worm lineage has widened its credential sweep from 189 paths to 469 locations covering developer environments, CI/CD systems, cloud configuration and AI tool settings. The harvesting logic reads .env files, shell history, package-manager configuration, CLI caches, IDE settings and pipeline secrets, and the prizes it looks for are package-publishing credentials that let it propagate onward through trusted distribution, production cloud tokens, database credentials, Kubernetes cluster credentials and deployment-system tokens. The shift the researchers highlight is strategic rather than technical: instead of attacking trust relationships directly, the operators harvest credentials that are already sitting inside build environments. That is the same objective BREEZE COMET pursues when it greps CI/CD systems for hard-coded payment credentials, arrived at from a different direction. Financial institutions have spent a decade instrumenting production and comparatively little instrumenting the build plane, yet a bank’s CI runners commonly hold cloud deployment roles, database credentials and, in fintech and payments engineering, live provider API keys — which is exactly what the Stripe key exposure demonstrated in practice. A compromise here does not look like an intrusion; it looks like a normal deployment.

Medium Phishing

Microsoft details a business-lending phishing wave that hid its own keywords using invisible Unicode

Microsoft’s security research team published analysis of a high-volume phishing operation built around small-business finance lures — business loans, lines of credit and funding advances — aimed largely at Small Business Administration loan applicants. The evasion technique is the notable part: the operators inserted invisible Unicode tag characters from the U+E0000 to U+E007F range inside financial keywords, so that a word such as ‘funding’ is fragmented at the byte level while still rendering normally to the recipient. Filters matching on literal keywords therefore see nothing to match. Volumes ran between roughly one and 2.37 million messages on weekdays, following a Monday-to-Friday pattern, peaking on 26 February 2026 before declining sharply after 15 May. The campaign used hundreds of finance-themed sender domains and relayed through a legitimate marketing platform’s click-tracking infrastructure, which lends the links a reputable-looking domain. Fortra’s research team had described the same invisible-character approach in September 2025. For banks and lenders there are two consequences. Content filtering that leans on keyword lists for financial-fraud themes can be bypassed by a technique that costs the sender nothing, and brand-impersonation monitoring keyed to your own name misses lures that impersonate the category — generic funding brands that borrowers cannot distinguish from a real lender.

Medium Intel

CISA’s ‘A Tale of Two SOCs’ shows one organisation fully compromised without noticing and another containing the same tradecraft in minutes

CISA published red-team assessment report AA26-237A comparing two engagements. Organisation A, in government services and facilities, was compromised end to end without detecting the intrusion; Organisation B, a water and wastewater utility, spotted phishing payloads during execution and isolated the affected workstations within two to twenty minutes, before command-and-control could establish. Neither is a financial institution, which is worth saying up front — but the failure modes are ones bank and insurer SOCs share. In Organisation A, thousands of false positives from routine operations buried the genuine activity, several disconnected SOCs and endpoint tools had no shared visibility, analysts lacked the standing to escalate, and a legitimate alert on an SCCM server was written off as a false positive simply because nobody could identify who owned the system. The enabling weaknesses were equally familiar: the default machine account quota letting any domain user add computer accounts, an Active Directory Certificate Services template misconfigured in the ESC1 pattern, service-account passwords sitting in plaintext on reachable file shares, and static cloud access keys with no expiry and no revocation path, alongside over-permissioned Entra applications with broad mail-read rights. CISA’s own summary is the line worth carrying into a board pack: detection tooling is only as good as the people, processes and ownership behind it.

Critical Advisory

Citrix NetScaler is being exploited for pre-authentication code execution — web shells are already landing on gateways

CVE-2026-8452 is a memory-overflow flaw in NetScaler ADC and NetScaler Gateway appliances configured as AAA virtual servers or Gateway VPN servers. Citrix’s own advisory describes the consequence conservatively as unpredictable behaviour and denial of service; researchers demonstrated that it yields unauthenticated remote code execution, and watchTowr published proof-of-concept code on 14 August 2026. Exploitation began shortly afterwards. Attackers have been observed dropping web shells named x.php and z.php and running basic discovery commands such as id and echo; one vendor’s telemetry recorded 36 exploitation attempts over twelve days from sources in several countries. CISA added the flaw to the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog on 26 August 2026 with a 29 August federal remediation deadline. Fixed builds are 14.1-72.61 (FIPS), 13.1-63.18 and 13.1-37.272. For financial services this is the highest-priority item of the window. NetScaler Gateway is the remote-access and application-delivery front door in a very large share of bank, insurer and asset-manager estates, it terminates employee VPN and published-application sessions, and it sits by definition on the internet edge. A pre-auth foothold there is adjacent to identity infrastructure and to the internal networks the rest of this page’s campaigns are trying to reach by phone.

CVE-2026-21962 is an improper access-control flaw in Oracle HTTP Server and the Oracle WebLogic Server Proxy Plug-in, scored CVSS 10.0, allowing an unauthenticated attacker with HTTP network access to create, delete or modify critical data. Oracle shipped patches in its January 2026 update. Exploitation has since been confirmed by several private threat-intelligence firms including GreyNoise, CloudSEK and SOCRadar, and a China-linked actor has been reported using it across more than 100 countries to deliver a downloader tracked as SNOWLIGHT. The financial-sector relevance is straightforward: Oracle WebLogic and Oracle HTTP Server remain a common application tier under core banking front ends, insurance policy administration, treasury systems and internet-banking middleware, and the proxy plug-in typically sits in front of them where it is reachable. The window between a January patch and an August KEV listing is the familiar problem — the fix has existed for seven months, and the systems that still have not taken it are the ones held back by change control precisely because they are business-critical.

The three-day and fourteen-day clocks behind this month’s KEV deadlines: BOD 26-04 in practice

The remediation deadlines attached to the KEV additions on this page — 27 August for Oracle WebLogic, 29 August for Citrix NetScaler, 5 and 16 September for the 2 September batch — come from CISA Binding Operational Directive 26-04, issued 10 June 2026, which replaced the flat 14-day and six-month timelines of BOD 22-01 with a risk-scored model. Four variables decide the clock: whether the asset is publicly exposed, whether the flaw is in the KEV catalog, whether exploitation is automatable, and whether the technical impact is total or partial system control. The resulting matrix produces five outcomes, from three days with mandatory forensic triage for KEV flaws yielding total control of an exposed system, through three days without triage, fourteen days for most other KEV items, sixty days for lower-risk combinations, down to fix-on-upgrade where none of the four criteria are met. The methodology is CISA’s Stakeholder-Specific Vulnerability Categorization rather than raw CVSS. Only federal civilian agencies must comply, but this is why it is carried here: financial regulators and examiners consistently reach for federal benchmarks when assessing whether a firm’s patching standard is reasonable, and a bank whose policy still says ‘critical within 30 days’ is now measurably behind a published government standard. The forensic-triage requirement is the part most firms have not budgeted for — the directive assumes an exposed, exploited, total-control flaw may already have been used.

CISA Binding Operational Directive 26-04 • Federal civilian agencies; voluntary elsewhere
Standing directive — carried forward as the framework behind current KEV deadlines, not new activity this window Sets KEV remediation timelines; not itself a vulnerability
No patch applicable — vulnerability-management policy control Action: Re-express your vulnerability SLAs using the four BOD 26-04 variables instead of CVSS bands alone, so an internet-facing KEV flaw with total impact is not sitting in the same 30-day queue as an internal medium. Confirm you can actually answer ‘is this asset publicly exposed?’ from data rather than opinion — that is usually the weakest input. Decide now who performs forensic triage on a three-day-tier finding and whether that is in your IR retainer. Bring the revised standard to the risk committee with the current KEV deadlines as the worked example.

Nothing new emerged from the SEC, NYDFS, the FFIEC agencies, the EBA or the FCA between 23 August and 4 September 2026 in the sources reviewed for this update, and it is more useful to say so than to inflate a commentary piece into a policy event. The obligation that is actually approaching is the one the FCA confirmed on 18 March 2026, jointly with the Prudential Regulation Authority and the Bank of England, covering operational incident reporting and third-party arrangements. It takes effect on 18 March 2027, giving firms a twelve-month implementation period that is now roughly half spent. The regime introduces a single reporting portal shared across the three authorities, removes duplicate reporting obligations for payment service providers and credit rating agencies, allows most solo-regulated firms to submit a short form, and provides clearer guidance on thresholds, definitions and responsibilities. The FCA noted that around 40 per cent of incidents reported to it in 2025 involved a third party, citing large cloud and CDN disruptions as examples — which is the same fourth-party visibility problem the U.S. Bancorp claim illustrates from the other side. The practical preparation is unglamorous: know which incidents cross the threshold, know who signs the submission, and know your material third-party arrangements well enough to report on them within the clock. Firms in scope of both this regime and the EU’s DORA will be running two sets of definitions.
