Still the sector’s defining campaign: Silent Ransom Group works law firms by phone, and sometimes by walking through the front door

Carried forward and re-checked for this edition; nothing newer has been published, and in a week with no new law-firm incident disclosed this remains the most likely way a firm actually gets hit. The group tracked as Silent Ransom Group, Luna Moth, Chatty Spider and UNC3753 has been active since 2022 and targeting law firms since spring 2023. An FBI FLASH alert issued in May 2026 and Google Mandiant’s June 2026 reporting on a targeted campaign against US law firms both describe the operation as still running; no update to either has appeared since. The tradecraft is almost entirely human. Operators call or email posing as IT support, talk a partner, paralegal or billing clerk into a remote-support session, and install a legitimate remote monitoring and management tool. Exfiltration runs over WinSCP or a hidden or renamed copy of Rclone, with data pushed to Google Drive, Microsoft OneDrive, external hard drives or USB storage. There is frequently no encryption stage at all — the leverage is the client material itself, which in a firm means privileged files that cannot be restored away. The outlier detail, and the one that breaks most legal threat models, is that operators have turned up in person, claiming they need to image a device or take a backup to deal with the effects of a phishing email. Halcyon’s research centre ranked law firms the fourth most-targeted industry in early 2026, attributing it to the sensitivity of client data, regulatory pressure to resolve incidents quickly and a perceived willingness to pay. Read this alongside the unpatched ScreenConnect issue further down the page: the tool this campaign relies on being opened for it is the same class of tool now carrying an open advisory. The FBI’s own recommendations are unglamorous and correct: verify visitor identity against ID, deploy phishing-resistant multi-factor authentication, restrict remote access and external-drive permissions on sensitive systems, and train staff to recognise the pretext.

Microsoft documents help-desk vishing that enrols the caller’s own phone, then quietly drains SharePoint and Exchange through the Graph API

Microsoft researchers detailed on 10 September 2026 at least two initial-access-broker clusters — tracked as Storm-3032 and Storm-3121 — running voice-phishing campaigns since May 2026. The operators call staff while impersonating the IT help desk, steer the victim’s personal device to a convincing fake Microsoft sign-in page on a lookalike domain of the “company.add-passkey.com” pattern, and capture credentials and session tokens. With a token in hand they abuse the Microsoft Graph API to enumerate and exfiltrate data from SharePoint, OneDrive and Exchange in small, periodic transfers rather than one conspicuous bulk pull. Access is reportedly sold on to extortion groups including ShinyHunters. Microsoft says it has not yet tied these campaigns to a confirmed corporate breach, and no law firm has been named as a victim — this is a documented technique, not an incident report. It is carried here because the technique maps almost exactly onto how firms are built. A firm’s privileged material does not sit in a bespoke application an attacker has to learn; it sits in SharePoint sites and mailboxes that Graph is designed to read. Three details deserve a firm’s attention specifically. First, the bring-your-own-device angle defeats endpoint controls entirely — the fee-earner’s personal phone is not carrying your EDR agent, so the credential theft happens somewhere you have no telemetry. Second, Graph exfiltration is API traffic to a Microsoft endpoint the tenant legitimately talks to all day; without mailbox and SharePoint audit logging switched on and actually retained, there is no record to reconstruct afterwards. That is not only a detection problem but an evidentiary one: a firm that cannot show which matters were reached cannot scope client notification, cannot advise on privilege waiver, and cannot answer an e-discovery custody question about its own custody. Third, the slow, periodic transfer pattern is chosen precisely to stay under volume-based alerting. Note this is a separate cluster set from the PREY-0058 activity carried elsewhere on this page; the recurrence of the help desk as the entry point across unrelated crews is itself the finding.

A phishing service beat multi-factor authentication at 258 organisations and collected more than 5,000 Microsoft 365 logins

Researchers detailed on 7 September 2026 a phishing-as-a-service platform called BigBear 2.0 that has bypassed multi-factor authentication at 258 organisations and harvested over 5,000 Microsoft 365 credentials. The mechanism is adversary-in-the-middle: the victim is steered to a proxy that relays the real Microsoft sign-in page, so the login looks correct, the MFA prompt is genuine and the code the user approves is passed straight through. What the operator keeps is the resulting session token, which grants access without ever needing the password again. Sold as a service, it puts this capability in the hands of subscribers with no technical skill of their own. For law and accounting firms the relevance is the sheer ordinariness of it. Most firms treat “we have MFA” as the answer to credential phishing, and against push notifications, SMS and authenticator codes it is no longer true. The distinction that matters is between MFA that can be relayed and MFA that cannot: passkeys and FIDO2 security keys bind the login to the real domain, so a proxy page simply fails. Everything else on the list — conditional access on device compliance, short token lifetimes, alerting on new mail rules or unfamiliar OAuth grants — is compensating control. Where a full rollout is a project, start with the accounts an attacker would want most: partners, finance, IT administrators and anyone with delegated access to client mailboxes.

Unit 42 investigated an intrusion driven by AI agents that finished in under ten hours — with no zero-days, which is the part firms should read twice

Palo Alto Networks’ Unit 42 published an investigation on 2 September 2026 into an extortion intrusion in which the attacker used frontier AI models and autonomous agents to run the operation. The compromise ran end to end in under ten hours, against a manual tradecraft baseline Unit 42 puts at roughly two weeks. Multiple specialised agents worked in parallel against different defensive layers, handling network mapping, credential harvesting, privilege escalation and exploitation of the victim’s CI/CD pipeline while monitoring results, evaluating them and re-planning in real time. More than 50 MITRE ATT&CK techniques were exercised. Initial access came through a publicly reachable web service used to tunnel into the network. No novel zero-day was required — the operation ran on known vulnerabilities and misconfigurations, executed faster than a human team could. That last point is what makes this worth a legal-sector reader’s attention. A great deal of law-firm security planning quietly assumes a response window measured in days: someone notices the odd login on Monday, the managed detection provider calls on Tuesday, the incident-response retainer is activated on Wednesday. An intrusion that completes inside a working day does not leave room for that sequence, and it does not need a sophisticated flaw to get started — the internet-facing remote-access appliance you have not patched, or the forgotten extranet, is sufficient. Unit 42’s defensive recommendations are correspondingly about speed and automation rather than about detection cleverness: pre-built playbooks that revoke credentials and freeze pipelines without waiting for a human decision, an inventory of AI endpoints, API keys and model gateways with rate limiting, hunting for machine-speed behavioural patterns such as bursty API activity, and mandatory multi-party review with immutable branch protection on infrastructure repositories. For a firm without a CI/CD pipeline, the transferable half is the first: decide now, in writing, what your service desk is allowed to disable at 2am without calling a partner.

The G7 tells every sector to start the post-quantum migration now — and no sector holds secrets that must stay secret for longer than this one

On 3 September 2026 the national cybersecurity agencies of the G7 states, with the French agency ANSSI chairing the G7 Cybersecurity Working Group and alongside the European Commission and ENISA, issued a joint call to accelerate the transition to post-quantum cryptography. The framing is the notable change: the group explicitly reframes the quantum threat from a distant future problem to a near-term one demanding action across all sectors, not only critical infrastructure. Two dates give it teeth. ANSSI has said it will stop vetting products that lack quantum-safe encryption from 2027, and post-quantum security becomes mandatory for procurement of certain security products by 2030. The statement sets out five priority areas — awareness, national transition strategies and supply chains, research and development, public-private partnership, and building post-quantum cryptography into cybersecurity requirements — and advises organisations to build a cryptographic asset inventory, identify systems holding critical data, map dependencies and take up integrated post-quantum products during normal renewal cycles rather than as a separate programme. This is a policy signal rather than a rule that binds a law firm today, and it is easy to file under “not yet”. The reason not to is specific to legal and professional services: the harvest-now-decrypt-later problem bites hardest where confidentiality obligations run longest. Privilege does not expire. Sealed material stays sealed. Deal documents, trust structures, patent prosecution files, arbitration records and personal data in long-running matters all carry confidentiality duties measured in decades, which is the same horizon the G7 is now telling everyone to plan against. A €500,000 CNIL fine sets out, in order, the four control failures a regulator will fault after a breach

France’s data-protection authority CNIL announced a €500,000 fine on 3 September 2026 against Hôpital privé de la Loire in Saint-Étienne, part of the Ramsay Santé group, over a summer 2025 breach affecting 524,867 patients and 202,246 trusted third parties — 727,113 people in total. A single compromised doctor’s account gave the intruder system-wide access to the electronic patient record. The findings are worth reading as a checklist rather than as healthcare news, because they are the same four things any regulator or opposing counsel will ask about: external users were not required to connect over VPN or to use multi-factor authentication; access controls did not limit how many records one account could reach; there was no real-time monitoring or alerting, so the intruder operated undetected for days; and the organisation failed to notify the 202,246 third parties directly. The cited breaches were of Article 32 on security of processing and Article 34 on communication to data subjects. For advisory and privacy practices, this is a usefully concrete data point on how a European regulator weighs a breach where the attacker — a teenager using the alias “Marak” — claimed the data was neither sold nor published: the penalty attached to the controls and the notification failure, not to downstream harm. For firms as controllers in their own right, the second and fourth findings are the uncomfortable ones. Broad standing access to a document management system, and a habit of notifying the client but not the individuals in the file, are both common in this sector — and the second of those is precisely the question the C-Track courts are working through this week.

The ruling removes state immunity as a blanket procedural defence and gives spyware victims in the UK a clearer route to sue a foreign state; it does not decide the merits. The case now goes to the High Court, where attribution, causation and injury all still have to be proven. That last point is the part that lands on firm IT rather than on the litigation team. A claim of this kind is won or lost on technical evidence about a handset: which implant, when it arrived, what it touched, and whose infrastructure it talked to. Where a firm takes instructions in a transnational-repression, spyware or civil-society matter, the first hours matter — a client’s device should be preserved rather than reset, wiped or “cleaned”, forensic acquisition should be done by someone who can later stand behind the method, and chain of custody has to be documented from the moment the device is handed over. Firms should also expect to be inside the target set themselves: counsel acting in these matters, and the paralegals handling the exhibits, are a recognised route to the same material. Practically that means mobile-device management and lockdown modes on the devices of the people staffing such matters, a defined intake procedure for potentially-compromised client hardware, and somewhere secure to hold forensic images that is not the general document management system.
