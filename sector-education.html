Arctic Wolf confirms the PaperCut attacks are landing on schools and universities — and the intruders are there for credentials, not printing

The PaperCut story stopped being a generic advisory this week. On 5 September, Arctic Wolf’s Adversary Research team published an analysis of intrusions it observed against education organisations in the United States and Europe, ranging from K-12 schools to major universities, in which attackers chained CVE-2026-81578 (an authentication bypass) with CVE-2026-82078 (remote code execution) in PaperCut NG and MF. What happened after the exploit is the part that matters. Arctic Wolf describes ordinary reconnaissance first — whoami, tasklist, ver, uname — then the creation of a privileged local account named Administrator17, then credential harvesting: certutil pulling tooling down, lsa_collect.exe variants and save_hives.exe dumping Windows registry hives, and BootKey extraction used to reach the SAM database. The attackers also searched PaperCut’s own configuration files for passwords, secrets, LDAP settings and tokens, and retrieved and ran a Meterpreter Java payload. Two addresses were named in the reporting: 45.142.193[.]132 for exfiltration and 194.180.48[.]134 for payload delivery. PaperCut disclosed the flaws on 27 August, CISA added both to the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog on 31 August with a 14 September federal deadline, and an early hotfix had to be followed by a second round after the first fix was bypassed. Read the target selection plainly rather than as coincidence. Print management is one of the few software categories where schools, colleges and universities are the core market rather than an afterthought; PaperCut servers are usually domain-joined because they enforce per-student quotas and charge accounts against directory services; and they are frequently owned by a print or desktop team rather than by security. That is a credential store sitting in a low-attention part of the estate, and the LDAP settings in its config files are the reason someone bothered.

Citrix NetScaler authentication bypass went from advisory to live exploitation on 3 September, and campus remote access is the classic deployment

CVE-2026-19490 is a critical authentication bypass in NetScaler appliances configured as AAA virtual servers or as Gateways — that is, in the SSL VPN, ICA Proxy, CVPN and RDP Proxy roles. Citrix published its advisory (CTX696939) in August. Exploitation began after a proof-of-concept became public: researcher Ryan Dewhurst reported that on 3 September one of his NetScaler sensors logged requests matching the PoC from three distinct source addresses geolocated to Australia, the United States and Germany. Shadowserver sees more than 22,000 NetScaler ADC appliances and close to 1,700 Gateway instances exposed, patch state unknown. CISA and Belgium’s national cybersecurity centre have both urged immediate patching. For education this is a familiar shape and a bad one. NetScaler Gateway is the standard front door for staff VPN, published desktops and virtual labs at a large number of universities and larger districts, and it is precisely the sort of appliance that gets a change-freeze exemption in September because taking it down locks everyone out during the first weeks of term. An authentication bypass on that device is pre-authentication access to whatever sits behind it, and historically NetScaler compromises have been followed by session-token theft that survives a later patch. Patching alone is not sufficient here.

JetBrains was breached through its own unpatched TeamCity server — and TeamCity is a fixture in CS departments and research groups

JetBrains disclosed a compromise of Cadence, its hosted CI/CD service. The way in was CVE-2026-63077, a deserialization flaw in TeamCity rated 9.8 that lets an unauthenticated attacker bypass authentication and run operating-system commands. It was disclosed in July 2026 and added to the CISA KEV catalog on 5 August; the server involved had simply not been patched, and JetBrains has not explained the delay. The intrusion window is given as 8–24 August, with JetBrains discovering the activity on 23 August. What the attackers reached: personal data including usernames, real names, email addresses, login timestamps and IP addresses; a 2024 server backup containing credentials, configurations and artifacts; AWS IAM user credentials and secrets; files in S3 buckets in JetBrains AWS accounts; and potentially synchronised PyCharm project source code. JetBrains told customers to immediately revoke or rotate every credential and secret that may have been used to run Cadence executions, review connected systems including AWS, S3 and registries, audit repositories for unauthorised changes, and treat all executions in the window as untrusted. Education has two exposures here rather than one. JetBrains tooling is everywhere in academia because of its free educational and classroom licensing, so a meaningful share of the affected user population is students, teaching staff and research developers. And self-hosted TeamCity instances sit in computer-science departments, engineering labs and campus development teams — environments that are rarely inside central IT’s patch programme, and where a KEV entry from 5 August can go unactioned for a month without anyone noticing.

MikroTik routers are being taken over through exposed SSH with no credentials — go looking for the edge devices nobody owns

CERT Polska reported on 5 September that attackers have been chaining two RouterOS vulnerabilities, referred to together as “MikroTrick”, since at least 2 September to gain full administrative control of internet-exposed MikroTik routers without authenticating. No CVE identifiers were published with the disclosure, so treat the version guidance rather than a CVE as the thing to act on. Fixed releases are 6.49.21 for the 6.0.0–6.49.20 branch, 7.23.4 for 7.0.0–7.23.3, 7.24.2 for 7.24.0–7.24.1, and 7.25beta3 on the development branch. Where an upgrade cannot happen immediately, CERT Polska recommends disabling the exposed services — SSH, WWW and WWW-SSL, and bandwidth-test — or restricting them to trusted source networks. Its recovery advice for a device believed compromised is unusually blunt and worth following: isolate it, preserve the logs and configuration for analysis, reset to factory settings, and rebuild from a verified configuration rather than restoring a backup that may already contain the attacker’s changes, then change every credential the device held. MikroTik is the budget edge and lab router, which is exactly why this matters in education. These appear at small district sites and outbuildings, athletics facilities, residence halls, field stations, maker spaces and student networking labs — often bought on a purchase card by a department, configured once, and never entered in an asset register. A compromised router is a traffic-interception and pivot position on whatever segment it serves, not just a device to replace, and unauthenticated administrative access means there is no credential-strength argument to fall back on.

440,000 blocked exploit attempts against two WordPress form plugins — and departmental sites are where education runs WordPress

Wordfence reported blocking more than 440,000 exploitation attempts against two unauthenticated arbitrary-file-upload flaws. CVE-2026-14894 (CVSS 9.8) in Super Forms – Drag & Drop Form Builder has drawn over 250,000 attempts since 14 July, peaking on 18 August with more than 40,000 requests in a day; it is fixed in 6.3.314. CVE-2026-32475 in Elementor Pro has drawn over 190,000 attempts beginning 19 August — the same day the fix shipped in 4.2.2. The Elementor technique submits a form file-upload field as an array whose first element is empty and whose second carries a PHP payload with a .php filename, which slips past validation; the resulting webshell lands in /wp-content/uploads/elementor/forms/ under a random name. Education is unusually exposed to this class of bug, not because central IT runs WordPress badly but because it often does not run it at all: admissions microsites, department and lab pages, conference sites, student-organisation sites and faculty pages accumulate outside the managed estate, on hosting nobody has inventoried, with a plugin set nobody patches. Any of those with a published form containing a file-upload field is a candidate for remote code execution on a domain that carries your institution’s name and, quite often, its SSO cookie scope.

5,400 hacked WordPress and PrestaShop sites are serving fake-CAPTCHA lures whose payload lives on a blockchain

Netskope published research on 5 September into a campaign that has compromised more than 5,400 websites, mostly WordPress and PrestaShop, and injected a script that shows the visitor a ClickFix lure — a fake CAPTCHA instructing them to open the Windows Run dialog and paste a PowerShell command. The infrastructure is the interesting part. The operators use the EtherHiding technique, storing the malicious code inside smart contracts on the BNB Smart Chain testnet, so there is no C2 domain or hosting provider to take down and no DNS record to sinkhole. Netskope observed roughly 300 to 536 infected sites contacting BNB Smart Chain endpoints on a given day, with the campaign running since spring 2026, and more recent builds replacing the ClickFix stage with a WebRTC data-channel stager for quieter code delivery. Education intersects this twice. The compromised-site population is departmental and small-organisation WordPress, which is precisely the shape of the unmanaged web estate on institution domains — lab pages, conference sites, student-organisation sites, admissions microsites — and a lure served from a genuine subdomain of your own institution defeats every “check the address bar” message you have ever sent staff. The second is that ClickFix is a social-engineering attack the user executes themselves, so there is no patch to deploy: the controls are endpoint policy and one very specific piece of user education. No real CAPTCHA has ever asked anyone to paste anything into the Run dialog, and that single sentence is more useful to a staff briefing than a general phishing module.

Medium Threat Intel

39 documented ways to defeat a passkey without breaking the cryptography — read it before the campus MFA rollout, not after

A survey published on 4 September catalogues 39 distinct techniques that compromise a passkey-protected account while leaving the FIDO2 cryptography completely intact, drawing on published work including SpecterOps’ “Pass the Passkey” research, which showed malware abusing the authentication flow without ever extracting a private key. The techniques group into four families. Interface and prompt attacks: passkey prompt flooding, credential-interface deception, application metadata spoofing, window-handle spoofing. Credential theft: compromise of synced vaults, takeover of the cloud account the vault syncs to, mobile malware. Enrollment and recovery abuse: shadow passkeys registered by an attacker, enrollment vishing, help-desk takeover, SIM-based recovery. And protocol manipulation: assertion mining, assertion replay, circuit-breaker attacks. Some are reported as already appearing in real-world attack patterns; not all 39 have been operationalised. The recommended defences are structural rather than reactive — restrict authentication to approved authenticator classes, prefer dedicated hardware over software-resident passkeys, require proof from an already-authorised authenticator to enrol a new one rather than a weaker recovery channel, and validate challenges and user verification properly with no fallback path. For education the enrollment and recovery family is the one to sit with. A university service desk resets credentials for tens of thousands of transient users, at volume, under a term-start queue, with identity proofing that was designed for convenience; a district helpdesk is often one person who recognises the teacher’s voice. Passkeys do not remove that path. They make it the path, which means the identity-proofing script deserves as much design attention as the authenticator choice.

High Threat Intel

The Shai-Hulud worm now sweeps 469 credential locations, and research computing is full of all of them

GitGuardian documented a new variant of the Shai-Hulud credential-stealing worm that scans 469 distinct locations across developer environments, CI/CD tooling, cloud configuration files and AI-tool configs — up from 189 paths in earlier builds. The design goal the researchers describe is worth restating plainly: the worm is not trying to break trust relationships, it is trying to collect the credentials that already make those relationships work. It prioritises package-publishing tokens, because a stolen publishing credential lets the operator push a poisoned package into a registry that downstream consumers already trust, and the infection propagates forward through supply chains rather than sideways through networks. Universities are an awkward fit for the standard advice here. A research group’s pipeline typically has cloud credentials with real spending authority, registry tokens for internally published packages, long-lived SSH keys to HPC clusters, and increasingly API keys for hosted models — often in a repository owned by a postdoc who will graduate, on a runner nobody in central IT administers, with secrets in a dotfile because that is what worked. Central IT usually has no inventory of these environments and no mandate over them. The practical consequence is that a single compromised student laptop or a single malicious dependency in a lab’s requirements file can hand an attacker credentials that reach institutional cloud accounts and shared compute.

HPE patched an unauthenticated RCE in ArubaOS-CX — the switch operating system running a large share of campus networks

CVE-2026-73749 is a buffer overflow in an ArubaOS-CX daemon that allows an unauthenticated remote attacker to execute code with elevated privileges by sending specially crafted packets to the affected service. HPE published fixed builds on 3 September: upgrade 10.18.0001 to 10.18.1002 or later, 10.17.1021 and earlier to 10.17.1030 or later, 10.16.1051 and earlier to 10.16.1060 or later, 10.13.1180 and earlier to 10.13.1190 or later, and 10.10.1180 and earlier to 10.10.1181 or later. The same bulletin carries a further 23 issues rated between 8.1 and 8.8 — command injection, authentication bypass, cross-site scripting and cross-site request forgery — tracked as CVE-2026-73750 through CVE-2026-73782. HPE reported no observed exploitation and no public proof-of-concept at publication, and strongly encourages upgrading. Two things make this awkward for education specifically. The second is that campus switch management interfaces are frequently reachable from the general staff network rather than an isolated out-of-band VLAN, because that was easier when the network was built and nobody has revisited it. Unauthenticated remote code execution on a switch is control of the segment and of the traffic crossing it, so the compensating control worth acting on today — even before the firmware plan — is the reachability of the management plane.

Chrome V8 zero-day exploited in the wild — the sixth this year, on the browser that is the whole endpoint in most classrooms

Google patched CVE-2026-85046, a type-confusion flaw in the V8 JavaScript and WebAssembly engine, and acknowledged that an exploit exists in the wild. Type confusion means the engine misinterprets one object type as another, corrupting memory in a way that a crafted HTML page with malicious JavaScript can steer toward code execution inside the renderer sandbox. Fixed builds are 152.0.7977.82/.83 on Windows and macOS and 152.0.7977.82 on Linux; the release also closed nine other high-severity issues across crash reporting, networking and graphics. Google withheld technical detail to buy patching time, and credited the report to Salvatore Gulizia. This is the sixth actively exploited Chrome bug fixed since January 2026. The education angle is scale rather than novelty. In K-12 in particular the browser is not one application among many — it is the entire student computing environment, and a managed Chromebook fleet numbering in the thousands per district updates on a channel and a reboot cadence that IT controls only loosely, because the device belongs to a student who may not restart it for weeks. Verify enforcement rather than assuming it: set the minimum version policy in your admin console, check the reported version distribution across the fleet, and identify the tail of devices that has not taken the update. On the staff side, the same check applies to Chrome and Chromium-based Edge on managed laptops.

SonicWall SMA1000 zero-days are being chained for remote code execution on remote-access appliances

SonicWall warned on 2 September that threat actors are chaining two SMA1000 zero-days in remote code execution attacks. CVE-2026-83548 is a server-side request forgery in the Workplace interface that leads to command injection and carries a maximum-severity rating; CVE-2026-83549 is a command-injection flaw in the management console that requires administrative privileges and supplies the second half of the chain, yielding arbitrary OS command execution. Affected models are the SMA1000 6210, 7210 and 8200v. SonicWall states that SSL-VPN on its firewalls and the SMA 100 Series are not affected — check which product you actually have before acting or standing down. Hotfix releases are available and CISA added both CVEs to the KEV catalog on 2 September. Shadowserver sees more than 400 SMA1000 appliances exposed online, some of which may already be patched. SonicWall’s own guidance goes further than patching: where indicators of compromise are present it recommends re-imaging the appliance and resetting credentials and TOTP tokens. That is the right instinct for any secure-remote-access box in an education estate, where the appliance is often the only thing standing between the public internet and the administrative network, and where a compromised device yields not just VPN access but the enrolled identities of everyone who authenticated through it.

Critical Advisory

Seven KEV additions on 2 September, and the education-relevant ones are the phone system and the artifact repository

CISA added seven actively exploited vulnerabilities to the KEV catalog on 2 September, with remediation deadlines of 5 and 16 September. Alongside the two SonicWall SMA1000 flaws covered separately, the batch includes CVE-2026-9586, an unauthenticated SQL injection in Sangoma Switchvox rated 9.3; CVE-2026-82329, an improper-authentication flaw in JFrog Artifactory rated 9.8; CVE-2026-48710, a request-smuggling issue in Starlette rated 6.5 and chained with CVE-2026-42271; CVE-2026-49869, a maximum-severity command injection in Kestra OSS; and CVE-2026-59822, an improper-authentication flaw in Berri LiteLLM rated 8.8. Two deserve attention on a campus or district estate. Switchvox is a VoIP phone system: the flaw sits in the /pa HTTP endpoint, which parses XML phone notifications and concatenates the PhoneIP field straight into an unparameterised SQL query, giving unauthenticated remote code execution; it is fixed in 8.4.0.2, released 14 July, and roughly 4,000 exposed devices are visible on Shodan, mostly in the United States. Schools run phone systems and rarely patch them on a security cadence. Artifactory matters wherever a research computing or central development group runs a self-managed instance — the flaw lets an unauthenticated attacker on the network forge administrative tokens, which is a supply-chain problem, not just a server problem, because it allows replacement of trusted artifacts. JFrog patched on 28 August across the 7.111.21, 7.117.28, 7.125.20, 7.133.29, 7.146.38 and 7.161.20 branches; cloud instances were already protected.

Medium Advisory

A 9.3-rated VMware Workstation and Fusion flaw breaks out of the guest — which matters wherever a course hands students a VM

CVE-2026-59346, rated CVSS 9.3, is an integer overflow reachable by a local administrator inside a virtual machine that uses the VMXNET3 network adapter, and it allows code execution on the host. Alongside it, CVE-2026-59347, rated 8.1, is a stack-based buffer overflow in HGFS — the host-guest file sharing component — that allows execution as the virtual machine’s VMX process on the host. Both affect VMware Workstation and Fusion 25H2 and 26H1, and both are fixed in Workstation 26H1u1 and Fusion 26H1u1. There are no workarounds; patching is the only mitigation. Neither shows evidence of exploitation. The first was reported by researchers credited as @h4urek, @cameudis and Stan S; the second by Yeonghyeon Choi and Tianchu Chen of Tencent Xuanwu Lab. The reason this sits on an education page rather than getting filed as a desktop-software update is the precondition. “Local administrator inside the guest” is an unusual starting position in a corporate fleet and a completely ordinary one in teaching: security, forensics, networking and systems courses routinely hand a student a virtual machine and expect full administrative control inside it, and malware-analysis and reverse-engineering courses go further and expect deliberately hostile code to run there. The same applies to research groups detonating samples on analyst workstations. Anywhere the hypervisor boundary is doing pedagogical work rather than just providing convenience, it is a security control and should be patched like one — and shared folders should be off on any lab image that does not specifically need them.

CISA has retired six free assessment services that districts and campuses used instead of buying one

CISA has discontinued six assessment offerings, effective 25 August and reported on 1 September: Cyber Resilience Reviews, Cyber Resilience Essentials surveys, Ransomware Readiness Assessments, Incident Management Reviews, External Dependencies Management Assessments and Cyber Infrastructure Surveys. The agency framed the change as retiring legacy questionnaire assessments to reduce redundancy for itself and for requesting organisations, and points users toward the Cross-Sector Cybersecurity Performance Goals instead. Reporting attributes the decision to workload and resource constraints, in the context of CISA having lost roughly a third of its workforce since 2025, and describes internal disagreement between its Cybersecurity Division and Integrated Operations Division over the allocation. The substantive difference is that the retired services involved a CISA advisor working through the questions with you, while the replacement is self-service. For a large university with a security team this is an inconvenience. For a district or a small college it removes something that had no affordable equivalent: an outside professional assessment, at no cost, whose written output could be put in front of a board or a superintendent to justify a budget line. This lands alongside the MS-ISAC funding loss noted below, and the pattern is the same — the free tier that under-resourced education institutions relied on is thinning, and nothing has replaced it. If you were planning to request one of these, plan a different route now.

Education Department guidance treats student surveys and mental-health screenings as requiring written parental consent

The Education Department’s Student Privacy Policy Office issued a Dear Colleague letter in late August setting out its interpretation of the Protection of Pupil Rights Amendment and FERPA. The core position is that mental-health screenings and student surveys must be treated as required activities subject to parental notification and written consent, even when the district presents them as optional, on the reasoning that students commonly feel obliged to participate when an authority figure administers a survey. Under the guidance, districts must give parents an advance opportunity to review survey content and obtain written consent; a simple opt-out procedure is stated not to satisfy the requirement. The letter also addresses parental access to student information, including material some districts have treated as restricted. This page takes no position on the policy; the reason it appears here is that the operational burden falls largely on IT and data teams. Guidance of this kind converts a workflow question into a records question: which platform collects the survey, where the responses are stored, whether the SIS can record and evidence consent per student per instrument, how a parent request to review content is served and logged, and what the retention and deletion rules are for responses collected under an older process. Screening and social-emotional-learning tools are usually third-party SaaS, so the answer often sits in a vendor’s configuration rather than yours. Read the letter and inventory the tools before the first survey of the term goes out.

Carried forward: CISA’s free K-12 guidance, and the 70% of MS-ISAC membership that is no longer there to back it up

CISA published two free guides for school and district leaders on 14 August — one on building a foundational cybersecurity programme, the other on sustaining it — covering credential protection, device security, tested backups, incident-response planning and adoption of the NIST Cybersecurity Framework. The agency cited 34 ransomware attacks against K-12 and higher-education organisations in the first half of 2026, of which twelve were confirmed and 22 unconfirmed, and pointed to the March breach at Alamo Heights ISD in Texas, which affected 26,629 individuals and shut systems down for five days. The unconfirmed proportion in that count is worth noticing alongside the leak-site cluster reported on this page: most education ransomware claims never get institutional confirmation, which makes the sector’s true incident rate genuinely unknown. The context that has not improved is the Multi-State ISAC, long the free threat-intelligence channel for schools, which lost federal funding and has since lost 70% of its membership, including dozens of states and over 10,000 local jurisdictions. Read together with the retirement of CISA’s free assessments in the card above, the direction is consistent: self-serve documents are replacing staffed services, and districts that relied on the staffed version need to plan for that rather than wait for it to come back.
