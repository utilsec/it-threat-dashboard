About 700 French supermarkets had their refrigeration controllers taken over and forced into defrost on 11–13 October — CERT-FR says the campaign is probably still running

On the nights of 11–12 and 12–13 October attackers took remote control of web-managed refrigeration controllers in French supermarkets and pushed display cases and freezers into continuous or extended defrost, with setpoints up to about 50°C. Finance Minister Roland Lescure told Parliament on 13 October that about 700 of France’s roughly 10,000 supermarkets were affected; Carrefour, Coopérative U, E.Leclerc, Intermarché (with Netto) and Monoprix have confirmed being hit, and Intermarché’s own count is reported as both about 150 and about 300 stores. Stock was destroyed, the Paris prosecutor reports fires breaking out at some sites, and the chief executive of Les Mousquetaires, Intermarché’s parent, expects losses in the tens of millions of euros. CERT-FR’s alert CERTFR-2026-ALE-012 named Danfoss AK-SM and AK-SC controllers on 12 October, added Copeland xweb and Dixell xweb on 13 October, and in its third version on 16 October says attack campaigns are “probably still ongoing”. Danfoss says most affected units are end-of-life AK-SM 800 and AK-SC 355 models or AK-SM 800A units on outdated firmware; Copeland points to a discontinued, end-of-life controller generation. No group has claimed it, no ransom has been demanded and the entry route has not been established; the prosecutor’s cybercrime section opened an inquiry on its own initiative and assigned it to OFAC, the national anti-cybercrime office. Sekoia told Reuters that Spain, Portugal, Poland, Germany and Belgium were also affected, without detail, and no authority outside France has confirmed that. Censys saw French-exposed Danfoss panels fall from more than 1,800 to 537 between 11 and 14 October while counts elsewhere barely moved, and Reuters still found at least 4,500 Danfoss interfaces on Shodan on 13 October. For retailers this is the part of the store estate the IT team often does not own: refrigeration, HVAC and energy controllers installed and remotely managed by contractors, frequently left reachable from the internet so the contractor can log in.

ASOS confirms customer data was taken from a third-party communications platform, after attackers used its own app to push an extortion message to shoppers

ASOS app users received an “unauthorised customer notification” in the first week of October, which The Register reported on 6 October. Addressed to the retailer’s data protection officer and IT department, it claimed that ASOS data hosted on Snowflake had been “fully compromised” and threatened a leak unless the company engaged. In a London Stock Exchange filing reported by TechCrunch on 8 October, ASOS confirmed that hackers had broken into a third-party platform it uses to communicate with customers and taken names and contact information; its 13 October update adds some non-personal account information and says no payment-card data or account passwords were affected. ASOS has not said how many customers are involved; it has about 17 million. The BBC reported a wider set of fields, including home addresses, phone numbers and website search queries, and BleepingComputer reported that the Snowflake instance was reached with credentials obtained by impersonating a trusted contact; those accounts are the outlets’, not ASOS’s. The attackers call themselves “Xuanye Group”, and Snowflake says its own systems were not breached. The lesson is the channel more than the data: the platform that sends push notifications and marketing messages reaches every customer at once, so whoever controls it can phish the whole base in the brand’s own voice.

ATB, which runs more than 1,300 stores, confirmed on 5 October that it had been hit by a cyberattack after a group calling itself DataSuckers posted an extortion message and countdown timer on the retailer’s website, The Record reported. ATB took some online services offline for what it called technical maintenance, and its Facebook statement said the message did not affect the security of customers’ data and that the site remains under its control. Everything else is the group’s claim: records on 7.9 million customers, including names, contact details and password hashes, plus employee passport details and more than 11 million orders, with a $400,000 demand and sample screenshots on Telegram. A message planted on the storefront reaches customers before the company’s statement does, so web-content integrity monitoring and a pre-agreed holding line belong in the response plan. And a claim of stolen password hashes is the prompt to check how loyalty and e-commerce passwords are stored and how quickly resets can be forced.

Times Car, Park24’s car-sharing service in Japan, confirms a breach of its web system — about 6.6 million current and former accounts reported affected, and driver’s licence and other ID images exposed for about 1.6 million

Park24, the Japanese parking and mobility group, disclosed on 25 September that the web system of its Times Car car-sharing service had suffered unauthorised external access. The intrusion was detected at 9:07 a.m. that day and the access route was blocked by 7:25 a.m. on 26 September; the company has notified Japan’s Personal Information Protection Commission and says the service is running normally. Its third report, dated 29 September and updated with an FAQ on 1 October, confirms that identity-verification images were exposed for about 1.6 million accounts: driver’s licence images, utility bills used to confirm addresses, student ID cards for student-plan members and family-plan verification documents. Hackread and SC Media, reporting the company’s figures, put the total at about 6.6 million current and former accounts, including corporate users, with names, addresses, dates of birth, phone numbers, email addresses, driver’s licence details, linked service IDs and account passwords stored in a non-recoverable form; card data was not affected. An external forensic firm is investigating, the entry point has not been disclosed, and the company says it has seen no sign of the data being published or misused. Times Car is a mobility service rather than a shop, but the exposure is one this page keeps returning to: identity documents collected once to verify a customer and then kept. A retailer that photographs licences for rental, age-restricted delivery, returns-fraud checks or account recovery holds the same material, and the IDScan breach further down this section shows how long such images linger once taken.

Gambit’s AI-agent skimming campaign: 27 organisations breached, skimmers confirmed on 19 of their sites and more than 600,000 card records taken from two of them — still one vendor’s figures, with no victim named

Gambit, an AI-security company, has published an investigation, reported by BleepingComputer on 23 September and The Register on 25 September, into a financially motivated operator who used three open-source AI-agent frameworks to find, break into and skim e-commerce and booking sites. According to Gambit, Strix handled vulnerability scanning, Cairn autonomous exploitation and Hermes orchestration, with the human operator giving the agents short goal instructions; the reporting describes the attacker’s own use of the frameworks and does not describe any compromise of the model provider. The Register’s account, citing Gambit’s threat-intelligence director Eyal Sela, puts 27 companies breached “to varying degrees”, skimmers ordered against all 27 and confirmed present on 19 of their websites, and more than 600,000 card records “from just two victim companies”. Separately, more than 100 other infected sites have been identified, for at least 119 in all. The operator’s spend was roughly $12,000–18,000. Gambit also says the agents ran deletion or cleanup routines that could disrupt a victim’s operations, “and this has indeed happened in some of the breaches”, which turns a skimming incident into a data-loss one. Victims are described only by type: a Fortune 500 hospitality company, a major US airline, a private US industrial-supplies distributor and a US online fashion retailer. How the sites were first breached is not stated and no CVE is named. It led the 24 September edition and stays below confirmed incidents because the figures still come from a single vendor, with no company, regulator or card brand confirming any of them. What stays true either way is the economics: at about $25 a scan, small and mid-sized storefronts that were never worth a human operator’s time are now in range.

BigCommerce, the hosted e-commerce platform, has told merchants that attackers obtained an access key belonging to Ribon and Ribon 1.5, third-party shopping-experience apps from its marketplace owned by Be A Part Of, a Fastr company, and used it to inject malicious scripts into “a small number of merchant storefronts”. SecurityWeek, reporting on 22 September, sets out the timeline: the credentials were misused from 13 to 17 September, revoked on 17 September, and merchants were notified on 18 September. The data taken was names, email addresses, phone numbers and addresses; BigCommerce says passwords and payment-card data are held separately, and no card data has been reported taken. BigCommerce says its own platform was not breached. The UK spirits retailer Master of Malt, the first store to confirm it was affected, says that once the attackers had the Ribon key “they used it to access data held inside BigCommerce”, downloading customer records one page at a time until the key was disabled, a day after the developer detected the misuse. The app was installed on hundreds of storefronts, so BigCommerce’s “small number” is the figure to watch, and no count has been published. Neither Be A Part Of nor Fastr had commented at publication. In its notice to customers, reported by The Register on 23 September, Master of Malt’s founder Justin Petszaft wrote that “the attackers had access to your name, email address, phone number, and address” and asked customers to “be extra vigilant against potential phone calls, spam and phishing attacks”; the retailer says BigCommerce’s security team uninstalled the Ribon app the day the attack was discovered, that there is no ongoing compromise, and that payment data and passwords are held in a separate system that was not affected. No customer count was given, and no other merchant has been named. For shoppers the risk is delivery and refund scams that use a real name, address and recent order, and they will contact the retailer, not the app developer.

Critical Breach

A stolen Cloudflare key let attackers rewrite Brevo’s embedded marketing scripts for more than four hours — shoppers were shown ClickFix lures and logged-in WordPress admins were served a backdoor plugin

Brevo, the email-marketing and CRM platform formerly called Sendinblue, has confirmed a supply-chain compromise that turned its own embedded scripts against the sites carrying them. Attackers obtained a long-lived Cloudflare API key with full account permissions that had been hardcoded in Brevo’s application source, and used it to deploy a malicious Cloudflare Worker that rewrote responses at the CDN edge on 14 September, from about 16:07 to 20:30 UTC. The Worker altered the SDK loader and the Conversations chat-widget script that customers embed, plus Brevo-hosted pages including sibforms.com form hosting, and stripped Content-Security-Policy headers on the way through; Brevo says its origin servers and files were never modified, which is why standard integrity checks missed it. Sansec, which found the attack, estimates more than 100,000 sites were exposed. Ordinary visitors were shown a fake “Cloudflare, verify you are human” ClickFix page telling them to paste and run a command; visitors with a live WordPress admin session had the script try to install a plugin called “Web Media Optimizer” as a persistent backdoor. It follows a linked 10 September incident in which a mis-scoped SAML sign-on path exposed 138 Brevo customer accounts. It moved down from the lead on 22 September because nothing had changed: Sansec’s write-up was last revised on 17 September, no count of backdoored sites or infected visitors has been published, and no regulator action or further Brevo statement surfaced between 18 and 22 September. For merchants, a newsletter pop-up or chat widget loaded on every page, checkout included, has the same power as your own code: check WordPress sites for plugins installed on 14 September and rotate admin passwords, and treat any PC where someone ran a “verify you are human” command as infected.
