Boston Scientific cyberattack halts manufacturing and order fulfilment worldwide, and hospitals are still working through the backlog

Boston Scientific detected a cyberattack on its IT environment on 25 August 2026 and disclosed it in an SEC Form 8-K the following day. The company confirmed the intrusion knocked out access to operating systems and business applications to the point that it could not manufacture products or process and ship customer orders, and described the effect on its operations as global. Two details matter clinically. Implanted cardiac devices that are not connected to Boston Scientific networks are unaffected, but new remote-monitoring activations were disrupted during the outage — so cardiology programmes enrolling new patients may have a gap to reconcile. And this is a supply-chain availability event, not a data event: interventional cardiology, electrophysiology, endoscopy and urology inventories at your facility can be depleted by an attack on a manufacturer you have no visibility into.

High Breach

Legacy-data archiving vendor Aesto Health posts a 9.54 million-person breach to HHS, nine months after the intrusion

Aesto Health, which provides data migration, EHR-to-EHR exchange and legacy clinical data archiving to provider organisations, appeared on the HHS Office for Civil Rights breach portal on 31 August 2026 with 9,540,000 individuals affected. The unauthorised activity occurred in its Amazon Web Services infrastructure between 2 and 18 December 2025 and was detected on 18 December. Aesto did not confirm a breach until 26 May 2026, began notifying its covered-entity customers on 26 June, and reported to HHS on 31 July. The exposed set is close to a worst case for identity fraud: names, dates of birth, medical information, driver’s licence numbers, financial account numbers, health insurance information, taxpayer identification numbers, government identification numbers and Social Security numbers. No actor has claimed the intrusion and Aesto says it has no evidence of resulting identity theft. The structural lesson is about archives specifically. When a health system retires an EHR, the historical record does not disappear — it is copied to an archiving vendor and then largely forgotten, outside the scope of the access reviews, logging and segmentation that protect the live system. The archive is often broader than the production database because it spans every predecessor system the organisation ever ran. Note also the interval: more than five months between detection and the vendor telling its customers, and eight months before the count reached HHS. If your incident-response clock starts when a business associate tells you, it starts far too late.

High Breach

ShinyHunters publishes 7.1 million Baxter International records after the medical products maker declines to pay

Baxter International, the Deerfield, Illinois manufacturer of infusion pumps, IV solutions, renal therapies and surgical products, disclosed a cybersecurity incident on 13 August 2026. ShinyHunters claimed it the next day, set a 17 August ransom deadline and, when no payment came, released roughly 7.1 million records on 19 August. Baxter attributes the compromise to certain third-party applications rather than to its own core systems; ShinyHunters says the haul is Salesforce data. That framing matches the broader ShinyHunters pattern of the past year, in which the group phones a target’s help desk or staff, talks a user into authorising a malicious connected application against the company’s SaaS tenant, and then bulk-exports objects through the API. There is no exploited CVE and no malware on an endpoint; from the platform’s point of view the export is a legitimate authenticated action. Baxter says there was no impact on patient services or business continuity, that it continues to operate normally, and that it does not expect a material financial effect. It has not confirmed what personal data the released records contain, although ShinyHunters asserts some hold personally identifiable information. For health systems the exposure is indirect but real: manufacturer CRM objects typically hold clinician names, facility addresses, purchasing contacts and account histories, which is exactly the raw material for a convincing invoice-fraud or vendor-impersonation approach to your accounts payable team.

High Breach

Baylor Genetics revises its June intrusion up to 2,810,878 people — nine times the figure first reported

Clinical genomics laboratory Baylor Genetics has told HHS that 2,810,878 individuals had electronic protected health information exposed in an intrusion between 11 and 17 June 2026. Suspicious activity was identified on 15 June, the forensic investigation closed on 30 July, and the lab disclosed publicly on 19 August with roughly 310,000 people known affected across Texas, Massachusetts and Vermont. The HHS filing on 2 September multiplies that by nine. Patient data includes names with one or more of date of birth, medical testing information, laboratory results, health insurance information and, for a subset, Social Security numbers; employee records exposed Social Security numbers, government identification and financial account details. No group has claimed the intrusion and no vulnerability has been named. Two things make a genomics laboratory a distinct problem rather than another breach statistic. First, the diagnostic content is genuinely unchangeable — a test result indicating a hereditary cancer syndrome or a carrier status cannot be reissued the way a card number can, and its sensitivity does not decay. Second, the exposed population is far larger than the ordering institution because a reference laboratory aggregates specimens from many health systems, so a hospital that sent a modest volume of send-out testing can still have thousands of its patients inside this count. The upward revision is also a reminder that first-week numbers in a laboratory or reference-vendor breach are provisional, and that a communications plan built around the initial figure will need rewriting.

High Ransomware

Nutex Health confirms patient and employee data stolen in an August attack claimed by The Gentlemen

Houston-based Nutex Health, which runs 27 hospital and outpatient facilities across 12 states, first disclosed a cybersecurity incident on 24 August 2026 and in a follow-up SEC filing on 1 September confirmed that data was taken and that the attackers are demanding payment under threat of publication. The stolen set spans patient records, employee information, data on external providers and confidential financial information. A putative class action has already been filed on behalf of individuals whose personal and protected health information was allegedly accessed; Nutex says it cannot yet predict the outcome or estimate the business impact. The Gentlemen ransomware operation has claimed the attack. That group is worth understanding rather than filing away as another brand name. It emerged in mid-2025, has claimed several hundred victims since, and pays affiliates a 90/10 split against the 80/20 that established operations offer, which has been pulling experienced operators toward it. Research into the group finds healthcare among its most-hit sectors, with no sign it observes the informal restraint on hospital targeting that some competitors still claim. Its documented entry route is exposed internet-facing infrastructure — VPN concentrators, remote-access gateways and firewall management portals — followed by domain-wide encryption pushed through Group Policy within hours of gaining administrative rights. That timeline is short enough that detection has to fire on the privilege escalation, not on the encryption.

Medium Extortion

DireWolf claims 253 GB from the National Kidney Registry, including donor records and HLA typing

The DireWolf ransomware group listed the National Kidney Registry on its leak site in late August 2026, claiming to hold 253 GB across eight datasets and around 180,000 documents — among them roughly 15,050 donor medical files and 24,791 transplant recipient files. The group says the material includes Social Security numbers, dates of birth, full medical histories, imaging, HLA tissue-typing results, financial proofs such as pay stubs and tax forms, and personal contact details. None of this is confirmed: the non-profit has not responded publicly to enquiries, no sample has been independently validated, and the count comes entirely from the extortionists. Unusually, DireWolf told researchers it had not affected the organisation’s operations and does not disrupt organisations of this type, and it set a countdown of roughly eleven days for contact. Whether or not the claim is accurate, the target choice is the point. Living-donor matching is time-critical and depends on a national registry to run paired exchanges and chains across transplant centres; an outage does not corrupt a record, it delays an operation. The data type is also permanent in a way most PHI is not, since HLA typing and family medical history describe biological relatives who never consented to anything. Transplant programmes should treat registry connectivity as a clinical dependency with a documented manual fallback, in the same category as blood bank and pharmacy interfaces.

Medium Incident

Luminis Health takes MyChart and CareConnectNow offline during an unfolding cybersecurity incident

Luminis Health, the Annapolis-based system that operates three hospitals including Anne Arundel Medical Center along with roughly 100 ambulatory sites, confirmed a cybersecurity incident that pulled its MyChart patient portal and its CareConnectNow platform out of service. As of the evening of 1 September certain systems remained unavailable and the organisation said it was working urgently to restore them. It engaged outside counsel and third-party investigators, stood up a dedicated patient helpline, and told patients that outpatient appointments would go ahead as scheduled unless individually contacted, while warning of possible delays. The system has not disclosed when the incident began, what caused it, or whether patient data was accessed; notification will follow if the investigation requires it. Nothing here is yet attributable and no group has claimed it. What is instructive is the failure mode. Systems that rehearse EHR downtime rarely rehearse portal downtime, yet the portal is usually the first thing disconnected because it is the internet-facing edge of the clinical estate.

Because the messages arrive outside your infrastructure, no email gateway control you own will stop them; the only levers are pre-emptive patient communication, a clear statement of what MyChart will and will not ask for, and a monitored channel for patients to report what they receive.

Medium Campaign

A phishing operation across 46 countries tricks users into installing legitimate remote-management software

ANY.RUN researchers have connected 601 cases into a single campaign that spans 46 countries, with 45% of observed activity aimed at the United States. The method avoids malware entirely at the first stage: victims receive a document lure — Canada Revenue Agency tax forms, shipping and UPS notices, Adobe PDF prompts, US Social Security Administration themes, invoices — and are led through a fake document page to download and install a genuine, signed remote monitoring and management agent. Once it runs, the attacker has interactive remote control using a tool that is code-signed, frequently allow-listed, and indistinguishable from what an IT department legitimately deploys. The infrastructure is deliberately disposable: 425 kit URLs across 240 hosts, 94% of them observed for only a single day, hosted on Vercel, GitHub Pages, Netlify and compromised websites, which defeats blocklists built on reputation. Researchers name education, technology and government as the most-targeted sectors, with banking, finance and manufacturing also prominent; healthcare is not called out in the reporting. It belongs on this page anyway, because the technique is sector-agnostic and healthcare estates are unusually exposed to it — large distributed workforces, high volumes of legitimate external correspondence about claims and invoices, and an existing tolerance for RMM agents installed by managed service providers across affiliated practices.

Critical Advisory

SonicWall SMA1000 remote-access appliances hit by two chained zero-days, one scoring a maximum 10.0

SonicWall disclosed on 1 September 2026 that two flaws in its SMA1000 secure access appliances were exploited in the wild before any patch existed. CVE-2026-83548 (CVSS 10.0) is a pre-authentication server-side request forgery in the Appliance Work Place interface that lets an unauthenticated remote attacker reach sensitive internal functionality. CVE-2026-83549 (CVSS 7.8) is an OS command injection in the Appliance Management Console that normally requires administrative access — but chained behind the SSRF, that requirement falls away and the pair yields unauthenticated command execution on the appliance. SonicWall found the flaws internally and says it has evidence of both being chained in attacks, without publishing indicators of compromise or attributing the activity. OCR settles its 55th right-of-access case, over records a patient waited nearly two years to receive

The HHS Office for Civil Rights announced a $50,000 settlement with Azul Vision, Inc., a California optometry and ophthalmology practice, on 27 August 2026. Right-of-access failures are usually process failures rather than security failures — requests arriving by several channels with no single queue, no clock, and no owner — which makes them cheap to fix and conspicuous when they are not.
