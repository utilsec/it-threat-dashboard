Italian tech collective serving civil-society groups shuts down, taking its email, web and mailing-list hosting with it

Autistici/Inventati, a volunteer-run Italian technology collective that provided privacy-focused infrastructure to civil-society groups, announced on 6 September that it is dissolving and closing all of its services. The sequence was quick. The US State Department designated the collective an extremist group on 26 August. Two days later the Public Interest Registry suspended its domain, and Banca Etica suspended its account the same day over sanctions risk. With no domain and no banking, the collective concluded it could not continue. What disappears with it is working infrastructure: roughly 16,000 email addresses, 1,500 websites, 5,500 mailing lists and around 10,000 blogs. For the organisations relying on those services this is an availability event with no incident response available and no vendor to escalate to, and the practical question is where the data and the mailing lists go next.

Critical Incident

Forensic work in Serbia confirms mercenary spyware on activists’ phones, including the first Pegasus infection verified this year

Three organisations working together published findings on 2 September 2026 on spyware targeting civil society in Serbia. The SHARE Foundation identified 14 people who had been targeted, among them a member of parliament and a local government official. Citizen Lab assessed with high probability that a student activist’s device carried a zero-click Pegasus infection dating from December 2025 to January 2026 — the first forensically confirmed Pegasus infection reported in 2026. Amnesty International confirmed two further devices carrying a new variant of NoviSpy, with the evidence pointing to infection while the devices were in the hands of police during detention. What set the whole investigation in motion were Apple’s threat notifications to affected users, and Apple updates have since broken the specific exploit involved. Two points travel beyond Serbia. First, zero-click means no mistake was made by the target: there is no link to avoid and no attachment to decline, so awareness training is not the control. Second, physical access to a device is an infection vector in its own right, which matters for staff crossing borders, attending protests or working in environments where a phone may be taken from them. For organisations supporting activists, journalists or human-rights defenders, the practical response is to enable Apple’s Lockdown Mode or the Android equivalent for high-risk staff, keep devices current, and know in advance which forensic support organisations you would contact.

High Incident

Around 5,000 Dropbox accounts were taken over without a single password being guessed — and every one of them was missing MFA

Dropbox has confirmed that an unauthorised party gained access to roughly 5,000 customer accounts between 4 and 21 August 2026 by abusing a flaw in Lenovo’s email verification process. The attacker could register a Lenovo ID against an email address they did not control, then use Lenovo’s single sign-on pathway into Dropbox. No Dropbox password was ever needed, and no Dropbox software was at fault: the login was accepted because Dropbox trusted Lenovo’s assertion that the email address had been verified. Files were viewed or downloaded in fewer than 1,600 of the affected accounts. Dropbox says every compromised account was one that had not enabled multi-factor authentication, and that accounts with MFA were not affected. It has since expired all Lenovo-linked sessions, unlinked the affected accounts, notified regulators and users, and now requires password confirmation before a Lenovo ID can be used to sign in. This stays on the nonprofit page for two reasons that have nothing to do with Lenovo. Dropbox is close to universal in the sector — it is where grant applications, safeguarding files, donor spreadsheets, trustee packs and beneficiary case notes actually live, precisely because it is cheap, discounted or donated and needs no server. And the failure mode is the sector’s oldest one: MFA that was never switched on for volunteers, seconded staff, shared team logins and the founder’s account. The quieter lesson is that “sign in with…” buttons inherit the security of whoever is on the other side of them, and nobody on the nonprofit side could have patched anything.

Confirmed — Dropbox states unauthorised access occurred 4–21 Aug 2026; affected customers and regulators notified No CVE — abuse of a federated-identity verification weakness at the identity provider, not a Dropbox software flaw

Then open your Dropbox security settings and review connected accounts and linked devices; unlink any third-party sign-in you do not deliberately use, Lenovo included. Review the account activity log for the 4–21 August window and for sign-ins from a country or device you do not recognise. If you find one, assume the documents in that account were read, and treat safeguarding records, beneficiary case notes and donor financial detail as the priority for a data-protection assessment. Finally, write down which third-party logins your organisation permits at all.

Critical Incident

The Magento zero-day backdooring online stores now has a CVE, an emergency patch and a place in CISA’s KEV catalog

Sansec, the Dutch e-commerce forensics firm, reported on 5 September that a previously unknown flaw it names StyleSmuggler is being used to plant backdoors in Magento Open Source and Adobe Commerce stores. Sansec describes it as giving an unauthenticated attacker remote code execution with no user interaction, delivered in two stages: PHP code is smuggled in through the platform, then executed by triggering a built-in scheduled email job. The implant that follows disguises itself as a system process, re-establishes itself through cron, and reads session data. Sansec observed exploitation from 4 September. The position has since changed for the better: the flaw is now tracked as CVE-2026-75650 at CVSS 10.0, affecting all versions of Adobe Commerce and Magento Open Source, Adobe has shipped an out-of-band patch and confirmed exploitation in the wild against merchants, and CISA added it to the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog on 8 September. Attackers used it to deploy a Rust backdoor and a PHP web shell; the injection path abuses Magento’s template system through a “Payment Transaction Failed Reminder” email. The same Adobe release fixed over 170 vulnerabilities. Nonprofits reach Magento by a predictable route — a charity shop moved online, merchandise, Christmas cards, event ticketing, membership fulfilment — usually built years ago by an agency that is no longer on retainer, which means nobody is watching for an advisory that has not been written yet. If your store takes card payments, the exposure is not only your own data.

Critical Incident

Attackers are taking over MikroTik routers through internet-facing SSH — the cheapest capable box in the sector

CERT Polska, Poland’s national CERT, published confirmation on 5 September that vulnerabilities it disclosed in MikroTik RouterOS are being exploited in the wild, chained into an attack it calls MikroTrick. The chain gives an unauthenticated attacker administrative control of a router whose SSH service is reachable from the internet: a flaw in how RouterOS verified the public key presented during SSH authentication provides the entry point, and improper handling of disallowed characters in the username then escalates that session to full administrator. A third issue affects the bandwidth-test service. CERT Polska reports attacks observed since at least 2 September. Fixed builds have been published by MikroTik and the advisory names the specific versions; apply whichever your device tracks. This matters to nonprofits not because MikroTik is glamorous but because of who owns one. It is the router that ends up in small charities, community organisations, faith groups, hospices and overseas field offices precisely because it does serious routing for very little money — frequently installed once by a volunteer, a trustee’s relative or a contractor who has long since moved on, and then never touched again. The exposure is not a misconfiguration you made; it is a device nobody has logged into for years with management left open to the internet. If the router falls, everything behind it is reachable and every DNS lookup on your network is the attacker’s to steer.

High Incident

“Hi, this is IT” — a voice-phishing operation runs through Microsoft Teams and ends in domain compromise

Palo Alto Networks’ Unit 42 published an analysis on 31 August of an operation it tracks as Spring Ring, which used Microsoft Teams itself as the approach route. The attackers created Teams accounts impersonating internal IT support using look-alike .onmicrosoft.com tenants, sent external chat requests, and moved the conversation to a voice call in which they posed as the help desk. Victims were then talked into running remote management software or downloading malware; in the more advanced variant the operators went on to attempt NTLM relay attacks against domain controllers, using SMB scanning and PetitPotam-style coercion. Unit 42 documents more than ten targeted organisations across several industries and over 150 individual employees contacted, with activity running from January to April 2026. No nonprofit is named, and that is exactly why it belongs here. In a small charity there is no internal IT department to impersonate badly — support is genuinely an outside person you have never met, who genuinely does contact you unannounced and genuinely does ask you to install a remote-access tool. The social defence that works in a corporate is missing by design. The control is a rule, not a product: your organisation uses one named support tool, contact only ever comes through one named channel, and anything else gets hung up on and verified by voice on a number you already held.

10+ organisations • 150+ employees contacted • activity Jan–Apr 2026 • no CVE — social engineering plus abuse of legitimate remote-management tooling
Active technique — Unit 42 documents a sustained campaign; the method remains widely available and is being copied No CVE — identity impersonation and legitimate software abuse, not a software flaw
No patch — tenant configuration, allow-listing and a verification habit Action: In Microsoft Teams, restrict or block external chat from unknown tenants — in a small nonprofit the list of organisations that legitimately need to Teams-message you is short enough to allow-list. Tell every member of staff and volunteer, in plain words, the name of the one support provider you use and the one channel they will contact you through, and that anyone else asking them to install anything is to be refused without embarrassment. Remove local administrator rights so an installer cannot complete silently, and block or alert on remote-management binaries your provider does not use. On the domain side, enable SMB signing and LDAP channel binding, and restrict NTLM where you can — those are what turn a single tricked user into a domain-wide incident.

Medium Incident

Someone spent a month guessing AWS root passwords at 150+ organisations — the account nonprofits set up once and never revisit

Datadog Security Labs published research on 31 August describing a password-spraying campaign that attempted to sign in as the AWS root user across more than 150 organisations between 24 July and 23 August 2026. Spraying deliberately keeps the volume low to avoid lockouts and detection: the median organisation saw only two failed attempts across the whole month, and the busiest saw eight. Datadog reports no successful authentications in the activity it observed. The source addresses were spread across many countries and autonomous systems — hosting providers and residential proxies — so blocking by geography achieves nothing, and the traffic presented two recurring browser user-agent strings. The reason this earns space on a nonprofit page despite causing no known compromise is what the AWS root account typically is in this sector. Many nonprofits hold AWS through a credits or grant programme, and the root account was created years ago by a consultant, a volunteer or a founding staff member, secured with a shared password in a document, tied to a mailbox nobody reads, and never touched again because day-to-day work happens under other logins. It is the one credential that cannot be restricted by policy and can undo every other control — and it is the one nobody owns.

High Campaign

Attackers are phoning IT help desks to take over senior staff accounts — and a small charity’s “help desk” is usually one trusting colleague

Arctic Wolf published research on 7 September 2026 on a data-theft and extortion cluster it tracks as PREY-0058. The method is voice phishing aimed at IT help desks: the caller impersonates a director or vice-president and asks for a password reset or a multi-factor re-enrolment. Once that succeeds, the operators use adversary-in-the-middle infrastructure to steal the session token and sign in through residential proxies, so the login looks ordinary, then work through Microsoft 365 and connected SaaS applications taking data for extortion. Arctic Wolf notes tradecraft overlapping Mandiant’s UNC6671. Small organisations should not read the corporate framing and assume this misses them. In most charities there is no help desk — there is an operations manager, a part-time IT volunteer or an outsourced provider, and the same social pressure applies with more force, because refusing a trustee or a chief executive feels like obstruction. Two details are worth carrying away. Residential proxies defeat the country-blocking and impossible-travel rules many small tenants rely on, so geography will not flag it. And because the attacker steals a live session token, resetting the password afterwards does not evict them unless sessions are explicitly revoked.

High Campaign

A subscription phishing service bypassed MFA at 258 organisations and collected over 5,000 Microsoft 365 logins

Researchers described on 7 September 2026 a phishing-as-a-service platform called BigBear 2.0 which has defeated multi-factor authentication at 258 organisations and harvested more than 5,000 Microsoft 365 credentials. It works by adversary-in-the-middle: the victim lands on a proxy that relays the genuine Microsoft sign-in page, so the page is real, the MFA prompt is real, and the code the user approves passes straight through to the attacker, who keeps the resulting session token. Because it is sold as a service, no technical skill is required to run it. The uncomfortable part for this sector is that “we turned on MFA” has been the standard answer to phishing in nonprofit security guidance for years, and against app codes, SMS and push approvals that answer no longer holds. What still works is authentication bound to the real website: passkeys or hardware security keys, which simply fail on a proxy page. Many nonprofits qualify for donated or heavily discounted Microsoft 365 licensing that includes the necessary controls, and security keys are inexpensive enough to buy for the handful of accounts that matter most — finance, the shared inbox, the chief executive and whoever administers the tenant.

High Research

Commodity malware now switches Microsoft Defender and Windows Update off on the way in — which matters most where Defender is the only control you have

Elastic Security Labs published research on 2 September documenting four modules distributed alongside REVSTEALER, an infostealer sold commercially since at least February 2026, matching roughly 4,700 samples on VirusTotal over the past year. The module worth your attention is LockAppHost: before running a cryptocurrency miner it adds Microsoft Defender exclusions for common folders and file types, disables Windows Update services, deletes scheduled update and malware-removal tasks, and hides the miner inside legitimate Windows processes, obtaining administrator rights by abusing a built-in Windows utility. The other three modules steal wallet files, browser extensions and typed passwords, hijack the clipboard to swap cryptocurrency addresses, and turn the machine into a reverse proxy for the attacker’s traffic. Delivery is through game-cheat lures promoted on hijacked video channels and through fake software downloads, including bogus installers impersonating well-known AI assistants. Two things make this a nonprofit item rather than a gamer one. Most nonprofits run Microsoft Defender because it comes with the Microsoft nonprofit grant and there is no budget for anything else, so malware whose first act is to switch it off removes the entire control. And shared, loaned and hand-me-down laptops used by volunteers, placements and family members are precisely where a game-cheat or fake-installer download lands.

Medium Research

Research puts an end-to-end enterprise breach at under ten hours — which is the whole problem for an organisation with no night shift

Unit 42 has published research describing an emulated enterprise compromise carried out by autonomous AI agents that chained more than fifty MITRE ATT&CK techniques from initial access to exfiltration in under ten hours, using no zero-day vulnerabilities. Separately, Rapid7’s 2026 Global Threat Landscape report finds the number of exploited high and critical vulnerabilities up sharply year on year, with the interval between public disclosure and observed exploitation continuing to collapse. Read together, the two findings say the same thing in different registers: the defender’s reaction window is closing faster than most patching processes were designed for. Treat the ten-hour figure as a research result under controlled conditions rather than a measured average of real intrusions — but the direction of travel is not in dispute. For nonprofits the implication is uncomfortable and specific. A compromise that completes overnight or across a bank holiday weekend will run to completion in an organisation whose IT support is a part-time contractor, a shared inbox monitored on weekdays, or a volunteer who checks in on Tuesdays. The realistic responses are not more staff. They are automatic patching wherever you can tolerate it, MFA everywhere so the first step costs the attacker something, backups that are offline or immutable and have actually been restored from once, and a written answer to “who do we call at 2am on a Sunday, and does that arrangement exist in a contract?”

Critical Advisory

CISA adds four exploited flaws, led by an N-able N-central bug at CVSS 10.0 — the software an outsourced IT provider uses to manage your laptops

CISA added four actively exploited vulnerabilities to its Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog on 8 September 2026, all with vendor fixes available. The headline entry is CVE-2026-86218 in N-able N-central at CVSS 10.0, a static code injection flaw giving remote code execution before authentication. N-able confirmed exploitation in the wild, Huntress observed attacks against N-central’s API and appliance logs from 4 September, and N-able shipped 2026.3 Hotfix 4 on 5 September — its fourth hotfix in five weeks. The federal deadline for US agencies is 11 September. The batch also included the Adobe Commerce flaw covered above and two Windows zero-days. Almost no charity runs N-central directly, and that is precisely why it belongs here. N-central is a remote monitoring and management platform used by outsourced IT providers, and a great many nonprofits buy their IT support from exactly that kind of provider. The platform holds an agent with administrative rights on every machine it manages, so a compromise of the provider’s server is a compromise of every customer’s laptops at once, with no involvement from the customer at all. The only useful action available to you is to ask.

The Charity Commission’s guidance after the Beacon CRM incident is still the best free incident rehearsal in the sector

Carried forward and re-checked for this edition: the guidance published on GOV.UK for charities affected by the Beacon cyber security incident remains current, and no further regulatory action or new findings were published in this window. Its value now is less about Beacon than about what it asks trustees to have decided in advance. The incident itself — a supplier compromise affecting a customer relationship management platform used by charities, in which supporter contact and donation-history data was accessed — is the sector’s most instructive recent case precisely because none of the affected organisations had done anything wrong technically. Their data was somewhere else, on a system they could not patch, and their obligations to their supporters did not transfer with it. That is the shape of most nonprofit breaches: not your server, still your duty. The guidance sets out serious-incident reporting to the Commission, data-protection notification duties, and what to tell supporters — and it is worth reading even if Beacon never touched you, because the questions it forces are the ones nobody has time to answer during an actual incident.
