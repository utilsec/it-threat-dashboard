What happened. On 16 September TechCrunch reported that the ShinyHunters extortion group had published what it described as hundreds of thousands of files taken from DAVID, the Florida Highway Safety and Motor Vehicles (FLHSMV) driver and vehicle information system used by law enforcement and other authorised agencies. TechCrunch reviewed a copy. The files are largely vehicle ownership certificates carrying owners’ names, addresses and vehicle identification numbers; a subset includes Social Security numbers and government documents such as non-US passports and immigration papers. No driving-licence images or photographs were seen. Florida had confirmed the unauthorised access on 11 September. According to WCJB, FLHSMV says the credentials used came from a Plant City Police Department employee’s personal device; the group claims about 200,000 records; the department will notify affected Floridians, has informed Attorney General James Uthmeier, and describes the group as “international”. FLHSMV declined to comment on the published data. The record count is the attacker’s own figure; the publication itself has been independently reviewed. As of 24 September there is no new primary-source development: no revised count from FLHSMV, no published notification timeline, and no group statement beyond the publication.

Why it matters. No software flaw was involved. DAVID behaved exactly as designed for a session it had every reason to trust; the weak point was an authorised user at a partner agency, on a device the state did not manage. That shape — a shared state database, thousands of accounts spread across local agencies, and credentials usable from anywhere — exists in every state, around driver data, criminal-justice information, benefits eligibility and court records. Publication changes the job. The data is now available to anyone, so the work moves from containment to notification and fraud prevention, and a vehicle record tied to a home address is doxxing material, above all for people whose safety depends on an address staying out of reach: officers, judges, and survivors enrolled in address-confidentiality programmes. It is also the second licence-and-vehicle data exposure in a fortnight after IDScan, which is the argument for treating that category of record as compromised by default. And it was not alone in its shape that week: Revolut’s forged data requests, covered below, began with a government certified-mail credential believed to have come from infostealer logs — the same lesson from the other side of the request (see the Revolut card).
