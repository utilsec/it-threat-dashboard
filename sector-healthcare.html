Top Story

Poland confirms criminal access to a clinical software vendor holding records on up to 19 million people

Polish authorities have confirmed that criminals gained unauthorised access to historical data held by the vendor behind MyDr, a clinical and practice-management software suite used by more than 12,000 medical facilities across Poland and connected to the country’s national P1 e-Health platform. Officials put the potential population at up to 19 million people — roughly half the country — which makes this one of the largest healthcare software-supply-chain events on record in Europe. What is confirmed is the unauthorised access itself and the fact that it reached historical records held by the vendor. What remains unverified is the attackers’ own claim to have exfiltrated and now hold that data: independent investigators have not corroborated the volume, contents or existence of a stolen dataset, and the figure circulating publicly derives from the vendor’s customer footprint rather than from a validated victim count. As a precaution the state replaced the digital certificates used in the e-Health integration rather than waiting for the investigation to conclude — a sensible assumption that credential material inside the vendor’s estate should be treated as untrusted. For health-system IT leaders anywhere, the transferable lesson is the shape of the exposure rather than the geography: a single clinical software provider that both aggregates records for thousands of practices and holds trusted credentials into a national data exchange concentrates far more risk than any of its individual customers can see from their own logs. Ask your clinical software and health-information-exchange vendors two questions — what historical data do they retain that you assume was deleted, and what credentials or certificates do they hold that would let an intruder pivot into shared infrastructure.

CareCloud has confirmed in HHS filings that 3,756,469 individuals were affected by an intrusion into its AWS environment between 10 and 16 March 2026, with attacker access lasting roughly eight hours. CareCloud reported the incident to law enforcement and filed an 8-K with the SEC on 24 March, meaning the material-event filing preceded individual notification by about five months. What makes this the week’s most consequential healthcare disclosure is position rather than headcount. Most of the exposed population has no direct relationship with the breached entity and little reason to recognise the notification when it lands. For provider organisations that inverts the usual incident-response posture: your patients sit inside someone else’s breach, your front desk absorbs the calls, and your business-associate agreement decides who carries notification cost and regulatory exposure. The eight-hour dwell time is the other operational lesson. This was a fast, targeted staging operation against a cloud tenancy rather than a months-long ransomware siege, and detection tuned for slow lateral movement across an on-premises estate would not have registered it.

The AHA tells hospitals their climate and door controllers are in scope of the S7 campaign — the assets in question sit under facilities, not IT

The American Hospital Association issued an alert on 20 August pointing hospitals at the joint NSA/CISA/FBI/DOE/EPA advisory published two days earlier, which describes unattributed actors using AI-generated exploitation scripts disguised as legitimate monitoring tools, alongside internet-wide scanning, against Siemens S7-series programmable logic controllers. The agencies assess the activity as reconnaissance and capability development rather than completed attacks. The AHA’s contribution is to state plainly which hospital systems fall in scope: climate control and physical access control. That is the part most health systems will handle badly, because those controllers usually sit under facilities management rather than IT or clinical engineering, and are frequently absent from the asset inventory that feeds vulnerability management and patching. A compromised S7 governing air handling touches operating-theatre pressure differentials, isolation-room containment, pharmacy and laboratory temperature ranges and data-centre cooling; one governing door hardware touches physical security for controlled-substance storage, behavioural health units and neonatal wards. None of that presents as a patient-data risk, so none of it competes well for attention against the week’s breach disclosures — which is precisely why it needs an owner named now. The honest read on likelihood: nothing here is confirmed exploitation of a hospital, and the federal assessment stops at reconnaissance. But scanning is indiscriminate, and an internet-exposed S7 controller in a hospital plant room is indistinguishable from one in a water utility. Exposure, not targeting, is the variable a health system actually controls this week.

Medium Campaign

Hospitals are telling staff that the Microsoft Teams call from “IT” may be an attacker — the lure matches SynkLoader, which profiles Active Directory before ransomware

Multiple hospitals have issued confirmed internal advisories warning that a Teams call or chat purporting to come from the internal IT helpdesk may be an attacker. Malware research corroborates the technique independently: the SynkLoader campaign, reported 21 August, opens with Teams impersonation of IT and steers the target to a fake “PowerShell Cleaner” hosted on Microsoft Azure — a domain most allow-lists trust. The loader is multi-language, spanning Python, PowerShell, C# and C++, and was first compiled around 28 July 2026. Once resident it profiles Active Directory size and infrastructure, which researchers read as ransomware pre-staging rather than commodity credential theft. A module called PhishLocker renders a full-screen fake Windows 11 lock screen to harvest credentials; Alt+Tab exposes it. The actor is unattributed and no CVE is involved. Healthcare fits this lure for structural reasons rather than accidental ones. Clinical staff are conditioned to accept unscheduled IT contact mid-shift, rotate across sites and rosters often enough that an unfamiliar helpdesk name is unremarkable, and work under time pressure that makes “just run this cleaner” look like the fast path back to the chart. Teams external access, commonly left open for referring physicians and vendors, supplies the delivery route. The Active Directory profiling step is the detail that should set response priority: this is reconnaissance ahead of an encryption event, not opportunistic credential theft. Twenty-one health systems separately warned of a MyChart “Medicare Kit” scam in the same week, so patient-facing and staff-facing social engineering are running concurrently against the same institutions.

A single phone call put an intruder on Quantum Health’s network — vishing remains healthcare’s dominant way in

Quantum Health, a care-navigation company that sits between employers, health plans and providers, has disclosed that an intruder obtained network access after a voice-phishing call — a caller impersonating a legitimate party persuaded someone to hand over what was needed to authenticate. Disclosures from Precision Imaging Centers and Heart of America were reported alongside it in the same window. The Quantum Health case is worth dwelling on because it defeats the reassurance most healthcare organisations currently rely on. Multi-factor authentication does not stop this: the attacker is not bypassing MFA, they are convincing a human to complete it on their behalf, or convincing a help-desk agent to reset or re-enrol a factor. Healthcare is unusually exposed to it because clinical urgency is a legitimate and constant reason to override process, staff turnover and rotating clinicians make unfamiliar callers normal, and 24-hour service desks handle high volumes of genuine access emergencies. The countermeasure is procedural rather than technical: identity verification for help-desk password and MFA-reset requests that does not depend on knowledge the caller could have researched, and an escalation path that does not penalise an agent for refusing a plausible-sounding urgent request.

CISA and FBI refresh the Medusa advisory: 500+ critical-infrastructure victims, healthcare a core target, and exploits used before public disclosure

CISA and the FBI have updated joint advisory AA25-071A on Medusa ransomware. The cumulative victim tally passed 500 critical-infrastructure organisations as of April 2026, up from roughly 300 in March 2025 — about 200 new victims in a year — with healthcare named among the core targeted sectors alongside critical manufacturing, the defence industrial base, government facilities, IT and financial services. The finding that should change how hospital IT teams plan patching is the timing: the agencies assess that Medusa weaponises newly disclosed vulnerabilities within roughly 24 hours, and has been observed using exploits up to a week before public disclosure, because the group buys working exploits rather than developing them. A 30-day patch cycle keyed to disclosure dates is not a defence against that model. What survives it is compensating controls that do not depend on knowing which flaw is being used: segmentation between clinical and corporate estates so an edge compromise does not reach the EHR, offline or immutable backups verified by restore testing, and detection tuned to post-exploitation behaviour rather than to specific exploit signatures.

Critical Advisory

Suspected China-nexus actor mass-exploits critical VMware vCenter flaw across 361 victim networks in five days

A critical directory-traversal flaw in the VMware vCenter Server syslog service, CVE-2026-59310 (CVSS 9.8), allows unauthenticated remote code execution against the platform most large IT shops — including hospital systems — use to run their virtualization fabric. Broadcom shipped a fix on 29 July 2026, but DFIR firm QUIRSO observed the first attacker contact just five days later, on 3 August, and by 7 August had logged 361 distinct victim IP addresses across 47 countries, with Germany, the United States, Turkey, Iran and France hit hardest. The observed chain uses the path-traversal flaw to gain a foothold, plants a malicious cron job for persistence, and then installs the open-source reverse_ssh tool for command-and-control. A suspected China-nexus operator has been seen deploying a Babuk-derived ransomware payload that researchers assess, with moderate confidence, may be a smokescreen for deeper intrusion rather than the primary objective. CISA added CVE-2026-59310 to its Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog on 18 August 2026, with a federal remediation deadline of 21 August. In a hospital environment, vCenter typically manages the guest virtual machines, datastores and often the backup infrastructure behind clinical applications and the EHR, so a compromise here can reach far beyond any single system.

Four new CISA KEV entries with a 21 August federal deadline hit Windows, SharePoint, vCenter and macOS

CISA added four actively exploited vulnerabilities to the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog on 18 August 2026, with a 21 August remediation deadline for federal agencies that is a reasonable target for hospital IT as well. They are CVE-2026-33824, a double-free in the Microsoft Windows IKE service extensions (IKEEXT) leading to remote code execution; CVE-2026-59310, the Broadcom VMware vCenter path-traversal flaw covered separately on this page; CVE-2026-55040, a weak-authentication flaw in Microsoft SharePoint permitting a security-feature bypass; and CVE-2026-65400, an improper-authentication flaw in Apple macOS Screen Sharing. Three of the four map directly onto things that are ordinarily present in a health system: IKEEXT sits in the Windows IPsec/VPN path, SharePoint routinely holds policies, incident documentation and departmental files that are more sensitive than their owners assume, and macOS Screen Sharing turns up in research groups, imaging and clinical-informatics teams that fall outside standard Windows management. Each is a discrete, patchable exposure — but all four are confirmed as being exploited in the wild, which is what separates them from ordinary advisory traffic.

Windows Task Host privilege-escalation flaw is now being used by ransomware crews — a hunt-for-stragglers signal

CISA updated its KEV entry for CVE-2025-60710 on 18 August 2026 to record that ransomware groups are now abusing the flaw. It is a link-following weakness in the Windows Task Host that lets a low-privileged local user escalate to SYSTEM on Windows 11 and Windows Server 2025. Microsoft patched it in November 2025 and it entered KEV on 13 April 2026, so for most organisations this is not a new patch cycle — it is a prompt to go looking for the machines that never took it. In healthcare estates those stragglers are predictable: clinical workstations kept out of automated patch rings because a vendor-supported application is validated against a specific build, biomedical and imaging endpoints under change-control regimes measured in quarters, shared kiosks and workstations-on-wheels that are rarely online long enough to complete a reboot cycle, and long-lived terminal-server sessions. Privilege escalation is what converts a phishing or vishing foothold — the dominant entry route in this sector right now — into domain-wide ransomware deployment, which is exactly why extortion crews have picked it up.

CISA, FBI and partners name healthcare among Gunra ransomware’s confirmed target sectors in a joint advisory

A joint cybersecurity advisory, AA26-222A, published 10 August 2026, details Gunra, a Conti-derived ransomware-as-a-service operation running double-extortion attacks against healthcare, government and other critical-infrastructure organizations. The advisory describes affiliates gaining initial access primarily through internet-facing VPN gateways and firewalls, including two previously disclosed FortiOS/FortiProxy authentication-bypass flaws, CVE-2024-55591 and CVE-2025-24472, that remain unpatched in some environments more than a year after disclosure. Once inside, affiliates steal data before encrypting it and use short ransom deadlines to pressure payment. Because healthcare is explicitly named as a targeted sector in the advisory rather than merely inferred from leak-site claims, it is a direct signal that hospital and health-system edge devices are within this group’s stated targeting, not just incidental collateral. It is also the same category of exposed VPN and firewall infrastructure that keeps appearing as the entry point in mid-size clinic ransomware cases — unpatched edge appliances with remote access enabled and no phishing-resistant MFA in front of them.

Federal judge locks down handling of stolen Change Healthcare data with seven new security rules

A federal magistrate judge overseeing the combined multidistrict litigation against UnitedHealth Group, Change Healthcare, Optum and United HealthCare Services approved seven new security protocols governing how the dataset stolen in the 2024 Change Healthcare ransomware attack may be handled during the litigation. The rules, agreed by both parties before judicial approval, limit plaintiffs to a single complete copy of the data on a federally certified encrypted drive, require re-encryption to current standards with passwords transmitted separately, mandate that any computer used to process the data be fully air-gapped with wireless disabled, cap small working extracts at 25 individuals’ records behind complex 16-character-plus passwords, require detailed chain-of-custody logging visible to the defendants, set a 48-hour window to report any unauthorized access, and require destruction of all copies within 30 days after the litigation concludes. The order does not change what happened in the original 2024 breach; it governs how the already-stolen patient data may be used and secured going forward inside the litigation itself.
