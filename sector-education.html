The University of Copenhagen (UCPH) says an attack detected on Tuesday 20 October 2026, and then brought under control, gave hackers access to one of its test environments. That environment held data on about 525,000 people: full name, Danish national identity number, work and home phone numbers and personal email address. No group has claimed the attack, and UCPH has not said how the attackers got in. It is the second Danish university breach this month, after the Technical University of Denmark’s identity-system breach (reported in early October). The lesson is the location: a test environment loaded with real national ID numbers for half a million people, because non-production copies of identity and student data are often protected less well than the systems they mirror (editorial assessment).

Chrome was due to stop trusting an eMudhra root on the morning of 27 October — about 30,800 certificates were affected, 99.9% of them issued through InCommon, so check for any campus site still serving one

The private keys of eight eMudhra intermediate certificate authorities, created at an April 2025 key ceremony, were generated with so little randomness that a researcher recovered all of them almost instantly, and browsers blocked those intermediates from 17 October. eMudhra says it never issued certificates from them and was not breached, and no misuse has been confirmed. The fallout lands on higher education. Mozilla decided on 20 October to stop trusting the emSign ECC Root CA – G3, and Chrome’s removal was scheduled to reach users on the morning of 27 October (Pacific time). Chrome counted about 30,800 unexpired certificates under that root on 21 October, 99.9% of them issued through InCommon. Google said affected operators must immediately install a certificate from another hierarchy, and warns that standard ACME clients will not renew early unless they support ACME Renewal Information or an administrator forces it. Cornell, with more than 300 affected certificates, is telling holders to request RSA certificates, which chain to an eMudhra root that is not being removed for now; Michigan warns that root could be distrusted later and points to Let’s Encrypt. On 22 October eMudhra said it will leave the global TLS certificate business.
