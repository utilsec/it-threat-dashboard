CrowdStrike: one attacker driving the ARTEX agentic pentest tool with AI models breached several South Korean financial firms

CrowdStrike reported on 7 October 2026 that attacker directories it found exposed online show one operator using ARTEX, an open-source agentic penetration-testing tool developed in China, with several large language models to break into several South Korean financial organisations between late September and early October and take data. CrowdStrike names no victims; Korean press, cited by The Hacker News, names Shinhan and Yegaram Savings Bank. CrowdStrike assesses, with moderate confidence, a Chinese-speaking, financially motivated actor. The Financial Services Commission has ordered banks and card companies to audit every internet-facing system, and regulators warn of phishing and loan scams using the stolen data.

High Supply Chain

Ledger confirms a hidden hardware implant in wallets sold by a grey-market reseller was used to steal recovery phrases — buyers told not to set the devices up, and sellers across Asia asked to halt sales

Ledger confirmed on 12 October 2026 that a device belonging to one of the users whose funds had been drained contained an unauthorised hardware implant. Every drain report it has traced leads back to devices sold by CryptoBilis, a Malaysia-based reseller serving South-East Asia outside Ledger’s recommended sales channels. Ledger told buyers from that reseller not to set their devices up, or to move their assets to a new signer with a new recovery phrase, and BleepingComputer reported on 14 October that it had asked sellers across Asia to stop sales while it investigates. Ledger calls the number of affected devices limited, has given no victim count or loss total, and says it is working with the authorities; no actor has been identified. Funds were drained on 9 October. According to The Record, the implant read the recovery phrase from the screen during setup and sent it out over a mobile-data link through an embedded eSIM, leaving the secure element untouched so the device can still pass authenticity checks; that technical detail comes from independent researchers, not Ledger, and Ledger has not confirmed which model was tampered with. For exchanges, custodians, funds and treasury teams that hold digital assets on hardware signers, this is a procurement lesson: a genuine-looking device bought through an unofficial channel is a supply-chain risk to the keys themselves.

High Campaign

The FBI and Secret Service say the FortiBleed credential campaign against Fortinet firewalls and SSL VPNs is still running — no CVE, just leaked passwords, weak legacy hashes and sniffed logins, with ransomware following

The FBI and the US Secret Service published a joint advisory on 6 October 2026 warning that the campaign researchers call FortiBleed remains an active threat to internet-facing FortiGate firewalls and SSL VPN gateways. It has no CVE: in the agencies’ words it exploits reused or leaked credentials and legacy SHA-256 password storage. Researchers describe credential stuffing and spraying from leak dumps, captured password hashes sent to a GPU cracking cluster, and a Go-based tool, FortigateSniffer, that passively captures authentication traffic across 24 protocols. Some victims were locked out of their own devices after the attackers changed administrator passwords or deleted accounts, and the authorities say compromised FortiGates are being used as a way in for ransomware, with the advisory linking Payload ransomware. SOCRadar says it has confirmed at least 12 ransomware deployments from this access; the figure of more than 86,644 working device credentials across 194 countries dates from 19 June, when SOCRadar and Hudson Rock first documented the campaign, and is not a new count. The agencies say targets span all 16 US critical-infrastructure sectors, financial services among them. Researchers describe the operator as a Russian-speaking initial-access broker with overlaps to INC and Lynx ransomware; that is their assessment, not the agencies’. Editorial assessment: banks and credit unions that run FortiGate at branches or for remote access should treat this as a credential incident in progress rather than a patch job.

Critical Breach

Bitget’s investigators say the way in was two compromised third-party security appliances, one reached through a zero-day — Mandiant puts the hot-wallet loss at $351.6–387.5 million, and the North Korea link is still Bitget’s and blockchain analysts’ assessment, not a government attribution

The cryptocurrency exchange Bitget disclosed on 25 September 2026 that attackers had taken digital assets from its hot wallets. Two documents published since explain how. Mandiant’s status update of 28 September, released by Bitget, says the attackers gained unauthorised privileged access to two unnamed third-party security appliances, referred to as A and B, planted a web shell with a command-and-control connection on appliance B, and used that foothold to push malicious packages to the production server that runs wallet jobs. Mandiant dates the initial compromise to 24 September; the unauthorised transfers were detected at 02:31 (UTC+8) on 25 September and continued until about 05:23, and it estimates the loss at between $351.6 million and $387.5 million across several chains. Cold wallets were not affected and Mandiant found no evidence that private keys were compromised. On 1 October Bitget said its investigation had found malicious activity involving third-party security products, including a zero-day, and had recovered the custom tool used to start the withdrawals (The Hacker News). SlowMist’s report, as summarised by The Hacker News, puts the earliest malicious activity on 31 August, when a service on one of Product A’s nodes was hit by the zero-day and the attacker read an environment variable holding a database password; it describes similar activity on two more nodes on 23 and 25 September, access to Product B’s management platform with an employee’s identity on 25 September, and withdrawals across 11 blockchains. Mandiant’s and SlowMist’s start dates differ, and neither the appliance vendors nor the vulnerability has been named. About $1.1 million has been frozen by Circle, Tether and NEAR Intents. Correction: earlier editions of this page carried Bitget’s revised figure of about $390.06 million; this edition uses Mandiant’s range. On attribution this page stays with the evidence: Bitget’s chief executive, Gracy Chen, says IP behaviour and on-chain analysis are consistent with known North Korean groups; TRM Labs points to overlaps with TraderTraitor, and Elliptic links the funds to earlier North Korea-attributed thefts, including addresses tied to the 2025 Bybit theft. Mandiant’s published update makes no attribution, and no government has attributed the theft. The lesson has moved from the signing path to the security stack itself. The systems the attacker used were products bought to protect the network, and they had the reach and the credentials to touch wallet infrastructure. For any exchange, custodian, bank or payments firm, security appliances and their management planes belong in the same tier as the systems that move money: patched as emergencies, reachable only from management networks, watched for new files and outbound connections, and never holding secrets that open the funds path.

The data set is narrow, but a name paired with a real account number is exactly what a convincing phishing email or phone call to a brokerage client needs, and it is another case on this page of client data held outside the firm ending up in an attacker’s hands.

High Breach

Revolut’s forged government requests came from a hijacked Italian Interior Ministry certified-mail account — about 680 high-value customers exposed over five to six months, and the breach now has a second act: a smishing wave that lands inside genuine Revolut text threads and fakes the video identity check — and on 25 September a second notice: DriveWealth, the US broker that once carried Revolut customers’ US-stock trades, was breached through social engineering

Revolut has confirmed that fraudsters sent it fake government data requests, that its compliance process accepted them as genuine, and that customer data was released. The disclosure was first reported on 12 September 2026 and widely covered on 14 September. Revolut’s own description is that “an unauthorized third party utilized a legitimate government agency domain email to submit fraudulent requests for information”. Updated 17–18 September: SecurityWeek, citing Hudson Rock and other investigators, reports that the domain was genuine because the account was: infostealer malware had captured the login of an employee of Italy’s Ministry of the Interior on its certified-email (PEC) system, and Hudson Rock found around 300 compromised logins for that domain in infostealer logs. The forged requests were sent to Revolut Bank UAB in Lithuania and, per the same reporting, ran for five to six months. American Banker reports the Lithuanian entity appears to have been chosen because EU law obliges it to answer European Investigation Orders, and that about 680 customers were affected — described as high-net-worth and crypto “whales” — none of them in the US. Revolut itself still says only “a limited number of customers” and has not named the agency. An actor calling itself IAmNotAVillain posted a $3M ransom demand on 16 September; Revolut says it “has not received any direct contact or demand”. Earlier reporting had cited a 10,000 BTC demand. The same actor claims 147 GB taken from an Italian law-enforcement agency, which Italian police are investigating. The 680-customer, five-month and $3M figures come from researchers and the actor, not from Revolut. What was handed over is unusually complete: names, dates of birth, occupations, addresses, email addresses and phone numbers, passport and driver’s licence copies, identity-verification selfies, statements with IBANs, and transaction and crypto history. Revolut says its systems and customer funds were not affected and that it has notified law enforcement, data-protection authorities and financial regulators. The new detail removes the comfortable reading of this incident. The request did not come from a look-alike domain that a careful analyst could have spotted; it came from a real government mailbox whose credentials were sitting in infostealer logs, so every check that validates the sender’s domain, headers or reference format would have passed. Law-enforcement request desks, subpoena and court-order intake, emergency disclosure requests and regulator information demands all end at a small team authorised to release exactly this dossier, and most verify the request rather than the requester. The control that works is an out-of-band callback to a number the institution already holds for that authority, dual authorisation before any bulk identity or transaction export, and volume alerting on the request desk itself — a single requester drawing hundreds of high-value customer files over months is a pattern somebody should have seen. It is also a reporting event: under DORA the initial notification clock for a major ICT-related incident runs in hours, and US banking institutions sit under a 36-hour supervisory notification rule, both starting from classification. Updated 21 September: the follow-on has arrived. Malwarebytes, reported by Infosecurity Magazine, has tracked a smishing campaign against Revolut customers running since 14 September, two days after the disclosure. The texts warn that the recipient must confirm their identity or lose access, and because they arrive inside the existing Revolut SMS conversation on the phone they read as a continuation of genuine bank messages. The link opens a page that asks for camera access and imitates Revolut’s live-video identity check before asking for the password — a convincing liveness step followed by a credential screen, which Malwarebytes says is enough to take over an account outright. Several hundred accounts are thought to have been affected, with high-net-worth crypto users singled out. American Banker’s 18 September account, updated 20 September, confirms the vector: credentials for Italy’s PEC certified-mail system tied to the Interior Ministry domain, believed bought from infostealer logs, used to send requests over several months to Revolut Bank UAB in Lithuania; Revolut has notified data-protection authorities and financial regulators, and the OCC’s conditional approval of its US national-bank charter earlier this month is contingent on information-security compliance. Whether the smishing list is the stolen dossier or an opportunistic mailing is not established, but the targeting matches. For every other institution the lesson is the sequence: a breach of customer contact data is followed, within days, by a campaign that impersonates the victim firm’s own security process, and any bank whose app uses a video or selfie check has just watched that check be faked. Updated 25 September — a second, separate incident: The Register reports that DriveWealth, a US brokerage that provides execution and clearing services, has notified customers that a “sophisticated social engineering campaign” on 4–5 September exposed historic customer data, and that the people affected include Revolut customers who used to trade US stocks through DriveWealth. Revolut moved its UK, EEA and Australian customers off that arrangement between December 2023 and June 2025, so the exposed records are legacy data. Per the notices as quoted, the data includes names, email and postal addresses, phone numbers, employment information, country of citizenship, age, gender and partial DriveWealth account numbers; passwords, payment and card details and bank account information were not affected. DriveWealth warns the data “could potentially be used for identity fraud, impersonation, further social engineering, or unsolicited contact”; Revolut says its own systems were not compromised and customer funds and investments remain safe. Neither company has given a count, DriveWealth’s website carried no public notice at the time of writing, and only one outlet has reported it so far — treat the detail as provisional. It is a different company and a different vector from the forged-request breach, but for the same customers it means a second set of contact and identity details in circulation in one month, which is exactly the raw material the smishing wave above needs. It is also a reminder that customer data held by a former brokerage, custody or banking-as-a-service partner does not leave your risk register when the customers migrate: ask former partners what they still retain and on what deletion schedule.

Medium Incident

Hilltop National Bank has brought its digital services back after taking every system offline on 9 September — and across more than two weeks of updates it has not said what happened or whether data left

Hilltop National Bank of Casper, Wyoming, a $1.14 billion institution with eight offices and about $988 million in deposits, has been operating without its core systems since 9 September 2026, American Banker reported on 15 September. The bank says its IT systems began degrading on 8 September; overnight on 9 September it confirmed a cybersecurity incident and took every system offline, closing all offices that day. Five offices reopened on 10 September with limited service, and by 15 September seven of eight were open, but online and mobile banking and the public website were still down, scheduled bill payments were suspended with the bank reimbursing late fees, debit cards were capped at $1,000 a day, and branches were handling only deposits, withdrawals and internal checks. President and chief executive Darren Cantlay acknowledged the incident in a video message to customers. The bank has notified the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency and the Federal Reserve Bank of Kansas City, which is what the 36-hour computer-security incident notification rule requires. What it has not done is name a cause: it has not called the incident ransomware, no extortion group had claimed it on a leak site as of the reporting, and there is no statement on whether customer data left its systems. Those gaps should be read as unknowns rather than reassurance. The shape of the event — every system down at once, a week-plus rebuild, services restored in order of how manual they can be made — is the shape a community bank should plan around whatever the cause turns out to be. Most institutions of this size run their core, online banking and card processing through a small number of providers, and the fallback when all of it is unavailable is a branch with a cash drawer, a card limit and a hand-written ledger. That is survivable for a day; it is a different problem in week two, when payroll, mortgage and loan payments start to miss. Updated 21–22 September: the bank’s own update site reports personal mobile banking restored on 21 September along with in-branch debit-card printing, then personal and business online banking that evening, and on 22 September business online banking with saved ACH and transfer templates and the business mobile app. Business positive pay — the check-fraud control that matches presented items against a business customer’s issued list — was the one service explicitly still offline. Transactions from the outage were processed manually and a backlog is still being worked; ATM balances were expected to lag until the backlog cleared. On 14 September the bank said it had “no evidence” of any impact to customers’ account balances, but across every update it has not named a cause, called the event ransomware, or said whether customer data was exposed. It also told customers that only information from its Facebook page and update site is valid, which is the right message for a bank whose customers are about to be targeted by recovery-themed fraud. For peers, the positive-pay gap is the detail to copy: business customers who lose positive pay lose their main defence against altered and counterfeit checks, so a restoration plan should say when that control returns and what the bank does with exception items until it does. Updated 23–24 September: the bank’s update site reported person-to-person payments, external transfers and business bill pay restored on 23 September, and on 24 September posted what reads as a closing note thanking customers for their patience. Positive pay was the one service listed as unavailable on 21 September; later updates do not mention it. None of the updates names a cause, mentions ransomware or says whether customer data was exposed.
