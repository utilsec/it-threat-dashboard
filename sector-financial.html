Top Story

Roughly 4,000 bitcoin drained from the Liquid Network federation wallet — and most, but not all, came back

Attackers took roughly 4,000 bitcoin, worth about $320 million, out of the Liquid Network federation wallet on 6 September 2026 by exploiting a bug in Elements, the software underpinning the Bitcoin sidechain operated with Blockstream. The following day about 3,400 BTC — some 85 percent of the haul — was sent back to a Liquid Federation address, an unusual outcome that has not been fully explained. Roughly 598.5 BTC, near $47 million, remained in attacker hands as of 8 September. The network stayed paused while the federation worked through the defect, which meant holders of L-BTC could not redeem their tokens for bitcoin. For financial institutions the transferable point is not the cryptocurrency: a defect in the code that governs custody and settlement froze redemption for every participant at once, regardless of any individual firm’s controls.

High Breach

Trezor says a former fulfilment vendor still held customer records it had certified as deleted — roughly 80,689 US customers now exposed

Hardware-wallet maker Trezor disclosed on 5 September 2026 that a breach at its former fulfilment provider ShipMonk exposed a further 67,000 US customers, taking the reported total to about 80,689 once an earlier tranche of 13,689 is included. The records cover orders placed between November 2019 and August 2021 and contain names, email addresses, phone numbers, shipping addresses and order numbers. Trezor stated plainly that throughout the relationship it had repeatedly requested and received written assurance that the data had been deleted in line with its contract and data policy — and that the data was nonetheless still present in ShipMonk’s systems. The underlying compromise is attributed by blockchain security firm Holborn to the ShinyHunters extortion group, which reportedly reached ShipMonk through CVE-2026-72898, a critical SQL injection flaw in Metabase carrying a CVSS score of 10.0. ShipMonk has not publicly acknowledged the incident. Trezor is clear that its wallets themselves are unaffected; the exposure is that a list of confirmed crypto-hardware owners with home addresses and phone numbers is an unusually well-qualified target set for phishing, vishing, impersonation and, given the addresses, physical coercion. For banks, insurers, brokers and payment firms the transferable lesson has nothing to do with crypto. This is a fourth-party retention failure: a vendor kept data past a contractual deletion date, attested in writing that it had not, and the attesting firm carried the notification duty when the attestation proved wrong. Written deletion assurances are not evidence of deletion, and DORA, the FCA regime and most US supervisory expectations put the burden on the regulated firm regardless of who actually held the file.

High Breach

The 153-million driver-licence listing has moved from allegation to litigation — IDScan is notifying business customers and class actions are filed

A dark-web identity-theft service calling itself Nexus surfaced in late August 2026 offering searchable digital scans of more than 153 million US and Canadian driver licences, alongside about 10 million identification cards, roughly 3 million travel documents and some 579,000 medical cards. Brian Krebs reported on 1 September that he had verified the listing by searching the database and traced it back to IDScan.net, a Louisiana identity-verification firm whose technology is used by car-rental firms, retailers, gun shops and financial institutions. The story has moved on since. IDScan began notifying its business customers around 1 September; by 4 September multiple US law firms had filed or announced class actions in Louisiana, and the FBI’s New Orleans office had opened an investigation. The Nexus site is no longer online, but BleepingComputer reports that criminals retain access to the underlying database. IDScan.net has published no statement and did not respond to press enquiries, so the breach remains unconfirmed by the vendor and the number of individuals affected is unknown. For banks, insurers and fintechs the exposure sits directly under the onboarding stack. Remote account opening, step-up verification and contact-centre re-identification lean on a photographed government ID, and reporting describes multi-image captures — the output of an automated document scanner rather than a consumer phone upload. A corpus of authentic scans lets an attacker satisfy a document check without ever holding the document, which is exactly the assumption most know-your-customer flows are built on.

High Threat Actor

CrowdStrike profiles Slim Spider — a financially motivated actor that has learned how Brazilian instant payments and bank cloud actually work

CrowdStrike published research on 8 September 2026 on Slim Spider, a financially motivated adversary it has tracked against Brazilian financial institutions since at least March 2026. What distinguishes the group is domain knowledge rather than novel malware: it understands Pix instant payments, digital-asset platforms and the cloud services financial firms run underneath them, and it aims at the money rather than at resale data. In a late-March intrusion at a Brazilian bank, CrowdStrike observed custom Bash scripts querying cloud instance metadata to lift temporary cloud credentials, moving them out over socket connections, and then pursuing crypto assets and instant-payment accounts. The tradecraft is worth reading outside Brazil. Instance-metadata abuse is a generic cloud weakness — a workload that can reach the metadata endpoint can often mint short-lived credentials for whatever role it runs as, and those credentials rarely trigger the alerting that a stolen human password would. Pair that with an actor who already knows your payment initiation flow and your fraud thresholds, and the result is theft that looks like ordinary automated activity. UK, EU and US institutions running instant-payment schemes on cloud infrastructure face the same structural exposure even though this actor’s current targeting is regional.

Arctic Wolf detailed a data-theft and extortion cluster it tracks as PREY-0058 on 7 September 2026. The entry point is the IT service desk: the actor calls posing as a senior employee, talks an agent into a credential or MFA reset, then uses adversary-in-the-middle infrastructure to capture the session token rather than the password. Sign-ins arrive through residential proxy addresses, so the geolocation looks unremarkable, and from there the group moves into Microsoft 365 and other connected SaaS to stage data for extortion. Arctic Wolf notes tradecraft overlapping with the cluster Mandiant tracks as UNC6671. The targeting selection matters for banks, insurers and asset managers. It is also the population most likely to get an expedited help-desk reset because the caller sounds senior and impatient. Nothing here requires a vulnerability; it requires an identity-verification step at the service desk that a confident caller cannot talk past.

High Phishing

BigBear 2.0 bypassed MFA at 258 organisations and banked more than 5,000 Microsoft 365 credentials

Researchers reported on 7 September 2026 that a phishing-as-a-service platform called BigBear 2.0 defeated multi-factor authentication at 258 organisations and harvested over 5,000 Microsoft 365 credentials. The mechanism is adversary-in-the-middle session-token theft: the service proxies the genuine Microsoft login, relays whatever challenge the real site issues, and keeps the authenticated session cookie once the victim completes it. From the user’s side the sign-in succeeds normally, which is why these campaigns generate so few reports. For financial firms this is the same failure mode as the Outsider kit tracked elsewhere on this page, and the repetition is the point — the commodity phishing market has standardised on defeating the MFA that most institutions treat as their compensating control for credential theft. SMS codes, email codes and push approvals are all inside what these platforms handle. What they do not handle is an origin-bound FIDO2 credential, because the browser will not release it to a look-alike domain. Everything else is detection and response after the session is already stolen.

Critical Threat Actor

BREEZE COMET is inside Brazilian banks and payment processors — and it is pushing hundreds of fraudulent transfers through Pix, STR and Boleto

Google Threat Intelligence profiled BREEZE COMET on 1 September 2026, a financially motivated intrusion set previously tracked as UNC5669 that overlaps with activity others call Plump Spider. Its objective is the payment rails themselves rather than resale data. The pattern is repeatable: compromise a Brazilian bank, processor, exchange, fintech or banking-software provider; escalate until it holds access to the National Financial System Network and the mutual-TLS credentials that authenticate payment messages; learn the victim’s own transfer procedures and anti-fraud thresholds; then execute. Two documented waves pushed hundreds of fraudulent transactions through Pix, the STR reserves-transfer system and Boleto inside twenty-four to forty-eight hour windows. Initial access has come from password spraying, voice phishing to get AnyDesk installed, malware staged on compromised Brazilian municipal domains and rogue physical devices planted in retail store networks. Once inside, the group mines CI/CD systems and secret stores for hardcoded credentials and API keys using payment-specific search terms. The transferable risk is the interior trust boundary between a bank’s corporate estate and its payment-messaging infrastructure — mTLS certificates left in a build pipeline are not a Brazilian problem, and Google reports expansion indicators toward Paraguay, Venezuela, Nigeria and Ghana.

High Phishing

The Outsider phishing kit shrugged off an FBI and Google takedown and rebuilt with 700 new domains — and its whole point is defeating your MFA

Group-IB published research on 3 September 2026 on Outsider, a phishing-as-a-service kit that has proved unusually resilient. Between December 2025 and May 2026 researchers attributed more than 100,000 phishing pages to it, across upwards of 10,000 unique domains. In June, Google filed a civil suit and Operation Ghost Hook — run with the FBI and Lumen’s Black Lotus Labs — seized the operation’s core admin servers, a Shopify storefront, thousands of domains and about 100,000 US dollars from its payment wallets. The kit came back: Group-IB counts more than 700 new domains created since. The commercial scale is the part worth internalising. Outsider ships 267 ready-made templates, has over 230 buyers and more than 5,000 Telegram subscribers, and targets financial services, brokerage firms, telecommunications, postal services, government systems and toll operators across more than fifty-four countries. Financial services and brokerage sit at the top of that target list because the fraud conversion is immediate. The capability that matters is adversary-in-the-middle: the kit proxies the real login in real time and dynamically serves whichever challenge the genuine site asks for — SMS code, emailed code, PIN or app push — then redirects the victim back through earlier pages so the flow feels normal. A one-time code entered into that page is relayed to the real site within seconds, and the operator keeps the authenticated session. This is the concrete argument for phishing-resistant authentication on customer and staff logins: SMS, email and push-approval factors are all inside what this kit already handles, while origin-bound FIDO2 credentials are not, because the browser refuses to release them to a look-alike domain.

Critical Vulnerability

Microsoft’s largest Patch Tuesday on record carries two Windows zero-days already being exploited

Microsoft released its September 2026 updates on 8 September, and by volume it is the largest Patch Tuesday the company has shipped: 974 CVEs by Microsoft’s own count, 999 including third-party entries, with BleepingComputer counting 966 Microsoft flaws and more than 100 rated critical. The number is not the operational story. Two of the fixes close vulnerabilities that attackers are already using: CVE-2026-81963, a link-following elevation-of-privilege flaw in the Windows Update Stack, and CVE-2026-85880, a heap-based buffer overflow in Windows ALPC. Both carry a CVSS score of 7.8, both yield SYSTEM, and CISA added both to the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog the same day. A 7.8 that is being exploited outranks an unexploited 9.8 in any sane patch queue, and both are local privilege escalation — the step that turns a phished session or a compromised contractor laptop into full control of a banking workstation, a trading desktop or a domain-joined server. For institutions under DORA or supervisory change-control expectations, the volume argues for triage rather than a single monolithic release: patch the two exploited flaws on an emergency track and let the remaining 900-plus follow normal cycles.

ConnectWise disclosed a new vulnerability in ScreenConnect Remote Access on 7 September 2026 without a fix in hand, publishing temporary mitigations and saying a patch would follow later in the week. A second critical NetScaler flaw is now drawing live attack traffic — CVE-2026-19490 walks past gateway authentication entirely

Citrix disclosed CVE-2026-19490 on 19 August 2026: an authentication bypass carrying a CVSS v4 base score of 9.3 in NetScaler ADC and NetScaler Gateway. Rapid7 records the affected builds as 14.1 before 14.1-73.32 and 13.1 before 13.1-63.21, with NetScaler ADC FIPS before 14.1-73.32 FIPS and FIPS/NDcPP before 13.1-37.277; systems are exploitable where a SAML action is configured or an authentication or VPN virtual server is in use. SonicWall SMA 1000 zero-days are vendor-confirmed as exploited and now in KEV — the same appliance family behind last year’s bank-vendor ransomware

CISA added two SonicWall SMA 1000 flaws to the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog on 2 September 2026: CVE-2026-83548, a server-side request forgery carrying a CVSS score of 10.0, and CVE-2026-83549, a post-authentication operating-system command injection scored 7.8. SonicWall’s product security team has confirmed zero-day exploitation, and analysis from Rapid7 and Sophos notes the two may be chained — the SSRF to reach the appliance interior without credentials, the command injection to run code once there. The SMA 1000 series is a secure mobile access appliance: it exists to publish internal applications to remote staff and third parties, which places it in the same architectural position as the NetScaler Gateway items above and gives it the same blast radius when it fails. For financial institutions there is a specific and recent precedent worth remembering. The ransomware attack on Marquis Software — a data-analytics and marketing vendor to hundreds of US banks and credit unions, whose breach eventually reached hundreds of thousands of consumers across dozens of institutions — was reported to have begun with a vulnerability in the company’s SonicWall firewall device. The pattern that produced that incident was not a bank being breached directly; it was a small vendor with an internet-facing security appliance and a slow patch cycle, holding customer data on behalf of institutions that carried the notification duty. Your own appliances are the first job here. Your vendors’ appliances are the second, and they are the ones you cannot patch yourself.

Critical KEV Additions

Seven new KEV entries on 2 September, and the ones to read first are the build-pipeline and AI-middleware flaws

CISA added seven vulnerabilities to the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog on 2 September 2026. Two are the SonicWall SMA 1000 flaws covered separately above. The remaining five are: CVE-2026-9586, SQL injection in Sangoma Switchvox, scored 9.3; CVE-2026-82329, improper authentication in JFrog Artifactory, scored 9.8, with reporting describing reverse shells and stolen administrator tokens; CVE-2026-48710, request smuggling in the Kludex Starlette Python framework, scored 6.5 and chained with CVE-2026-42271; CVE-2026-49869, command injection in Kestra OSS, scored 10.0, with reverse shells and cryptocurrency mining observed; and CVE-2026-59822, improper authentication in Berri LiteLLM’s Model Context Protocol handling, scored 8.8. For a financial-services reader the Artifactory entry is the one that should move first. Artifactory is where build artefacts, container images, signing material and deployment credentials live; an improper-authentication flaw there that yields administrator tokens is a route into the software supply chain of every application the institution builds, and it is precisely the class of internal credential store that BREEZE COMET searches for payment secrets in. Kestra is a workflow orchestrator, so a CVSS 10.0 command injection means arbitrary execution wherever data pipelines run. The LiteLLM entry is a reminder that AI middleware has now entered the exploited-in-the-wild population — those proxies commonly hold API keys for multiple model providers and often sit outside the change control applied to production services.

Critical Zero-Day

An unpatched Magento and Adobe Commerce zero-day is backdooring live stores — and the first symptom merchants notice is a wave of failed-payment emails

Sansec published research on 5 September 2026 on a zero-day it calls StyleSmuggler, an unauthenticated remote code execution flaw affecting current versions of Magento and Adobe Commerce, including the 2.4.9 line. It is under active exploitation and, at publication, unpatched — there is no vendor fix to apply. Sansec reports attackers using it to install persistent backdoors on merchant servers and lists indicators including a malicious domain, 247.cdnflare.xyz, and suspicious processes masquerading as kworker and gvfsd-user. Notably, Sansec flags an unusual early symptom: a spike in failed-payment emails, which is what a compromised checkout tends to produce when injected code interferes with the payment flow. That symptom is the reason this belongs on a financial-services page. Unauthenticated code execution on an e-commerce platform is the standard precondition for payment-page skimming: the attacker does not need the card database, only the page where the card is typed. The consequences run outward from the merchant. Acquirers and payment service providers carry the fraud and chargeback exposure from their merchant portfolios; card issuers absorb the downstream fraud; insurers underwrite the resulting claims; and payment-card industry compliance obligations land on merchants that many banks sponsor. The immediate task for an acquiring or sponsoring institution is portfolio-level: identify which merchants run Magento or Adobe Commerce, get virtual patching or web application firewall coverage in front of them, and raise monitoring on common-point-of-purchase signals over the coming fortnight.

High Vulnerability

An Exchange authentication bypass with roughly 22,000 unpatched servers still exposed — and email is where payment instructions get changed

Microsoft’s CVE-2026-62911, an authentication bypass in Exchange carrying a CVSS score of 8.0, was reported on 3 September 2026 alongside an estimate that around 22,000 internet-facing servers remain unpatched. No confirmed in-the-wild exploitation has been reported at the time of writing, which keeps this below the actively-exploited items above — but Exchange has a long history of the gap between disclosure and mass exploitation closing quickly, and the exposure count suggests a large population that has not moved. On-premises Exchange remains common in financial services for the reasons it is hard to remove: retention and supervision tooling built around it, regulatory archiving obligations, and hybrid deployments that were meant to be temporary and were not. The specific risk in this sector is business email compromise rather than data theft. Access to a mailbox in finance, treasury, client services or corporate lending is access to payment instructions, wire confirmations, invoice threads and settlement correspondence — and the fraud that follows does not need malware, only a plausible reply from a real internal address. An authentication bypass provides exactly that access, without the phishing step that most staff training and inbound filtering are designed to catch. Patch the servers, and take the opportunity to check that a change of bank details in an email thread cannot be actioned without out-of-band verification, since that control is what actually stops the loss.

Medium Policy

The US and UK sign a memorandum on dismantling South-East Asian scam compounds — the source of most of the fraud losses your customers report

On 4 September 2026 the US Department of Justice and the UK’s National Crime Agency and Crown Prosecution Service signed a memorandum of understanding to coordinate against the scam compounds operating in Myanmar, Cambodia and Laos that run investment and romance fraud at industrial scale. The figures behind it are the reason this is a financial-services item rather than a law-enforcement one: more than 12 billion US dollars was stolen from Americans in cyber scams last year, a figure officials acknowledge is undercounted because victims frequently do not report, and the FBI attributes almost 85 percent of all reported losses to cyber-enabled fraud schemes. Enforcement has begun to reach the money as well as the operations — agencies seized roughly 15 billion US dollars in bitcoin connected to Prince Group, described as a Chinese money-laundering front. For banks and payment firms, authorised push payment fraud originating in these compounds is the single largest driver of customer loss, reimbursement cost and complaint volume, and it is the fraud type least amenable to authentication controls, because the customer authorises the payment themselves after being socially engineered over weeks. Closer transatlantic coordination should improve intelligence flow and the odds of recovery, but it does not change the near-term operational picture. The controls that reduce loss remain behavioural: detecting the pattern of a victim being coached, intervening in the payment journey, and making it easy for a customer to stop rather than to proceed.

Medium Policy

The G7 and CISA tell organisations to start the post-quantum migration now — and the sector with the longest data-secrecy horizon is this one

On 4 September 2026 the G7 Cyber Security Working Group, alongside CISA, urged organisations to begin moving to post-quantum cryptography rather than waiting for quantum computers to arrive. The reasoning is the harvest-now, decrypt-later problem: encrypted traffic and stored data captured today can be decrypted once the capability exists, so anything that must remain confidential for a long time is already exposed. UK authorities have set 2035 as the deadline for completing the transition to quantum-resistant cryptography. The G7 has been pointed about the financial sector before — in 2024 it called on finance ministries and central banks to prepare for what it described as impending threats. The reason financial services keeps appearing in this guidance is the secrecy horizon. Mortgage files, life and annuity policy data, pension records, know-your-customer documents, long-dated derivative confirmations and payment-network keys all have to stay confidential for decades, which is precisely the horizon over which harvest-now, decrypt-later becomes real rather than theoretical. Nothing here is urgent in the incident-response sense, and nobody should reprioritise this week’s patching for it. What it does justify is starting the unglamorous first step now, because it is slow: a cryptographic inventory. Most institutions cannot currently answer which algorithms and key lengths protect which data, which certificates and hardware security modules are involved, and which third parties and payment interfaces would need to change in step — and that inventory is a multi-year job that no deadline makes shorter.
