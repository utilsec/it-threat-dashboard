Top Story

Justice Department seizes the platforms behind a China state-sponsored campaign that reached NASA, DOJ, Energy, HHS, NIH, the Federal Reserve and the US Senate

On 26 August 2026 the Justice Department and FBI announced court-authorised seizures, out of the Southern District of California, of domains hardcoded into two tools operated by the China state-sponsored group tracked as QTFY (also QT and QTCYBER). The first, QScan, automatically scanned for and infected thousands of internet-connected devices worldwide. The second, QTRouter, stitched those compromised devices together with commercial proxies and rented virtual servers into an obfuscation network, so that operational traffic appeared to originate from machines outside China rather than from the operators themselves. Officials said the seizures rendered both tools inoperable by taking down the communication and authentication functions they depended on. The Department named Nanjing Xinjiuwei Network Technology Company as QTFY’s employer and described it as a contractor supplying hacking services to the Ministry of State Security and the People’s Liberation Army. The victim list is unusually specific for a public announcement and is almost entirely federal: NASA, the Federal Reserve, the Department of Energy, the Department of Justice, the Department of Health and Human Services, the National Institutes of Health and the US Senate. A joint advisory published the same day by the FBI, NSA and Cyber National Mission Force names the defense industrial base, communications, government and higher education as the primary target sectors and carries tactics, infrastructure detail and indicators drawn from incident response. For government IT owners the operational point is the relay layer: because QTRouter routed through compromised third-party devices, the source addresses in agency logs would have looked ordinary. Hunting for this activity means working from the published indicators and behavioural patterns, not from geography.

High Breach

Court case-management breach at Thomson Reuters reaches a dozen US jurisdictions and Ontario — including sealed records

Thomson Reuters disclosed in the first days of September that attackers had unauthorised access to its environment from roughly 1 March to 29 June 2026, affecting C-Track, the court case-management platform its West Publishing unit operates for judiciaries. Notifications name courts in Alabama, Kentucky, Montana, Nevada, New Hampshire, North Dakota, Ohio, Pennsylvania, South Carolina, Tennessee and Wyoming, plus the US Virgin Islands and Ontario’s Ministry of the Attorney General; Minnesota courts were also reported affected. The data at risk includes names, Social Security numbers, driver’s licence numbers, dates of birth, medical and health-insurance information, case numbers, party addresses and charge descriptions — and, in some jurisdictions, sealed or confidential court documents. The company discovered the intrusion on 30 June but did not notify the affected courts until 23–27 July, and did not disclose publicly until early September. Two details make this a supplier-governance story rather than only a breach story. First, several courts said the exposed material was backup data held on Thomson Reuters’ own cloud servers, drawn from database copies provided for troubleshooting; at least one court reported backups it had not authorised. Second, the vendor stated the compromise occurred in its environment, not in court systems, which means no court could have detected or prevented it with its own controls. Sealed records are the part that cannot be remediated: credit monitoring, offered here for 12 months, does nothing for confidentiality that has already been lost.

High Ransomware

Berlin confirms data theft from its state administration after Rhysida lists 5.79 TB on a leak site

Berlin’s city-state administration confirmed at the end of August that attackers stole data from its networks, after the Rhysida ransomware group listed the city on its leak site on 28 August 2026. The published timeline is the instructive part. Data outflow was first flagged internally around 7 August and exfiltration continued until roughly 12 August; the affected departments — principally the Senate Department for Mobility, Transport, Climate Protection and the Environment, plus a second department — were disconnected from the state network on 14 August, and all Senate departments were reconnected on 23 August after forensic checks. That leaves roughly a week between the first indication and network isolation, during which the theft completed. Rhysida claims about 1.44 million files totalling 5.79 TB, including personnel and administrative files, payroll data, plaintext credentials, identity documents, SQL and PST archives, bank account numbers and internal analyses; those figures are the group’s own and are not confirmed. City officials stated Berlin will not pay, and said they found no evidence that data relating to the state parliament election scheduled for 20 September was affected. The state criminal police office, the public prosecutor and federal security bodies are investigating. For peer administrations the lesson is about the isolation decision, not the malware: the gap between detecting anomalous outflow and cutting the affected segment off is the window in which the loss actually happens, and it is a decision-making and authority problem before it is a tooling problem.

Medium Availability

Norway’s national digital identity gateway absorbs the largest DDoS its agency has recorded

Norway’s Digitalisation Agency (Digdir) said it was hit by the largest distributed denial-of-service attack it has experienced, beginning early in the week of 24 August 2026 and running for several days. The target set was the shared plumbing of Norwegian digital government: systems used by citizens and businesses, including ID-porten, the single sign-on that fronts a large number of public services. Digdir reported that services stayed available practically the whole time and that socially critical systems were not affected, so the operational outcome was closer to degradation than outage. A pro-Russian group calling itself Server Killers claimed responsibility on Telegram on 26 August and tied the campaign to Norway’s support for Ukraine; Norwegian authorities had not publicly confirmed the attribution, and the Police Security Service, the National Security Authority and Kripos are investigating. The reason this matters beyond Norway is architectural. Consolidating authentication into one national gateway is good security practice and good service design, and it also produces a single availability chokepoint: if ID-porten is unreachable, dozens of downstream services become unusable even though none of them was attacked. Agencies that have centralised login should know which of their services fail closed when the identity provider is degraded, and whether any of them have a documented fallback for staff and citizens.

Carried forward from the previous edition and re-checked for this one, because nothing has changed in a way that reduces the exposure. Latvia’s Road Traffic Safety Directorate (CSDD) confirmed on 19 August 2026 that attackers extracted records on 1.2 million individuals and 200,000 legal entities — roughly two-thirds of the national population. The exposed material is identity-dense rather than credential-dense: personal identity numbers, company registration numbers, licence plates, payment amounts and dates, and addresses taken from vehicle registration certificates dating back to 2008. Governance consequences followed quickly, with the supervisory board resigning. For government IT owners elsewhere this remains the archetypal national-registry failure mode: one authoritative identity system, reachable from the internet, operated under contract, where the operator’s detection duties went untested until an adversary tested them.

High Research

CISA red-teamed a government-sector organisation to full domain compromise and its SOC noticed nothing

CISA released advisory AA26-237A, “A Tale of Two SOCs”, on 25 August 2026, publishing two red team assessments run with comparable tradecraft against two critical-infrastructure organisations. Organisation A is a government services and facilities entity; Organisation B is a water and wastewater utility. Both were compromised at the domain level. The difference was detection: Organisation B’s SOC identified and isolated phishing payloads within two to twenty minutes and stopped the intrusion spreading, while at Organisation A the entire operation went unnoticed. The path into Organisation A is depressingly ordinary. Default credentials on a web application gave initial access; Active Directory Certificate Services misconfiguration provided privilege escalation; cleartext credentials and static, non-expiring AWS access keys carried the team into sensitive systems. The advisory also flags default Machine Account Quota settings and over-permissioned Entra ID applications. There is no CVE here and nothing to patch, which is precisely why the advisory is worth an hour of a government security team’s time: every finding is a configuration or process decision that an audit would likely mark compliant. CISA’s own conclusion is the line to take to leadership — detection tools are only as effective as the people, processes and procedures behind them. Two organisations with similar tooling produced opposite outcomes because one had a SOC that was practised at acting on what the tooling said.

Medium Phishing

Phishing campaign spanning 46 countries installs legitimate remote-management software, using government-themed lures

ANY.RUN published analysis on 3 September 2026 of a phishing campaign that reached 46 countries and 601 observed cases, with the United States accounting for about 45 per cent of activity. The payload is not malware in the conventional sense: victims are led to install genuine remote monitoring and management software, which then gives the operator interactive remote access under a signed, legitimate binary that endpoint tooling is often configured to permit. Government is named among the targeted sectors alongside education, technology, banking and finance, and manufacturing — and the lures lean heavily on public-sector identity, impersonating Canada Revenue Agency tax forms and US Social Security Administration notices as well as shipping and invoice themes. The delivery infrastructure is deliberately disposable: 425 kit URLs across 240 hosts, 94 per cent of which were seen for a single day only, rotated through Vercel, GitHub Pages, Netlify and compromised sites. Blocking domains is therefore close to useless. The researchers’ recommendation is to detect the chain instead — recurring assets such as a shared font file and images, and the consistent secure.html to project archive structure — and to build detections that survive the operators switching RMM vendors, which they do routinely. For agencies the defensive question is narrow and answerable: which RMM products are approved in your estate, and does anything stop an unprivileged user installing one that is not?

Re-checked for this edition and unchanged. CISA and the FBI republished #StopRansomware advisory AA25-071A on 18 August 2026. The cumulative tally passed 500 critical-infrastructure organisations as of April 2026, up from roughly 300 in March 2025. Government facilities remain among the group’s core target sectors alongside healthcare, critical manufacturing, the defence industrial base, IT and financial services. The operationally load-bearing finding is about speed rather than volume: the agencies report that Medusa weaponises newly disclosed vulnerabilities within roughly 24 hours, and has been observed using exploits up to a week before public disclosure — consistent with an operation that buys working exploits rather than developing them. That timeline is what public-sector IT leaders should act on. The practical response is to split the estate: internet-facing and remote-access assets move to an emergency track measured in hours to days, driven by CISA KEV and vendor PSIRT feeds, while everything else stays on the routine cycle. Where a patch cannot land inside that window, compensating controls — removing the service from the internet, restricting management interfaces, adding authentication in front of it — are the fallback, not a deferral request. This edition’s KEV traffic, sixteen entries in eight days, is exactly the tempo the advisory describes.

Medium Espionage

A backdoor that replaces the organisation’s own HAProxy binary, timestamp and all

Rapid7 Labs published research on 4 September 2026 on an implant it calls “ted”, found at two South Korean organisations — one automotive, one media — and attributed with medium confidence to North Korean state-sponsored activity spanning the APT37, Lazarus and Kimsuky clusters. No government victim is named, and the technique rather than the victimology is why it belongs on a government dashboard. Ted exploits no vulnerability in HAProxy. After the operator already has root, the stager overwrites the legitimate HAProxy binary with a trojanised build and copies the creation timestamp from an unrelated system file so the replacement does not stand out in a directory listing. The implant then rides the reverse proxy that fronts the organisation’s web estate: it can intercept traffic, serve altered pages to selected visitors, take commands, move files and replace its own configuration — and it erases its activity from HAProxy’s own connection logs. Companion trojanised binaries reportedly include sshd, which captures passwords, plus agetty, atd and polkitd, alongside a separate implant that beacons every twelve hours. Both victims ran an HAProxy build from November 2024. For public-sector estates the takeaway is that a reverse proxy is a service-delivery component nobody usually monitors for integrity, and that timestamps are not evidence. File-integrity monitoring on the binaries and packages of internet-facing service hosts is the control that catches this.

Two SonicWall SMA 1000 zero-days exploited in the wild, KEV-listed with a 5 September federal deadline

SonicWall published an advisory on 2 September 2026 confirming active exploitation of two flaws in its SMA 1000 secure remote access appliances, and CISA added both to the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog the same day with a remediation deadline of 5 September for federal civilian agencies. The SMA 1000 line is marketed to large enterprises, government and critical infrastructure, so the installed base is concentrated exactly where the consequences are highest, and the appliance sits at the perimeter of the remote-access path — a compromise here is a credential and session problem, not only a host problem.

Seven flaws added to KEV on 2 September — and three of them are AI and automation infrastructure

CISA added seven vulnerabilities to the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog on 2 September 2026. Alongside the two SonicWall SMA 1000 entries are CVE-2026-9586, SQL injection in Sangoma Switchvox (CVSS 9.3); CVE-2026-82329, improper authentication in JFrog Artifactory (9.8); CVE-2026-49869, OS command injection in Kestra OSS (10.0); CVE-2026-59822, improper authentication in Berri LiteLLM (8.8); and CVE-2026-48710, HTTP request smuggling in the Starlette Python framework (6.5). The exploitation reported against the last three is worth reading carefully, because it is not espionage — operators used the Kestra flaw to establish a reverse shell, enumerate the Docker container environment and deploy a cryptocurrency miner, and abused LiteLLM authentication weaknesses to drop XMRig and extract API credentials and model configurations. That is commodity monetisation of AI and workflow-orchestration infrastructure, and it is a warning shot for agencies standing up AI pilots. These components rarely appear in a CMDB, are often deployed by data or platform teams outside the normal server build process, and hold exactly the credentials — model API keys, artifact repository tokens, orchestration secrets — that make onward movement easy. Artifactory in particular sits in the build chain, where a compromise becomes a software supply-chain problem.

Critical Advisory

A NetScaler flaw Citrix patched in June came under attack within days of a public proof of concept

CISA added CVE-2026-8452 to the KEV catalog on 26 August 2026 as part of a six-vulnerability batch, with a remediation deadline of 29 August. The flaw is a memory overflow in Citrix NetScaler ADC and Gateway when configured as a Gateway — SSL VPN, ICA Proxy, CVPN or RDP Proxy — or as an AAA virtual server. Citrix disclosed and fixed it on 30 June 2026 in builds 14.1-72.61, 13.1-63.18 and 13.1-37.272, characterising it as a denial-of-service issue. Researchers at watchTowr Labs published technical detail and proof-of-concept code on 14 August showing it could be chained into unauthenticated remote code execution, and exploitation followed shortly after, with attackers dropping web shells named x.php and z.php and running basic discovery commands. The sequence is the lesson: a flaw that was triaged as availability-only under its original vendor description became a perimeter RCE the moment someone published the chain, and the estates most likely to be exposed are the ones that deprioritised a June DoS patch. The same 26 August batch added CVE-2019-1068 in Microsoft SQL Server, which has had a patch available for seven years, plus older Linux kernel, Red Hat ABRT, Red Hat libuser and Ajax.NET Professional flaws linked to a cybercrime group targeting Windows and Linux web servers. A further three entries followed on 27 August, including an actively exploited Linux kernel IPv6 privilege escalation, CVE-2026-53362.

Cisco Nexus 9000 switches expose two management ports that allow unauthenticated root code execution

Cisco published an advisory on 2 September 2026 for CVE-2026-20212, a CVSS 9.8 flaw affecting Silicon One-based Nexus 9000 series switches. A service binds to an unrestricted IP address, leaving TCP ports 43210 and 43211 reachable, and an unauthenticated remote attacker who can reach those ports can execute code with root privileges. Ten models are named, including the N9K-C9804 and N9K-C9808, and the affected software spans NX-OS 10.3(1) through 10.6(3s) — 45 releases. The fix is NX-OS 10.6(4) or later; where that upgrade cannot be scheduled immediately, Cisco offers an infrastructure ACL blocking the two ports and a Live Protect shield with limited version coverage. Cisco states the flaw was not known to be exploited at disclosure, which is what keeps this below the actively-exploited items on this page rather than above them. It still deserves priority handling in government data centres for two reasons. First, the precondition is network reachability of an obscure port pair rather than any authentication step, so the control that matters is whether your data-centre fabric management plane is segmented at all. Second, a root compromise on a spine or leaf switch is not an application incident — it sits below the security controls that most agency monitoring is built around. Cisco released a separate hardening bundle for IOS XR the same day covering seven CVEs, two of them also rated 9.8.

High Advisory

Google patches a Chrome V8 zero-day already being exploited — the sixth of 2026

Google shipped a Chrome stable update on 4 September 2026 fixing CVE-2026-85046, a type-confusion flaw in the V8 JavaScript and WebAssembly engine that is confirmed exploited in the wild. The fixed builds are 152.0.7977.82 and .83 on Windows and macOS and 152.0.7977.82 on Linux; the issue is rated CVSS 8.8. Technically, the bug lets an array holding packed elements receive the wrong element-kind map, giving an attacker arbitrary read and write on the JavaScript heap and, from there, code execution inside the renderer sandbox from a crafted web page. Google has not published targeting details, which is normal for a zero-day still being investigated and should not be read as reassurance. This is the sixth Chrome zero-day patched in 2026. For government estates the browser is the highest-volume attack surface there is, and it is also the one where patch compliance is most often assumed rather than measured, because Chrome updates itself and staff simply do not restart. That is the gap to close here: a browser that has downloaded the update but has not been relaunched is still running the vulnerable build. The same fix applies to Chromium-derived browsers — Edge, Brave, Opera, Vivaldi and others — on their own vendors’ release schedules, which lag Google’s by days.

Active — Google confirms an exploit exists in the wild; targeting not disclosed Not in CISA KEV as of this edition
Fixed in Chrome 152.0.7977.82/.83; Chromium-based browsers follow separately Action: Do not treat Chrome’s auto-update as compliance. Force the update through your management platform and require a browser relaunch, then report on the actual running version across the fleet rather than the installed version — the two diverge on machines that are never rebooted. Extend the same check to Chromium-derived browsers in your estate, which pick up the V8 fix on their vendors’ schedules. Where you run a managed browser policy, confirm site isolation is enabled and that unnecessary extensions are blocked, since renderer-level exploitation is the first step rather than the last.

Medium Advisory

A twelve-year-old PostgreSQL flaw turns a backup account’s replication right into server takeover

Cyera published analysis on 1 September 2026 of CVE-2026-6471, nicknamed PostGREShell, a missing-authorisation flaw in PostgreSQL’s logical decoding that has been present since version 9.4 in 2014. The PostgreSQL project fixed it on 13 August 2026 in releases 18.6, 17.11, 16.15, 15.19 and 14.24; the issue is rated CVSS 7.2. Exploitation requires an account holding the REPLICATION attribute. The replication protocol’s parser accepts almost any character inside a double-quoted output plugin name — slashes, backslashes, dot-dot traversal, even Windows UNC paths — which lets an attacker point it at an arbitrary library and have the server load and execute it as the postgres system user. From there the outcome is code execution on the database host, durable superuser rights and a persistent backdoor. The reason this matters in government estates is who holds that attribute in practice. REPLICATION is routinely granted to backup tooling, standby servers, monitoring agents and data pipelines, and those service accounts tend to have long-lived passwords stored in configuration files, are excluded from rotation policies, and are rarely covered by MFA. That turns a privilege many teams regard as read-only and low-risk into a path to the underlying host. No exploitation had been reported and no public proof-of-concept code had appeared as of 4 September, and the CVE was not in KEV — which is why this sits below the actively-exploited items here.

CVE-2026-6471 CVSS 7.2 • PostgreSQL 9.4 through 18 • Fixed in 18.6, 17.11, 16.15, 15.19, 14.24
No known exploitation — no public PoC and no KEV listing as of 4 Sep 2026 Not in CISA KEV as of this edition
Fixed in PostgreSQL 18.6, 17.11, 16.15, 15.19 and 14.24 (released 13 Aug 2026) Action: Run an inventory query for roles carrying the REPLICATION attribute across every PostgreSQL instance you operate, including managed-service databases and the ones embedded inside appliances and vendor products. Expect the list to be longer than anyone predicts. Patch to the fixed minor release, and in the meantime remove REPLICATION from any account that does not genuinely need it, rotate the credentials of those that do, and restrict replication connections by source address in pg_hba.conf. Check whether logical decoding is actually enabled where it is not required.

Low Policy

A stopgap funding bill pushes the 2015 cyber information-sharing law’s expiry to 11 December

Reporting on 1 September 2026 confirmed that the continuing resolution carries a short extension of the Cybersecurity Information Sharing Act of 2015, moving its expiry from the end of September to 11 December 2026. The law is the statutory basis for the liability protections that allow private organisations to share cyber threat indicators with federal agencies including CISA and NSA without exposing themselves to litigation or regulatory action for doing so. Industry groups have argued that a lapse would reduce voluntary disclosure because legal teams would have to re-evaluate every submission; federal officials have described a lapse as disruptive to real-time threat intelligence sharing across public and private networks. The same bill extends the Technology Modernization Fund and the Federal Cybersecurity Enhancement Act, the latter of which authorises the National Cybersecurity Protection System used for intrusion detection on federal networks. For government IT and security leaders the practical consequence is a planning one rather than an operational one: authorities that underpin threat-sharing arrangements and some modernisation funding are now on a calendar that expires mid-December, so any programme that depends on them should have its assumptions written down and a contingency noted. Agencies that receive indicators from private partners should also confirm whether those partners’ participation is conditioned on the statutory protections, because that is the relationship most affected by a lapse.

Low Policy

BOD 26-04 is the reason federal deadlines in this window were three days, not thirty

The compressed remediation dates attached to this edition’s KEV entries — 5 September for flaws added on 2 September, 29 August for flaws added on 26 August — are not ad-hoc urgency. They are the mechanical output of Binding Operational Directive 26-04, “Prioritizing Security Updates Based on Risk”, issued on 10 June 2026, which replaced fixed 15-day and 30-day windows with timelines derived from four questions: is the asset publicly exposed, is the vulnerability on KEV, can exploitation be automated, and does exploitation grant control of the system. Where the answer to all four is yes, the remediation window is three calendar days and a forensic triage step is required as well. Mid-tier combinations get 14 days, lower-risk internal cases 60 days, and the lowest tier is fixed on the next upgrade. The directive also requires exposure status to be maintained continuously in Continuous Diagnostics and Mitigation dashboards, with a 60-day process deadline and a 180-day deadline for meeting the full timeline table. Non-federal government bodies are not bound by it, but the framework travels well and is worth adopting on its logic rather than its authority: the four questions are a better triage rubric than a CVSS threshold, and they explain to non-technical leadership why an internet-facing appliance on KEV outranks a higher-scored flaw on an internal server.

Directive and process requirement — no vulnerability or exploitation involved Not applicable — the directive governs how KEV entries are prioritised
No patch applicable — action is adopting the prioritisation rubric and evidencing timelines Action: Federal agencies should confirm their vulnerability management policy encodes the four-factor test and that asset exposure status is actually flowing into CDM rather than being maintained in a spreadsheet. State, local and non-US bodies should adopt the same four questions as a triage rubric even without the mandate — publicly exposed, KEV-listed, automatable, total control — and set a three-day track for the cases that meet all four. Either way, test whether your change process can physically deliver a three-day emergency patch on an internet-facing appliance, because the policy is worthless if the approval path takes a week.
