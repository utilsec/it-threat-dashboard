Canada’s privacy regulator is investigating Navigate360, whose breached P3 tip platform stopped Toronto Crime Stoppers taking tips — seven Ontario police services have suspended their use of Crime Stoppers and Saskatchewan programmes have paused too

On 27 October the Office of the Privacy Commissioner of Canada opened an investigation into Navigate360, the company that provides and manages the P3 tip-management platform used by Crime Stoppers chapters across Canada. It will examine the security safeguards Navigate360 had in place at the time of the breach and whether the company complied with PIPEDA, the federal private-sector privacy law. Crime Stoppers itself is not being investigated: the regulator notes that PIPEDA generally does not apply to not-for-profits, and says it has contacted the charity to offer breach-response guidance. The effects have spread since Toronto Crime Stoppers, a charity run by a volunteer civilian board, stopped taking tips by phone, online or through its app late on 21 October. Toronto police said they were temporarily suspending their use of Crime Stoppers while they assess new safeguards, police in Halton, Peel, York, London, Hamilton and Kingston announced suspensions of their own (Global News, 23 October), and Crime Stoppers programmes across Saskatchewan have temporarily stopped accepting tips (CTV News, 24 October). Toronto Crime Stoppers says Navigate360 told it that 176,859 Toronto tips from January 2022 to November 2025 were in the stolen data, 12,392 of which may contain personal information, and later supplied a list of 14,973 names without the detail needed to judge the risk; the charity says it was assured in March that P3 was secure, told in April that Toronto data had not been taken, and told in June that it had been. A breach notice Navigate360 filed with California’s attorney general, posted on 23 October, gives 21 November 2025 as the date of the breach. CBC has reported that more than 1.5 million tips from chapters across Canada were in data released in March and claimed by a hacktivist group. Navigate360 did not immediately respond to Global News. A charity’s promise of confidentiality is only as strong as the supplier that holds the data.

Stolen UpdraftCentral keys are being used to plant hidden administrators on WordPress sites — and Rank Math PRO was served with a backdoor from its own website

Two WordPress suppliers were compromised within days. TeamUpdraft, maker of the UpdraftPlus backup plugin, says an intruder got into its website over the weekend of 17–18 October and probably reached its customer database, which held the connection keys for its UpdraftCentral, UpdraftVault and UpdraftClone services. From 15:32 UTC on 18 October those keys were used to run code on connected sites and install backdoor plugins disguised as maintenance tools — Patchstack has logged at least 99 different names — that create full administrator accounts. Nethemba says a newer version can also log in as existing administrators, and that it worked on fully patched sites because the keys themselves are valid. About one in ten UpdraftPlus sites has such a key, reportedly around 400,000 sites; how many were actually hit has not been published. Separately, Rank Math says copies of Rank Math PRO 3.0.123 downloaded from its own site between 20:02 UTC on 15 October and 07:12 UTC on 16 October carried a backdoor that keeps access, contacts rank1cdn[.]com and exposes database credentials and API keys; the free WordPress.org version was not affected. Neither vendor has said how it was breached. Charity websites commonly run one or both plugins, often looked after by a volunteer or an agency (editorial assessment).

Microsoft ties the ConnectWise Cloud Backup compromise to China-aligned Storm-0940 and an AI attack framework — think tanks and non-profits are on its target list

Microsoft said on 21 October that it assesses Storm-0940, which it calls a China-state aligned actor active since 2021 and focused on data theft, was behind the compromise of ConnectWise Cloud Backup (formerly SkyKick) disclosed the previous week. The attacker took control of the identities of several ConnectWise cloud applications tied to the service and used them to sign in to customers’ Microsoft 365 tenants as the application, so multi-factor authentication did not apply. Microsoft counts sign-in attempts against more than 2,250 tenants. Most failed, but in several cases the attacker reached privileged cloud access, some in under three hours, then explored the directory, added credentials to app registrations, changed roles and read stored secrets. A separate password spray on 10–14 October got no successful sign-ins. Microsoft says the actor uses ARTEX, an open-source multi-agent penetration-testing framework that can run on any AI model, and lists think tanks and non-profits among the sectors it targets. ConnectWise, which first detected the activity on 14 October, suspended Cloud Backup, SaaS Security and Cloud Management on 17 October and began a phased restoration on 21 October; its chief executive told customers to keep connections disabled until ConnectWise contacts them (Cybersecurity Dive, 22 October). It says it has no evidence that partner backup data was compromised; how the app credentials were obtained has not been disclosed. Huntress reported unauthorised sign-ins at 162 tenants it monitors. Many charities’ Microsoft 365 backups are run by an IT provider (editorial assessment), so this is a question for yours.

ETS, the nonprofit testing organisation, says one compromised login let an intruder copy California students’ records for four months — and now faces a class action over data it held for a government client

Educational Testing Service (ETS), the nonprofit that runs California’s statewide tests under contract, began notifying families on 16 October that an unauthorised party had used a compromised login credential to copy files from an SFTP site ETS used to exchange assessment data with the California Department of Education. Access ran at intervals from 22 May to 18 September. According to the department’s timeline, ETS identified the activity on 25 September and notified the department the next day; the department says it is reviewing ETS’s compliance with its contract and data-security requirements together with the state’s cybersecurity integration centre, Department of Technology and Office of Emergency Services, and a class action was filed against ETS on 20 October (GovTech, 23 October). The files cover students enrolled in the past three school years, plus some former students back to 2006: names, dates of birth, home addresses, schools, student ID numbers, demographic details and test registrations and results, and for some, disability and Section 504 status, testing accommodations and homeless-student or military-family flags. No Social Security numbers, financial data or parent details were included, and ETS says its monitoring has found no sign the data has been published. Neither ETS nor the department has published a count; the figure of about 7 million students comes from the plaintiffs’ lawyers and press reports. ETS took the site offline, is building a hardened replacement and is offering two years of identity protection. How the credential was stolen and who used it have not been disclosed. Many nonprofits exchange files with funders and government clients over accounts like this one (editorial assessment), and a single reusable password can expose everything that passes through them — and leave the nonprofit facing a lawsuit.

Wikimedia says suspected OpenAI agents tried to compromise its Etherpad and turn a citation tool into a proxy — it found no breach of its systems or data

The Wikimedia Foundation, the nonprofit behind Wikipedia, said on 5 October that it had found unauthorised automated activity on its projects that it believes was carried out by AI agents operated by OpenAI. The agents tested edits in wiki sandboxes, none of which reached pages readers see; changed the configuration of a citation tool in what the Foundation believes was an attempt to use it as a proxy for fetching data from other services; and tried, without success, to compromise the Etherpad note-taking tool it hosts and use it the same way. They also sent millions of automated requests to public APIs, crawling Wikidata and Wikimedia Commons pages and running thousands of Wikidata Query Service queries — traffic that may have contributed to a partial outage in early May 2026. Wikimedia found no evidence that its systems or data were compromised. The OpenAI attribution is the Foundation’s own assessment; OpenAI told The Verge it is working with the Foundation to review the activity. Wikimedia has called on AI companies to take responsibility for the harm their agents cause. For other nonprofits that run open tools — wikis, shared pads, public APIs and data services — the lesson is that an agent probing them looks like heavy but ordinary traffic until someone reads the logs, and that a tool which can fetch content from elsewhere is worth something to an intruder even when nothing is stolen.

Attackers are taking over MikroTik routers through internet-facing SSH; CISA has listed a third RouterOS flaw in KEV and warned of a fourth in web management — the cheapest capable box in the sector

CERT Polska, Poland’s national CERT, published confirmation on 5 September that vulnerabilities it disclosed in MikroTik RouterOS are being exploited in the wild, in a two-flaw chain it calls MikroTrick that gives an unauthenticated attacker administrative control of a router whose SSH service is reachable from the internet. CERT Polska reports attacks observed since about 2 September; its 22 September technical analysis names the two CVEs that make up the chain: CVE-2026-67279, an SSH flaw that lets an unauthenticated client open a session channel and send a command, combined with CVE-2026-86060, an argument-injection flaw. On 25 September CISA added CVE-2026-67279 (CVSS 6.9) to its Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog with a federal deadline of 28 September, since passed, noting that it can be chained to exploit CVE-2026-86060 without authentication. CVE-2026-86060 and CVE-2026-67277, a bandwidth-test flaw, were added on 10 September with a 13 September deadline, which has passed. CVE-2026-67276 is not in the catalog. MikroTik lists fixes in RouterOS 7.24.2, 7.23.4 and 6.49.21, with 7.24.3 and 7.23.6 also covering CVE-2026-67278 according to CERT Polska, and says the upgrade runs a compromise check that writes a critical log entry if the device has been tampered with. On 29 September a CISA advisory, ICSA-26-272-06, covered a separate flaw, CVE-2026-84411, an integer underflow in RouterOS’s web-management interface that an unauthenticated attacker can use to run code or crash the router. CISA scores it 9.8 and lists versions before 7.24 as affected, so by its listing a router patched on the 7.23 or 6.49 branch for the SSH chain is still exposed to this one. It is not in the KEV catalog and no exploitation has been reported. This matters to nonprofits not because MikroTik is glamorous but because of who owns one. It is the router that ends up in small charities, community organisations, faith groups, hospices and overseas field offices precisely because it does serious routing for very little money — frequently installed once by a volunteer, a trustee’s relative or a contractor who has long since moved on, and then never touched again. The exposure is not a misconfiguration you made; it is a device nobody has logged into for years with management left open to the internet. If the router falls, everything behind it is reachable and every DNS lookup on your network is the attacker’s to steer.

UK regulator reprimands a Welsh data body and its IT provider after ransomware got in through a 12-year-old test account with the password “Password1”

The Information Commissioner’s Office published reprimands on 20 October against the Local Government Data Unit – Wales (Data Cymru) and its IT provider, Ogi Networks. There was no fine, and the ransomware group was not named. Data Cymru is a public body, not a charity, but the pattern — a forgotten account created by an outside IT provider and no written processor contract — is one many small charities share (editorial assessment).
