High Breach

Roughly 5,000 Dropbox accounts were taken over without a password — and Dropbox is where a great many firms still exchange files with clients

Dropbox has said that around 5,000 accounts were accessed without authorisation between 4 and 21 August 2026, with files downloaded from approximately 1,500 of them. The route in was a federated-identity failure rather than a password breach. Lenovo’s identity service allowed a Lenovo ID to be registered against an email address the registrant did not control, and Dropbox then honoured a “Continue with Lenovo” sign-in by resolving that email claim to an existing Dropbox account and issuing a session — no password, no step-up challenge, no consent step linking the two identities. Anyone who could register a Lenovo ID on a target’s email address could walk into that person’s Dropbox. Dropbox has since fixed the flaw and expired every session established through a Lenovo ID, and reporting indicates that accounts protected by multi-factor authentication fared best. Two things make this a professional-services item rather than a consumer one. First, the exposure is exactly the pattern that catches firms: Dropbox is one of the most common ways smaller and mid-sized practices move documents to and from clients, opposing counsel and experts, often on individual accounts outside any firm tenant — the kind of shadow file-sharing IT usually discovers during an incident. Second, the failure mode generalises. Any “sign in with” button on a document platform is a trust decision about somebody else’s identity provider, and this incident shows what happens when the upstream provider verifies email weakly and the downstream service treats the resulting claim as proof. The right question for your own estate is not whether Dropbox is patched — it is which social or vendor logins are enabled on the services holding client material, and whether any of them can bind to an existing account without a password.

High Campaign

Still the sector’s defining campaign: Silent Ransom Group works law firms by phone, and sometimes by walking through the front door

Carried forward and re-checked for this edition; nothing newer has been published, and in a week with no new law-firm incident disclosed this remains the most likely way a firm actually gets hit. The group tracked as Silent Ransom Group, Luna Moth, Chatty Spider and UNC3753 has been active since 2022 and targeting law firms since spring 2023. An FBI FLASH alert issued in May 2026 and Google Mandiant’s June 2026 reporting on a targeted campaign against US law firms both describe the operation as still running; no update to either has appeared since. The tradecraft is almost entirely human. Operators call or email posing as IT support, talk a partner, paralegal or billing clerk into a remote-support session, and install a legitimate remote monitoring and management tool. Exfiltration runs over WinSCP or a hidden or renamed copy of Rclone, with data pushed to Google Drive, Microsoft OneDrive, external hard drives or USB storage. There is frequently no encryption stage at all — the leverage is the client material itself, which in a firm means privileged files that cannot be restored away. The outlier detail, and the one that breaks most legal threat models, is that operators have turned up in person, claiming they need to image a device or take a backup to deal with the effects of a phishing email. Halcyon’s research centre ranked law firms the fourth most-targeted industry in early 2026, attributing it to the sensitivity of client data, regulatory pressure to resolve incidents quickly and a perceived willingness to pay. Read this alongside the unpatched ScreenConnect issue further down the page: the tool this campaign relies on being opened for it is the same class of tool now carrying an open advisory. The FBI’s own recommendations are unglamorous and correct: verify visitor identity against ID, deploy phishing-resistant multi-factor authentication, restrict remote access and external-drive permissions on sensitive systems, and train staff to recognise the pretext.

High Research

Unit 42 investigated an intrusion driven by AI agents that finished in under ten hours — with no zero-days, which is the part firms should read twice

Palo Alto Networks’ Unit 42 published an investigation on 2 September 2026 into an extortion intrusion in which the attacker used frontier AI models and autonomous agents to run the operation. The compromise ran end to end in under ten hours, against a manual tradecraft baseline Unit 42 puts at roughly two weeks. Multiple specialised agents worked in parallel against different defensive layers, handling network mapping, credential harvesting, privilege escalation and exploitation of the victim’s CI/CD pipeline while monitoring results, evaluating them and re-planning in real time. More than 50 MITRE ATT&CK techniques were exercised. Initial access came through a publicly reachable web service used to tunnel into the network. No novel zero-day was required — the operation ran on known vulnerabilities and misconfigurations, executed faster than a human team could. That last point is what makes this worth a legal-sector reader’s attention. A great deal of law-firm security planning quietly assumes a response window measured in days: someone notices the odd login on Monday, the managed detection provider calls on Tuesday, the incident-response retainer is activated on Wednesday. An intrusion that completes inside a working day does not leave room for that sequence, and it does not need a sophisticated flaw to get started — the internet-facing remote-access appliance you have not patched, or the forgotten extranet, is sufficient. Unit 42’s defensive recommendations are correspondingly about speed and automation rather than about detection cleverness: pre-built playbooks that revoke credentials and freeze pipelines without waiting for a human decision, an inventory of AI endpoints, API keys and model gateways with rate limiting, hunting for machine-speed behavioural patterns such as bursty API activity, and mandatory multi-party review with immutable branch protection on infrastructure repositories. For a firm without a CI/CD pipeline, the transferable half is the first: decide now, in writing, what your service desk is allowed to disable at 2am without calling a partner.

High Campaign

More than 5,400 hacked WordPress and PrestaShop sites are serving fake-CAPTCHA lures whose payloads live in a blockchain smart contract

Netskope published analysis on 5 September 2026 of a large ClickFix operation running from over 5,400 compromised websites, mostly WordPress and PrestaShop installations. The delivery mechanism is the notable part. Rather than hosting the malicious script on infrastructure that can be seized, the operators use a technique known as EtherHiding: the payload is stored in a smart contract on the BNB Smart Chain testnet, and an injected script on the hacked site fetches it at page load. There is no server to take down and no domain to sinkhole. Netskope observed roughly 300 to 400 infected sites contacting BNB Smart Chain endpoints daily through August, peaking at 536 in a single day. The lure itself is the now-familiar fake CAPTCHA: the visitor is told to prove they are human by pasting a command into the Windows Run box, which executes PowerShell under their own account. A later variant drops a WebRTC data-channel stager that establishes an encrypted covert channel to the operators’ command-and-control. Two things make this a legal-sector item rather than general noise. The first is that fee earners spend their day on unfamiliar third-party websites — court sites, county recorders, expert directories, opposing-party sites, foreign registries — and a compromised WordPress site is indistinguishable from a working one. The second is that many firms run their own marketing site on WordPress, so the same weakness that makes a site a delivery vehicle for someone else applies to yours. ClickFix defeats email security entirely because nothing arrives by email; the user is talked into running the code themselves.

Medium Research

Microsoft documents a phishing operation hiding invisible Unicode inside financial words to slip past keyword filters — a direct problem for billing and finance inboxes

Microsoft Security Research published analysis on 4 September 2026 of a high-volume phishing operation using ASCII smuggling: invisible Unicode Tag characters in the U+E0000 to U+E007F range inserted inside otherwise ordinary words. A message about “funding” is written with a hidden code point between the letters, so it renders normally to a human reader but no longer matches a filter looking for the literal string. Fortra researchers described the underlying technique in September 2025; what Microsoft adds is the scale. Between February and May 2026 the campaign sent between roughly one and 2.37 million messages on weekday peaks, dropping to near silence at weekends — a business-hours rhythm chosen to blend into normal mail flow. The lures were finance-themed, imitating business loans, credit lines and funding advances, with sender domains including guardiangrowthfunding[.]com, digitalcapitalboost[.]com and thebusinessloanexpress[.]com, and a substantial part of the targeting aimed at Small Business Administration loan applicants. The professional-services relevance is not exotic. Finance-themed lures land in the accounts, billing and practice-management inboxes that already process payment instructions daily, and those are the same inboxes involved in most invoice-fraud and completion-monies incidents in this sector. If your mail security depends meaningfully on keyword or phrase matching — and many DLP and business-email-compromise rules do — this technique defeats it silently and produces no alert to tell you so. The fix is normalisation: strip or flag invisible Unicode before rules run, and treat unusual density of these characters as suspicious in itself.

Critical Advisory

PaperCut attackers are now harvesting credentials and creating their own admin accounts — and print management is one of the few servers almost every firm runs

PaperCut warned on 27 August 2026 that two flaws in its NG and MF print-management products were being exploited, and CISA added both to the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog on 31 August with a federal remediation deadline of 14 September. CVE-2026-82078 and CVE-2026-81578 chain an authentication bypass into remote code execution for an unauthenticated attacker. The escalation this window is in what the intruders do once they are in. Arctic Wolf’s adversary research team published detail on 5 September describing hands-on activity against vulnerable PaperCut servers: discovery commands such as uname, whoami, ver and tasklist; creation of privileged accounts, including one named “Administrator17”; credential harvesting with tooling identified as lsa_collect.exe and save_hives.exe; deployment of Meterpreter Java payloads; and grepping PaperCut configuration files for the strings “password”, “secret”, “ldap” and “token”. The victims Arctic Wolf names are K-12 schools and universities in the US and Europe rather than law firms, but the target selection is opportunistic — internet-exposed PaperCut — not sectoral, and ShadowServer has put more than a thousand instances directly on the internet. PaperCut shipped emergency patches, then a second patch after the first was bypassed, with a consolidated release still pending, so patching once is not evidence of safety. For law and accounting firms the exposure is unusually direct. Print management holds the cost-recovery data binding documents to matters and clients, it sits on the internal network with service accounts that touch directories and file shares, and secure print release means the server has seen a copy of what people printed. That last point is now the operative one: the observed behaviour is specifically credential theft and LDAP-secret hunting, so an exposed PaperCut server is a directory-credential problem, not just a print outage.

Critical Advisory

Two separate NetScaler flaws are now being exploited, and NetScaler is the remote-access front door for a large share of firms

The Citrix NetScaler situation changed twice inside this window. CVE-2026-8452, a memory-overflow issue that Citrix patched on 30 June and described in terms of unpredictable behaviour and denial of service, turned out to be worse than advertised: watchTowr published proof-of-concept detail on 14 August showing unauthenticated remote code execution, in-the-wild exploitation followed shortly afterwards with web shells and discovery commands observed by Previdian and Defused, and CISA added it to KEV on 26 August with a remediation deadline of 29 August. Vulnerable builds reported include 14.1-72.61 FIPS, 13.1-63.18 and 13.1-37.272, and only appliances configured as AAA virtual servers or Gateway VPN servers are affected. Then on 3 September, following public release of proof-of-concept code, sensors picked up requests matching the PoC for CVE-2026-19490 — the critical authentication bypass Citrix disclosed in mid-August — from three distinct source addresses geolocated to Australia, the United States and Germany, with public reporting on 4 September. That one affects NetScaler ADC and Gateway deployed as AAA virtual servers or Gateways, including SSL VPN, ICA Proxy, CVPN and RDP Proxy configurations, with impact depending on firmware version and whether a SAML Action is configured; fixed builds are listed in Citrix bulletin CTX696939. For a firm, an authentication bypass on the remote-access appliance hands an attacker the same interactive session that Silent Ransom Group spends weeks on the phone trying to talk someone into granting. This is the patch to do first if you have not.

The G7 tells every sector to start the post-quantum migration now — and no sector holds secrets that must stay secret for longer than this one

On 3 September 2026 the national cybersecurity agencies of the G7 states, with the French agency ANSSI chairing the G7 Cybersecurity Working Group and alongside the European Commission and ENISA, issued a joint call to accelerate the transition to post-quantum cryptography. The framing is the notable change: the group explicitly reframes the quantum threat from a distant future problem to a near-term one demanding action across all sectors, not only critical infrastructure. Two dates give it teeth. ANSSI has said it will stop vetting products that lack quantum-safe encryption from 2027, and post-quantum security becomes mandatory for procurement of certain security products by 2030. The statement sets out five priority areas — awareness, national transition strategies and supply chains, research and development, public-private partnership, and building post-quantum cryptography into cybersecurity requirements — and advises organisations to build a cryptographic asset inventory, identify systems holding critical data, map dependencies and take up integrated post-quantum products during normal renewal cycles rather than as a separate programme. This is a policy signal rather than a rule that binds a law firm today, and it is easy to file under “not yet”. The reason not to is specific to legal and professional services: the harvest-now-decrypt-later problem bites hardest where confidentiality obligations run longest. Privilege does not expire. Sealed material stays sealed. Deal documents, trust structures, patent prosecution files, arbitration records and personal data in long-running matters all carry confidentiality duties measured in decades, which is the same horizon the G7 is now telling everyone to plan against. The practical first step costs almost nothing and is useful regardless: know what cryptography you are actually using and where.
