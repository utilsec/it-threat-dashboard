Top Story

Attackers are taking over MikroTik routers through internet-facing SSH with no password at all — and MikroTik is the cheapest capable box in the sector

On 5 September CERT Polska, Poland’s national CERT, published confirmation that vulnerabilities it disclosed in MikroTik RouterOS are being exploited in the wild, chained into an attack it calls MikroTrick. The chain gives an unauthenticated attacker full administrative control of a router whose SSH service is reachable from the internet. CVE-2026-67276 (CVSS 9.2) is the entry point: RouterOS did not properly verify the public key presented during SSH authentication, so someone who knows a valid username and the corresponding public modulus can log in without ever holding the private key. CVE-2026-86060 (CVSS 9.2) then takes that session to full admin, through improper handling of disallowed characters in the username. A third issue, CVE-2026-67277 (CVSS 8.8), lets the bandwidth-test service disclose uninitialised kernel memory or be restarted remotely. CERT Polska reports attacks observed since at least 2 September, from source addresses 82.192.72.4 and 103.102.31.18. Fixed builds are 6.49.21, 7.23.4, 7.24.2 and 7.25beta3. This is the top story for nonprofits not because MikroTik is glamorous but because of who owns one. It is the router that ends up in small charities, community organisations, faith groups, hospices and overseas field offices precisely because it does serious routing for very little money — frequently installed once by a volunteer, a founding trustee’s nephew or a contractor who has long since moved on, and then never touched again. The exposure here is not a misconfiguration you made; it is a device nobody has logged into for four years with management left open to the internet. If the router is compromised, everything behind it is reachable and every DNS lookup on your network is the attacker’s to steer.

Critical Incident

An unpatched Magento zero-day is backdooring online stores right now, and Adobe has published nothing

Sansec, the Dutch e-commerce forensics firm, disclosed on 5 September that a previously unknown flaw it names StyleSmuggler is being used to plant backdoors in Magento Open Source and Adobe Commerce stores. It gives an unauthenticated attacker remote code execution with no user interaction. The exploit runs in two stages: PHP code is smuggled in through Magento’s failure-report mechanism, then executed by triggering the built-in “Payment Transaction Failed Reminder” email job. The implant that follows disguises itself as a Linux kernel thread, re-establishes itself through cron, and reads session data out of Redis. Sansec says every current version is affected, with compromises confirmed on 2.4.6-p15, 2.4.7, 2.4.8 and 2.4.9. Exploitation began on 4 September; both Sansec and the Disrex Group have confirmed live victims, and Disrex logged 26 distinct attacker source addresses across two incidents. Be clear about the state of play: as of this edition there is no CVE identifier, no patch and no Adobe advisory. Adobe’s next scheduled release is 8 September. Nonprofits reach Magento by a predictable route — a charity shop moved online, merchandise, Christmas cards, event ticketing, membership fulfilment — usually built years ago by an agency that is no longer on retainer, which means nobody is watching for an advisory that has not been written yet.

Critical Incident

More than 440,000 exploit attempts are hitting Elementor Pro and Super Forms — the exact WordPress stack most charity donation pages run on

Two file-upload flaws in widely-installed WordPress form components are now under mass attack, and this is the item on this page most likely to apply to your organisation directly. Wordfence has blocked roughly 190,000 attempts against Elementor Pro CVE-2026-32475 and about 250,000 against Super Forms CVE-2026-14894 (CVSS 9.8). Elementor Pro is the more consequential of the pair: more than six million active installations, faulty validation of file-upload arrays, and an attack that works by submitting an empty file as the first array element followed by PHP code as the second. Payloads land under /wp-content/uploads/elementor/forms/ with random filenames and execute, giving an unauthenticated attacker a web shell, the ability to create administrator accounts, and full control of the site. Version 4.2.2 shipped on 19 August and exploitation began the same day — there was no grace period. Super Forms attacks began earlier, on 14 July, peaking above 40,000 requests on 18 August; the fix is 6.3.314. Note the change from the previous edition of this page, which recorded Elementor Pro as not known to be exploited and requiring an unusual configuration. That is no longer accurate: exploitation is confirmed, and a published form with a file-upload field is a common setup — it is how a great many nonprofits collect grant applications, volunteer forms, safeguarding referrals and event registrations. If your donation or campaign site runs Elementor Pro, treat this as tonight’s work rather than this month’s.

Critical Incident

A critical Citrix NetScaler authentication bypass has moved from proof-of-concept to live exploitation

CVE-2026-19490 is a critical authentication bypass affecting NetScaler ADC and Gateway appliances configured as AAA virtual servers or Gateways — that is, the SSL VPN, ICA Proxy, CVPN and RDP Proxy roles. Citrix disclosed it on 19 August in bulletin CTX696939 without evidence of exploitation. That has changed. A proof-of-concept was published, and on 3 September researcher Ryan Dewhurst reported requests matching it arriving from three distinct source addresses geolocated to Australia, the United States and Germany; Belgium’s national cybersecurity centre separately warned of exploitation attempts. Shadowserver counts over 22,000 exposed NetScaler ADC appliances and close to 1,700 Gateway instances reachable from the internet, patch status unknown. Whether an attacker can bypass authentication depends on firmware version and SAML Action configuration, so a specific appliance may or may not be reachable this way — but that is not a determination to make from a news article. Most small nonprofits will not own a NetScaler. The ones that do are typically larger federated charities, hospices, housing associations and international NGOs that inherited Citrix for remote desktop access, and in those estates the appliance is usually the single front door to everything else. If a managed service provider runs your remote access, ask them today in writing whether NetScaler is in your stack and what build it is on.

High Incident

More than 5,400 hacked WordPress and PrestaShop sites are serving fake CAPTCHA lures, with the payload parked on a blockchain

Netskope reported on 5 September that it has identified over 5,400 compromised websites — predominantly WordPress, with PrestaShop also represented — injected with code that pulls its next stage from a smart contract on the BNB Smart Chain testnet. The technique, known as EtherHiding, matters because there is no malicious server to seize: the payload lives on a public blockchain that cannot be taken down or blocklisted in the usual way, and the attackers can swap it whenever they like. What visitors see is ClickFix: a fake CAPTCHA or “verify you are human” box that instructs them to paste a command into the Windows Run dialog or a terminal. Netskope observed the campaign shifting from that PowerShell stage to a WebRTC data-channel loader that opens an encrypted channel back to the attacker and runs whatever it receives. Traffic grew steadily through spring 2026, with roughly 400 infected sites contacting blockchain endpoints daily in August and a peak of 536. How the sites are being compromised in the first place is still unknown. There are two sides to this for a nonprofit. Your own site could be one of the 5,400, quietly attacking your supporters; and your staff and volunteers are exactly the audience that a “paste this to continue” box catches, because it does not look like a download and it does not look like a phish.

High Research

Commodity malware now turns Microsoft Defender and Windows Update off on the way in — which matters most where Defender is the only control you have

Elastic Security Labs published research on 2 September documenting four modules distributed alongside REVSTEALER, an infostealer sold commercially since at least February 2026. Elastic matched roughly 4,700 samples on VirusTotal over the past year. The module worth your attention is LockAppHost: before running a cryptocurrency miner, it adds Microsoft Defender exclusions for common folders and file types, disables five Windows Update services, deletes eleven scheduled update tasks and two malware-removal tasks, and hides the miner inside legitimate Windows processes. It obtains administrator rights by abusing the built-in Windows CMSTP utility. The other three modules steal wallet files, browser extensions and typed passwords (ProManager), hijack the clipboard to swap cryptocurrency addresses and collect recovery phrases (WinUpdate), and turn the machine into a reverse proxy for the attacker’s traffic (SoftManager). Delivery is through game-cheat lures promoted by at least seventeen YouTube channels, many of them hijacked from their original owners, and through fake software downloads — including a bogus free desktop app impersonating a well-known AI assistant. Two things make this a nonprofit item rather than a gamer one. Most nonprofits run Microsoft Defender because it comes with the Microsoft nonprofit grant and there is no budget for anything else, so malware whose first act is to switch it off removes the entire control. And shared, loaned and hand-me-down laptops used by volunteers, placements and family members are precisely where a game-cheat download lands.

A phishing operation across 46 countries tricks staff into installing legitimate remote-management software

ANY.RUN has tied 601 separate cases to a single campaign that persuades recipients to install genuine remote monitoring and management software, giving the attacker hands-on-keyboard access without any malware to detect. Forty-five percent of observed activity targeted United States entities, with 46 countries involved overall. The lures are ordinary administrative documents: Canada Revenue Agency tax forms, shipping and UPS notices, Adobe PDFs, US Social Security Administration themes and invoices. Delivery infrastructure runs on services that pass every reputation check — Vercel, GitHub Pages, Netlify and compromised legitimate sites, with payloads staged on Amazon S3, Cloudflare R2 and GitHub — and 94 percent of the observed domains lived for a single day. Named target industries are education, technology and government, with banking, finance and manufacturing also prominent; no nonprofit is named. It belongs here anyway for a specific reason. RMM tools are exactly what a small nonprofit’s outsourced IT support asks it to install, so “a support person emailed me a remote-access installer” is a normal Tuesday rather than a red flag, and the resulting session looks identical to real support traffic. The countermeasure is not a product. It is a rule everyone knows: your organisation uses one named remote-support tool, nobody installs another one, and any request to do so gets verified by voice on a known number first.

Medium Research

Invisible Unicode characters split financial keywords apart, letting millions of phishing emails walk past keyword filters

Microsoft security researchers have documented a high-volume campaign using what they call ASCII smuggling: invisible Unicode Tag characters in the range U+E0000 to U+E007F inserted inside sensitive words so that “funding” is stored as “fun” plus an invisible character plus “ding”. The recipient sees an ordinary word. A filter matching on keywords sees two fragments that match nothing. Weekday volumes ran between one and 2.37 million messages, peaking on 26 February 2026, sustained across roughly February to May before dropping sharply after 15 May. The lures impersonated business loan, line-of-credit and advance-funding offers aimed at Small Business Administration loan applicants, and the goal was to harvest detailed business and financial information for follow-up spear-phishing. The campaign abused a legitimate marketing platform, which is what makes it hard: mail arriving from established bulk-sending infrastructure carries good sender reputation, so reputation-based filtering does not help either. Nonprofits should read this as a warning about their own filter assumptions. If your defence against grant-fraud and donation-fraud lures rests on a rule that flags words like “grant”, “funding”, “bank details” or “wire”, that rule can be defeated by characters your mail client will not render. The controls that survive this are procedural rather than lexical: bank-detail changes verified out of band on a previously known number, and a second pair of eyes on any payment above a threshold you set.

High Advisory

Google patches a sixth actively-exploited Chrome zero-day of 2026 — a V8 type-confusion bug reachable from a web page

CVE-2026-85046 is a high-severity type-confusion flaw in V8, the JavaScript and WebAssembly engine at the heart of Chrome, and Google states that an exploit for it exists in the wild. Type confusion in V8 is the classic browser remote-code-execution primitive: a crafted script inside an ordinary HTML page can corrupt memory and run code, with no download and nothing for a user to approve. Google is withholding technical detail to give people time to update, and credits Salvatore Gulizia for the report. This is the sixth Chrome bug patched this year that was already being exploited. Fixed builds are 152.0.7977.82 or .83 on Windows and macOS and 152.0.7977.82 on Linux; the same release closes nine further high-severity issues. Because Chromium sits underneath Edge, Brave, Opera and Vivaldi, the same fix has to travel through each of those vendors separately, which usually takes a few days. For a nonprofit this is the cheapest high-value action of the week and it is almost entirely about closing browsers: Chrome downloads updates automatically but does not apply them until the browser restarts, and staff who leave a browser open for weeks are simply not patched. Send the message, then verify a sample rather than trusting it happened.

A SQL injection in the All-in-One WP Migration backup plugin fires when an administrator restores a backup — and 3.25 million sites are still unpatched

CVE-2026-19949 is a high-severity second-order SQL injection in All-in-One WP Migration and Backup, a plugin with more than five million active WordPress installations. The mechanism deserves attention because it inverts the usual timing. An unauthenticated attacker injects SQL through WordPress trackbacks, and nothing happens; the payload sits inert in the database until an administrator restores from a backup, at which point the archive’s database content is parsed and the injection executes, potentially leading to remote code execution and full site takeover. In other words the trigger is the recovery operation — the thing you do on your worst day, when you are already dealing with an outage or an incident and are least likely to be suspicious. Researcher Jack Taylor found the flaw in mid-August and reported it through Wordfence to developer ServMask on 15 August; version 7.110 shipped on 20 August, and the details were published on 2 September. Roughly 3.25 million installations remain vulnerable, with only about 35 percent of users updated. This is a very common plugin in the nonprofit web estate, because it is the standard free answer to “how do we back up the website” on a shoestring — and a poisoned backup is worse than no backup at all.

A €500,000 GDPR fine names the three failures a resource-constrained organisation is most likely to have

France’s data-protection regulator CNIL has fined Hôpital privé de la Loire, a Saint-Étienne hospital operated by Ramsay Santé, €500,000 (about $580,000) over a summer 2025 breach that exposed data on 524,867 patients and 202,246 trusted third parties — roughly 727,000 people. The intrusion started from a compromised doctor’s account. CNIL cited breaches of GDPR Articles 32 and 34, and the specific findings are the part worth reading if you run a charity rather than a hospital: external clinicians reached internal systems without a VPN or multi-factor authentication; access controls were loose enough to allow broad retrieval of patient records; monitoring was insufficient to notice the attacker exploring the system and extracting a large volume of data over several days; and the hospital notified patients but not the 202,246 third parties whose data was also taken. Strip out the clinical setting and that is a description of an ordinary small nonprofit: contractors and sessional workers signing in from anywhere, a case-management system where any logged-in account can see everything, no one watching the logs, and a notification list that covers the obvious group but not the people whose details appear inside other people’s records. Regulators are increasingly willing to price that. The exposure here is not hypothetical for charities holding safeguarding, immigration or health data on people who have no ability to protect themselves.

Carried forward: the Charity Commission and the ICO have published joint guidance for charities caught in the Beacon incident — and it doubles as a generic incident playbook

The Charity Commission for England and Wales, working with the Information Commissioner’s Office, published guidance on 7 August for charities affected by the Beacon CRM security incident. It stays on this page because the Beacon workstream is still live for many organisations and because nothing in the current window replaces it. The guidance asks trustees to do four things: report the matter as a serious incident where it results in, or risks, significant harm, loss or damage to the charity, its beneficiaries, assets, services or reputation; read the Commission’s existing guidance on dealing with cyber crime alongside the ICO’s guidance for organisations; work out their own obligations to notify the ICO and the individuals whose data sat in Beacon, rather than assuming the supplier has done it; and communicate clearly with stakeholders, which the Commission frames as central to retaining trust rather than as a public-relations afterthought. The Commission also acknowledged that the volume of incident reports has slowed its processing and said it will keep its regulatory engagement proportionate — a useful thing for an anxious trustee board to know. Read past the Beacon specifics and this is a compact template for any supplier breach: decide whether it is a reportable serious incident, separate the supplier’s duty to notify from your own, and say something honest to your supporters early. Worth putting in front of your board even if you have never used Beacon.
