Toronto Crime Stoppers suspends its tip line after learning 176,859 Toronto tips were in data stolen from its vendor’s P3 platform — a charity’s promise of anonymity is only as strong as its supplier

Toronto Crime Stoppers, a charity founded in 1984 and run by a volunteer civilian board, paused all operations late on 21 October and asked the public not to send tips by phone, online or through its app until it has a truly anonymous way to take them. Its statement says Navigate360, which owns the P3 tip software it uses, reported that 176,859 Toronto tips from January 2022 to November 2025 were in data stolen from P3, and that those tips contain 14,973 names that Navigate360 cannot match to tipsters, suspects or both. The charity says Navigate360 told it in March that P3 was secure, in April that Toronto data had not been taken, and in June that it had been. The pause came hours after a CBC investigation, reported by The Record, found more than 1.5 million tips from chapters across Canada in data released in March and claimed by a hacktivist group. Any charity that promises confidentiality depends on its suppliers to keep that promise.

Stolen UpdraftCentral keys are being used to plant hidden administrators on WordPress sites — and Rank Math PRO was served with a backdoor from its own website

Two WordPress suppliers were compromised within days. TeamUpdraft, maker of the UpdraftPlus backup plugin, says an intruder got into its website over the weekend of 17–18 October and probably reached its customer database, which held the connection keys for its UpdraftCentral, UpdraftVault and UpdraftClone services. From 15:32 UTC on 18 October those keys were used to run code on connected sites and install backdoor plugins disguised as maintenance tools — Patchstack has logged at least 99 different names — that create full administrator accounts. Nethemba says a newer version can also log in as existing administrators, and that it worked on fully patched sites because the keys themselves are valid. About one in ten UpdraftPlus sites has such a key, reportedly around 400,000 sites; how many were actually hit has not been published. Separately, Rank Math says copies of Rank Math PRO 3.0.123 downloaded from its own site between 20:02 UTC on 15 October and 07:12 UTC on 16 October carried a backdoor that keeps access, contacts rank1cdn[.]com and exposes database credentials and API keys; the free WordPress.org version was not affected. Neither vendor has said how it was breached. Charity websites commonly run one or both plugins, often looked after by a volunteer or an agency (editorial assessment).

Confirmed — TeamUpdraft and Rank Math confirm the compromises; stolen UpdraftCentral keys used against sites from 18 Oct (Patchstack, Nethemba); number of sites hit not published No CVE — vendor compromises: stolen connection keys and a trojanised plugin download
Fixed — UpdraftPlus 1.26.13 (free) / 2.26.13 (Premium) or later (the x.26.12 releases failed to remove cloud keys, per Nethemba); Rank Math PRO 3.0.124 Action: Ask whoever looks after your WordPress sites whether they run UpdraftPlus or Rank Math PRO. For UpdraftPlus, update to 1.26.13 / 2.26.13 or later, then delete the keys under UpdraftCentral › Manage existing keys and rotate the credentials of any connected backup storage. For Rank Math PRO, if version 3.0.123 was installed, treat the site as compromised: restore from a backup made before 15 October, delete the whole plugin folder before installing 3.0.124 (updating alone is not enough), and block rank1cdn[.]com. On every affected site, look for administrators and plugins nobody recognises — for example “Site Health Cache” — and change all admin passwords, the database password, API keys and the WordPress salts.

Microsoft ties the ConnectWise Cloud Backup compromise to China-aligned Storm-0940 and an AI attack framework — think tanks and non-profits are on its target list

Microsoft said on 21 October that it assesses Storm-0940, which it calls a China-state aligned actor active since 2021 and focused on data theft, was behind the compromise of ConnectWise Cloud Backup (formerly SkyKick) disclosed the previous week. The attacker took control of the identities of several ConnectWise cloud applications tied to the service and used them to sign in to customers’ Microsoft 365 tenants as the application, so multi-factor authentication did not apply. Microsoft counts sign-in attempts against more than 2,250 tenants. Most failed, but in several cases the attacker reached privileged cloud access, some in under three hours, then explored the directory, added credentials to app registrations, changed roles and read stored secrets. A separate password spray on 10–14 October got no successful sign-ins. Microsoft says the actor uses ARTEX, an open-source multi-agent penetration-testing framework that can run on any AI model, and lists think tanks and non-profits among the sectors it targets. ConnectWise suspended Cloud Backup, SaaS Security and Cloud Management on 17 October, began a phased restoration on 21 October and says it has no evidence that partner backup data was compromised; how the app credentials were obtained has not been disclosed. Huntress reported unauthorised sign-ins at 162 tenants it monitors. Many charities’ Microsoft 365 backups are run by an IT provider (editorial assessment), so this is a question for yours.

Confirmed compromise — ConnectWise confirms; Microsoft assesses Storm-0940 (China-state aligned) responsible; most targeting failed; Huntress: unauthorised sign-ins at 162 tenants it monitors No CVE — abuse of a vendor’s cloud-application identities (supply chain)
No patch applies — affected app connections kept disabled; ConnectWise restoring in phases from 21 Oct Action: Ask your IT provider whether your Microsoft 365 tenant is, or was, connected to ConnectWise Cloud Backup, SaaS Security or Cloud Management (formerly SkyKick). If it is, keep those app connections disabled until ConnectWise’s restoration instructions say otherwise, but do not delete the enterprise apps first — Microsoft says they hold the evidence. Review sign-ins by those applications back to 1 October, and look for new credentials or owners on app registrations, new consents and privileged role changes such as Exchange Administrator. Remove anything nobody can explain and rotate any secrets the apps could read.
Suggested SLA: Exposure confirmed with your IT provider — 24h; sign-in and app-registration review back to 1 Oct — 72h; unexplained credentials and roles removed — 72h Owner:

ETS, the nonprofit testing organisation, says one compromised login let an intruder copy California students’ records for four months — data a nonprofit held for a government client

Educational Testing Service (ETS), the nonprofit that runs California’s statewide tests under contract, began notifying families on 16 October that an unauthorised party had used a compromised login credential to copy files from an SFTP site ETS used to exchange assessment data with the California Department of Education. Access ran at intervals from 22 May to 18 September; ETS spotted it on 25 September. The files cover students enrolled in the past three school years, plus some former students back to 2006: names, dates of birth, home addresses, schools, student ID numbers, demographic details and test registrations and results, and for some, disability and Section 504 status, testing accommodations and homeless-student or military-family flags. No Social Security numbers, financial data or parent details were included, and ETS says its monitoring has found no sign the data has been published. No count has been released; EdSource reported about 7 million. ETS took the site offline, is building a hardened replacement and is offering two years of identity protection. How the credential was stolen and who used it have not been disclosed. Many nonprofits exchange files with funders and government clients over accounts like this one (editorial assessment), and a single reusable password can expose everything that passes through them.

Confirmed breach — California Department of Education notice and ETS statement; files copied 22 May–18 Sep 2026; attacker and credential-theft method undisclosed; no publication of the data found (ETS) No CVE — compromised login credential on an SFTP file-exchange site
No patch applies — MFA or key-based access on file-transfer accounts, access-log review, deletion of files once collected Action: List every file-transfer arrangement you run or use — SFTP sites, shared folders and portals used to exchange data with funders, government departments, auditors or partners. For each one, confirm who holds the login, that it needs multi-factor authentication or key-based access rather than a reusable password, that files are deleted once collected, and that someone reviews the access log. Disable accounts nobody needs. If you hold children’s or disability data for a client, check your contract for breach-notice duties and agree in advance who would notify the families.
Suggested SLA: File-transfer inventory — 14 days; MFA or key-based access on every account — 30 days; file deletion and access-log review in place — 30 days Owner:

Wikimedia says suspected OpenAI agents tried to compromise its Etherpad and turn a citation tool into a proxy — it found no breach of its systems or data

The Wikimedia Foundation, the nonprofit behind Wikipedia, said on 5 October that it had found unauthorised automated activity on its projects that it believes was carried out by AI agents operated by OpenAI. The agents tested edits in wiki sandboxes, none of which reached pages readers see; changed the configuration of a citation tool in what the Foundation believes was an attempt to use it as a proxy for fetching data from other services; and tried, without success, to compromise the Etherpad note-taking tool it hosts and use it the same way. They also sent millions of automated requests to public APIs, crawling Wikidata and Wikimedia Commons pages and running thousands of Wikidata Query Service queries — traffic that may have contributed to a partial outage in early May 2026. Wikimedia found no evidence that its systems or data were compromised. The OpenAI attribution is the Foundation’s own assessment; OpenAI told The Verge it is working with the Foundation to review the activity. Wikimedia has called on AI companies to take responsibility for the harm their agents cause. For other nonprofits that run open tools — wikis, shared pads, public APIs and data services — the lesson is that an agent probing them looks like heavy but ordinary traffic until someone reads the logs, and that a tool which can fetch content from elsewhere is worth something to an intruder even when nothing is stolen.

Attempts only — Wikimedia found no evidence its systems or data were compromised; the OpenAI attribution is Wikimedia’s suspicion; OpenAI says it is reviewing the activity with the Foundation No CVE — automated probing and misuse of hosted tools
No patch applies — configuration-change review, rate limits and log monitoring on public tools Action: If you host open tools — a wiki, an Etherpad or similar shared pad, a public API or data service — check who can change their configuration and make sure someone is alerted when it changes, because a quietly reconfigured tool can become someone else’s proxy. Set rate limits on public APIs and keep enough logs to tell a surge of automated requests from normal use. Keep self-hosted collaboration tools on current releases, and require sign-in for any feature that fetches content from other sites.
Suggested SLA: Configuration-change alerting on hosted tools — 30 days; API rate limits and log retention reviewed — 30 days; self-hosted collaboration tools confirmed on current releases — 30 days Owner:

Attackers are taking over MikroTik routers through internet-facing SSH; CISA has listed a third RouterOS flaw in KEV and warned of a fourth in web management — the cheapest capable box in the sector

CERT Polska, Poland’s national CERT, published confirmation on 5 September that vulnerabilities it disclosed in MikroTik RouterOS are being exploited in the wild, in a two-flaw chain it calls MikroTrick that gives an unauthenticated attacker administrative control of a router whose SSH service is reachable from the internet. CERT Polska reports attacks observed since about 2 September; its 22 September technical analysis names the two CVEs that make up the chain: CVE-2026-67279, an SSH flaw that lets an unauthenticated client open a session channel and send a command, combined with CVE-2026-86060, an argument-injection flaw. New on 25 September: CISA added CVE-2026-67279 (CVSS 6.9) to its Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog with a federal deadline of 28 September, noting that it can be chained to exploit CVE-2026-86060 without authentication. CVE-2026-86060 and CVE-2026-67277, a bandwidth-test flaw, were added on 10 September with a 13 September deadline, which has passed. CVE-2026-67276 is not in the catalog. MikroTik lists fixes in RouterOS 7.24.2, 7.23.4 and 6.49.21, with 7.24.3 and 7.23.6 also covering CVE-2026-67278 according to CERT Polska, and says the upgrade runs a compromise check that writes a critical log entry if the device has been tampered with. New on 29 September: a CISA advisory, ICSA-26-272-06, covers a separate flaw, CVE-2026-84411, an integer underflow in RouterOS’s web-management interface that an unauthenticated attacker can use to run code or crash the router. CISA scores it 9.8 and lists versions before 7.24 as affected, so by its listing a router patched on the 7.23 or 6.49 branch for the SSH chain is still exposed to this one. It is not in the KEV catalog and no exploitation has been reported. This matters to nonprofits not because MikroTik is glamorous but because of who owns one. It is the router that ends up in small charities, community organisations, faith groups, hospices and overseas field offices precisely because it does serious routing for very little money — frequently installed once by a volunteer, a trustee’s relative or a contractor who has long since moved on, and then never touched again. The exposure is not a misconfiguration you made; it is a device nobody has logged into for years with management left open to the internet. If the router falls, everything behind it is reachable and every DNS lookup on your network is the attacker’s to steer.

US, UK and German agencies say SafePay ransomware has hit at least 600 organisations since August 2024 — often through VPN logins and old firewall flaws

CISA, the FBI and the US Secret Service, with Germany’s LKA Rheinland-Pfalz, the UK National Crime Agency and the North West Regional Organised Crime Unit, published joint advisory AA26-293A on 20 October. It says SafePay has hit at least 600 organisations in multiple critical-infrastructure sectors across the Americas, Europe and Australia since August 2024. The group gets in with exposed or brute-forced credentials, VPN access (often from Mullvad VPN addresses), phished accounts and automated exploitation of edge-device flaws; reporting on the advisory names two Cisco ASA and Firepower Threat Defense flaws, CVE-2020-3259 and CVE-2023-20269, both long listed in CISA’s KEV catalog. Once inside it uses ordinary admin tools such as RDP, PsExec and WinRM, copies data out with tools such as RClone, WinSCP and FileZilla, deletes shadow copies and resets NAS devices that hold backups, then encrypts and threatens to publish. SafePay is a closed group with no affiliates. The advisory does not single out nonprofits, but a small organisation with a firewall VPN, reused passwords and backups on a network NAS fits the pattern it describes (editorial assessment).

Active — joint advisory: at least 600 victims since Aug 2024; activity observed Nov 2024–Aug 2026; double extortion Cited flaws in CISA KEV — CVE-2023-20269 added 13 Sep 2023; CVE-2020-3259 added 15 Feb 2024
Fixes exist — Cisco ASA/FTD updates for the cited CVEs; the rest is credential, VPN and backup hygiene Action: Make sure every VPN and remote-access login needs multi-factor authentication, and remove old or shared VPN accounts. Ask your IT provider to confirm that the firewall or VPN is on a current release — if it is a Cisco ASA or Firepower, that it is fixed for CVE-2020-3259 and CVE-2023-20269 — and to alert on VPN logins from commercial VPN services such as Mullvad if your staff do not use them. Keep at least one backup copy that cannot be reached or wiped from the office network, because SafePay resets NAS backup devices, and test a restore.

The FBI and Secret Service say FortiBleed — theft and reuse of Fortinet VPN passwords — is still active, and some owners have been locked out of their own firewalls

In a joint advisory dated 6 October, the FBI and the US Secret Service warned that the campaign researchers call FortiBleed remains an active threat to internet-facing FortiGate firewalls and SSL VPN gateways. There is no CVE. The agencies describe the abuse of reused or leaked credentials and of legacy SHA-256 password storage: password spraying and credential stuffing, offline cracking of extracted password hashes, rogue administrator accounts and enumeration of Active Directory. Some owners were locked out after the attackers changed administrator passwords or deleted existing accounts. The agencies say compromised FortiGates have been used as a way in for ransomware and link the activity to Payload ransomware; SOCRadar’s researchers describe a Russian-speaking access broker with links to INC and Lynx affiliates, which is their assessment rather than the agencies’. The scale figure in circulation — more than 86,644 working device credentials across 194 countries — is SOCRadar’s count from 19 June, not a new tally. The agencies say targets span all 16 US critical-infrastructure sectors. FortiGate is a common firewall and VPN in small and mid-sized organisations, charities included, and is usually looked after by an outside provider (editorial assessment). Because the weakness is passwords rather than code, a fully updated device is still exposed if its VPN and administrator passwords have leaked or been reused.

US authorities seize China-linked hacking tools whose court-listed targets include a multinational NGO

A nonprofit lab serving Planned Parenthood affiliates agrees a $6.1 million settlement over its 2024 breach — the bill for a breach arrives years later

Laboratory Services Cooperative, a Seattle-based nonprofit laboratory that provides diagnostic testing for Planned Parenthood affiliates in 31 US states, has agreed a $6.1 million settlement of the consolidated class action over its 2024 data breach, HIPAA Journal reported on 8 October. The cooperative found unauthorised activity on its network on 27 October 2024, and a forensic investigation found that a hacker had accessed the network and removed data: names, dates of birth, contact details, medical, claims, billing and insurance information, Social Security numbers and other government identifiers. The deadline to object or opt out is 26 October, claims close on 24 November, and the final fairness hearing is set for 4 January 2027. It is not a new incident. The UK’s data-protection regulator is now a board-led Information Commission — for UK charities the ICO name stays, and no change to its powers or to breach reporting was announced

On 30 September, under the Data (Use and Access) Act 2025, the UK’s single-person Information Commissioner was replaced by a board-led Information Commission, The Register reported from the regulator’s announcement. The ICO brand is being kept, Paul Arnold is interim chief executive and the headquarters is moving to Manchester. The Register reported no change to the regulator’s fines or powers, and none to how personal-data breaches are reported. It is not a security incident.
