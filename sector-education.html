Top Story

Attackers who got into a University of Copenhagen test environment reached the national ID numbers of about 525,000 people — and about 20,000 staff and students’ door-access PINs had to be reissued

The University of Copenhagen (UCPH) says an attack detected on Tuesday 20 October 2026, and then brought under control, gave hackers access to one of its test environments. That environment held data on about 525,000 people: full name, Danish national identity number, work and home phone numbers and personal email address. The university says there is no sign that its day-to-day systems were reached. On 25 October it added that the PIN codes of about 20,000 staff and students’ access cards had been compromised; it issued new codes, reopened the restricted campus locations and stressed that this was not a second breach. On 26 October activity that first looked like a new attack led to a controlled shutdown of administrative systems including Workzone and Nimbus; on 27 October UCPH revised that assessment and linked the activity to the original intrusion rather than a new one. Incoming network traffic is blocked, staff are working on campus or over VPN, and the university says the closed systems were not compromised and will reopen in stages after individual security checks. Affected people are being told by letter through Digital Post, and the breach has been reported to the Danish Data Protection Agency. No group has claimed the attack, and UCPH has not said how the attackers got in. It is the second Danish university breach this month, after the Technical University of Denmark’s identity-system breach (see the DTU card). The lesson is the location: a test environment loaded with real national ID numbers for half a million people, because non-production copies of identity and student data are often protected less well than the systems they mirror (editorial assessment).

A “cybersecurity event” at the University of New Mexico Health Sciences Center diverted ambulances for two hours and shut its medicine, nursing, pharmacy and population-health classes for two days — a group called BYOD claims it, and UNM says it made no concessions

UNM Health Sciences Center (HSC) reported a cybersecurity event on Monday 26 October 2026. Between 12:30 and 2:30 pm UNM Health limited new patient intake and diverted ambulances to other area health systems, and outpatient surgeries and appointments were suspended for the rest of the day. Students and non-essential staff at the School of Medicine and the Colleges of Nursing, Pharmacy and Population Health were sent home, and HSC classes, including online ones, were cancelled for the rest of Monday and on Tuesday 27 October; UNM’s central and branch campuses were not affected. UNM says its assessment found the event “affected some end-user devices but did not penetrate core systems or servers”. Hospitals and clinics were fully open again on 27 October, and classes and research resumed on 28 October. State and federal law enforcement are investigating. What is claimed, not confirmed: a group calling itself BYOD says it carried out the attack and took data on students and employees; UNM has not used the word ransomware and has reported no finding on data access. On 28 October UNM said it “did not make concessions” to any group and asked anyone contacted by people claiming responsibility to report it to the FBI’s IC3. An academic health centre shares devices, identity and networks between teaching and patient care, so one event can stop both at once (editorial assessment).

Running a school safely without the network depends on pickup lists, emergency contacts and medical information being available offline (editorial assessment).

High Breach

California student-data breach at testing contractor ETS: a class action was filed on 20 October, and the state now says ETS spotted the intrusion on 25 September and told it the next day

The California Department of Education (CDE) and its testing contractor, Educational Testing Service (ETS), began notifying students on 16 October 2026 that an unauthorised party used a compromised login credential between 22 May and 18 September 2026 to copy files from an ETS SFTP site used to exchange statewide assessment data with the department. According to the CDE, ETS identified the activity on 25 September and notified the department on 26 September; ETS took the site offline and has built a hardened replacement. A class action filed against ETS on 20 October brings claims including negligence, breach of contract, privacy violations and unfair competition under California law, GovTech reports. The plaintiffs’ lawyers put the number of students affected at about 7 million, the figure Politico also reported; the CDE has published no count. The CDE says it is reviewing ETS’s compliance with its contract and data-security requirements together with Cal-CSIC, the California Department of Technology and Cal OES. The notice covers all students enrolled in the three previous school years and some who left as far back as 2006. The files hold names, birth dates, home addresses, schools, grades, student IDs, demographics and test results and, where applicable, disability, Section 504, McKinney-Vento and military-connected status. No Social Security numbers, passwords, financial data or parent details were involved, and no actor has been named. When a state contractor holds student data, districts cannot control it but still answer to the families (editorial assessment).

Unit 42: Qilin and INC ransomware crews use the GlobalProtect flaw CVE-2026-0257 to open VPN sessions past MFA, then go for vCenter, ESXi and Veeam — and education is on their target list

Palo Alto Networks’ Unit 42 said in an update reported on 20 October 2026 that two ransomware operations it tracks as Spikey Scorpius (Qilin) and Tarnished Scorpius (INC) are exploiting CVE-2026-0257, an authentication bypass in PAN-OS GlobalProtect and Prisma Access that Palo Alto fixed in May. The attackers use it to set up VPN sessions despite MFA, then move with remote-management tools, RDP and SMB. In one case they went from the VPN gateway to the vCenter appliance, switched off the ESXi setting that blocks unsigned binaries (execInstalledOnly) and dumped domain credentials offline from a domain controller’s virtual disk, a route that sidesteps endpoint detection inside the guest; they also abused Veeam Backup & Replication flaw CVE-2023-27532 to reach backups. Unit 42’s list of targeted sectors includes education, alongside healthcare, government, manufacturing and others; no school or university has been named. ReliaQuest had earlier tied the flaw to Qilin and Settra. Separately, Group-IB reported on 23 October that an access broker it calls k0t used the same flaw and a FortiWeb bug against network edges worldwide, and that many of its victims later appeared on Qilin and The Gentlemen leak sites. A gateway patched in May can still carry access taken before then (editorial assessment).

Google Threat Intelligence Group and Mandiant, with ManageEngine’s threat lab, reported on 16 October 2026 that an actor Google tracks as UNC7017 altered two configuration files on AOMEI’s update servers for five to ten minutes at a time — confirmed windows on 28 May, 16 July and 20 July 2026, and probably others — so that AOMEI Backupper clients checking in during a window fetched and ran a malicious package. The chain installed a downloader called CROSSROAD and a backdoor called SHINYROAD, staged the legitimate Radmin remote-administration tool, pushed the Adaptix C2 framework disguised as Nitro PDF on some machines and, on 20 July, tried to leak NetNTLMv2 hashes through a hidden WebDAV pop-up. AOMEI first suggested the files were altered in transit, which ManageEngine says the evidence contradicts, and told Mandiant on 29 July that it had secured its servers; how the attacker got in is unexplained. Free disk-imaging tools like Backupper often reach lab and staff machines without central approval (editorial assessment).

ShinyHunters’ PeopleSoft flaw CVE-2026-35273 turns out to be three chained bugs, ZDI shows — the group used it mainly against academic institutions in June, is again exploiting it past WAF rules, and the FBI’s own breach is reported to trace to an unapplied patch

Update, 20–28 October 2026: an analysis by Trend Micro’s Zero Day Initiative (ZDI), dated 20 October and reported by The Hacker News on 28 October, shows the “zero-day” is three chained flaws in PeopleTools 8.61 and 8.62: a server-side request forgery in the Integration Gateway (PSIGW), a PSEMHUB trust check that ships with 127.0.0.1 in allowedhosts.properties, and a third flaw not yet detailed. All three are fixed under the single CVE, with separate IDs pending. Shadowserver honeypots logged exploitation attempts through 24 October. 9 October: FBI Director Kash Patel announced another arrest in the ShinyHunters investigation, describing the group as the one believed responsible for the FBIjobs.gov incident. The New York Times and CBS News, citing unnamed sources, report that the suspect is a Canadian citizen arrested in Pennsylvania; no name or charges have been made public. The FBI says the group has breached more than 140 organisations and collected at least $70 million in extortion payments since last year. A 24-year-old was arrested in Amsterdam on 15 September with FBI support, and Reuters’ sources report a further arrest in Jordan on 29 September; this page names none of the suspects. 6 October: FBI Cyber Division Assistant Director Brett Leatherman said the jobs-portal breach came from a security failure on a platform run by a third-party organisation, after a contractor did not apply a patch issued specifically for it, and the FBI has removed the contractor. The FBI has not named the product or a CVE. Reuters, citing sources, reports that the platform was Oracle PeopleSoft, and The Hacker News ties the breach to CVE-2026-35273 and the WAF bypass Mandiant described; treat that link as reported, not confirmed. The campaign itself is confirmed and unchanged.
