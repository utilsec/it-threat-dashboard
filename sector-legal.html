Top Story

Silent Ransom Group escalates a phone-first campaign aimed specifically at law firms, chasing litigation strategy and privileged client files

Reporting published 18 August 2026 describes Silent Ransom Group — also tracked as UNC3753, Luna Moth and Chatty Spider — concentrating on law firms and legal-services providers with a social-engineering playbook that starts on the telephone rather than in an inbox. The pattern: a caller posing as internal or outsourced IT support, often paired with an invoice or billing pretext, talks a staff member into installing a legitimate remote monitoring and management (RMM) tool, which hands the attacker interactive access under the cover of an approved-looking administrative agent. The group has also been described impersonating technicians in person to reach systems physically. The objective is not encryption for its own sake but the material a firm cannot afford to lose control of — litigation strategy documents, client work product and privileged files — taken quietly and then used for extortion. The write-up points to the May 2026 ransomware incident at Fox Rothschild LLP, and the proposed class action that followed it, as the sector's cautionary case. The distinguishing risk for law firms is what a privilege breach does beyond ordinary breach liability: exposure of privileged client material pulls in professional-conduct duties and client-notification obligations that do not attach to a typical corporate data loss. In an otherwise quiet week for the sector, this is the only item that is specifically about attacking law firms, and it deserves to be treated as the live threat rather than background noise. The controls that answer it are unglamorous — a verified call-back procedure for anyone claiming to be IT, an allow-list for RMM software with alerting on any new agent install, and a standing rule that nobody authorises remote access on an inbound call.

High Campaign

An FBI FLASH and a Mandiant report describe the same three-year campaign against US law firms — and it now includes operators walking in the door with USB drives

The group tracked as Silent Ransom Group, UNC3753, Luna Moth and Chatty Spider has been working US law firms since spring 2023, and both an FBI FLASH alert and Google Mandiant’s GTIG report “Seeking Counsel” confirm the campaign is still running. The tradecraft is almost entirely human. Operators call the firm with invoice-themed pretexts or pose as internal IT, talk a partner, paralegal or billing clerk into a screen-sharing session, and install a remote monitoring and management tool under the cover of routine support. Exfiltration runs over WinSCP and Rclone. There is no encryption stage at all — the leverage is the client data itself, which in a law firm means privileged material the firm cannot simply restore from backup. The outlier detail, and the one that breaks most legal threat models, is that operators have been sent in person to insert USB storage into victim machines. Physical access is not a hypothetical for a business built around reception areas, war rooms, deposition suites and visiting counsel. Fox Rothschild LLP is named in the reporting over a May 2026 incident and now faces a proposed class action in federal court in Pennsylvania, which is the part legal IT should carry to the management committee: the incident cost is not the ransom, it is the litigation and the client-notification obligations that follow a confirmed disclosure of privileged files.

Apollo’s breach notification is a legal-sector event too — the same help-desk impersonation playbook is already pointed at outside counsel

Apollo Global Management has filed a California breach notification confirming that attackers were inside its cloud platforms between 6 and 10 July 2026, an intrusion not discovered until 12 August. The exposed data is the damaging kind: names, dates of birth, contact details, home addresses and Social Security numbers. Google attributes the activity to BlackFile and its brands — Redact, Pink, Helix and Falcon — an affiliate cluster tied to The Com, operating through vishing and IT help-desk impersonation rather than any exploited software flaw. Extortion demands start at roughly $3 million. Blackstone and Bain Capital were targeted with malicious infrastructure, but a breach of either is NOT confirmed. Legal departments and outside counsel should read this as their event as much as a financial-services one. Private-equity cloud platforms hold what a deal team’s document room holds: draft purchase agreements, limited-partner registers, diligence files and personal identifiers for investors and portfolio-company staff. Where a firm acted as transaction counsel, it is sitting on parallel copies of the same material. The intrusion method is the second half of the point — Mandiant documents this identical help-desk impersonation playbook running against US law firms, so the technique that worked at Apollo is already aimed at its advisers. Firms on Blackstone or Bain matters should assume adjacent targeting rather than wait for a notification, and should expect litigation-hold and client-notification questions this week regardless of whether their own environment was touched.

High Advisory

Two patch items outrank the rest of the queue for a law-firm estate — an exploited Zimbra command injection and a NetScaler authentication bypass

Zimbra’s CVE-2026-73570 (CVSS 8.9) is already being exploited: CERT Polska has confirmed in-the-wild activity in which a crafted SMTP request reaches the SNMP notification path and executes operating-system commands as the zimbra user, with no authentication required. It affects ZCS below 10.1.20 where the zimbra-snmp package is installed and SNMP notifications are enabled. CISA added it to KEV on 21 August with a 24 August federal due date. Zimbra’s install base skews toward government, education and legal — small and mid-sized firms that wanted mail, calendar and document sharing without a Microsoft tenancy — so the population most likely to be running it overlaps closely with the population least able to run a 24-hour patch cycle. Mail-server compromise at a firm is a privilege event before it is an availability one. The Citrix item is the opposite shape. CVE-2026-19490 is a critical authentication bypass affecting NetScaler AAA vServer or Gateway deployments that have a SAML Action configured; no exploitation has been flagged and the exact CVSS is not confirmed in the advisory reporting. The same advisory carries CVE-2026-19489, a high-severity memory-overflow denial of service via SIP ALG on large-scale NAT. NetScaler is the remote-access front door for a great many firms, and an authentication bypass on it hands an attacker the same session context Silent Ransom Group currently works the phones to obtain.

A ransomware attack struck Colombia's Ministry of Justice on 2 August 2026, encrypting files and disrupting systems used for drug monitoring and legal-case processing. The timing put the incident five days ahead of the country's presidential transition, adding operational pressure to the ministry's response; that timing is noted here only as operational context. For legal-sector readers, the case is a reminder that court and justice-ministry IT — case-management systems, drug-monitoring platforms and the legal-process workflows that depend on them — sits squarely inside ransomware crews' target set, with downtime landing directly on active legal proceedings.

Hackers breach Liechtenstein's foundations and trusts registry, accessing data on ~31,000 entities

Attackers penetrated Liechtenstein's confidential register of foundations and trusts in late July 2026, gaining access to information on roughly 31,000 registered entities. Exposed data reportedly includes beneficial owners' names, dates of birth, nationality and place of residence; the government says no financial data, addresses or phone numbers were accessed. Prime Minister Brigitte Haas said the country will not pay a ransom if one is demanded and that officials are “examining various scenarios” for how the incident develops. The registry was established in 2021 as part of Liechtenstein's move to comply with international financial-transparency and anti-money-laundering standards after the jurisdiction's history as a low-transparency financial haven. Officials plan to rebuild the registry online, saying continued access for international financial authorities outweighs the risk of a repeat incident. For legal practices doing trust, estate and beneficial-ownership work, or AML/KYC due diligence touching Liechtenstein-registered structures, this is a direct hit on a source register those workflows rely on.

High Advisory

CISA and FBI refresh the Medusa advisory: 500+ critical-infrastructure victims and exploits used before public disclosure

CISA and the FBI published an update to the #StopRansomware Medusa advisory (AA25-071A) on 18 August 2026. The cumulative victim tally passed 500 critical-infrastructure organisations as of April 2026, up from roughly 300 in March 2025 — about 200 new victims in a year — across healthcare, critical manufacturing, the defense industrial base, government facilities, IT and financial services. The operationally important finding is tempo: the agencies report that Medusa weaponises newly disclosed vulnerabilities within roughly 24 hours, and has been observed using exploits up to a week before public disclosure, because the group buys exploits rather than developing them. For a law firm or legal-services provider, that timeline is the point. A monthly or even weekly patch cadence on internet-facing gear is not a defence against an adversary already operating inside the window before the advisory lands, which shifts the emphasis toward reducing internet-exposed attack surface, enforcing MFA everywhere, and building detection that assumes the edge device will eventually be reached.

High Advisory

Gunra joint advisory: unpatched Fortinet VPN entry, observed MFA bypass, terabytes exfiltrated before any demand

A joint advisory from CISA, the FBI and the Korean National Police Agency (AA26-222A, 10 August 2026) documents the Gunra ransomware operation. Initial access is repeatedly through unpatched internet-facing Fortinet VPN and firewall appliances — CVE-2024-55591 and CVE-2025-24472 are named — and the advisory records observed multi-factor authentication bypass, not merely an absence of MFA. In at least one case tens of terabytes were exfiltrated using OneDrive and SharePoint as the egress path, with extortion demands in the tens of millions; the operators work roughly 22:00 to 06:00 in the victim's local time, when a firm's help desk and monitoring coverage are thinnest. Two things matter for legal environments specifically. First, this is exfiltration-first extortion, the shape that hurts a firm most — privileged material leaves before anything is encrypted and before anyone notices an outage. Second, using sanctioned collaboration services as the exfiltration channel means the traffic resembles ordinary document work unless volume and destination-tenant anomalies are actually monitored.

Cameron Curry, a contract data analyst at Siemens-owned Brightly Software, was convicted on six counts of extortion and sentenced to two years in prison plus supervised release, reported 13–14 August 2026. This case is a usable precedent to cite internally when arguing for scoped, time-bound contractor access and for egress monitoring that does not exempt trusted vendor accounts.

Critical Advisory

CISA adds four actively-exploited flaws to KEV — including a SharePoint authentication weakness at the heart of firm document management

CISA added four vulnerabilities to the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog on 18 August 2026, with a federal remediation deadline of 21 August. The one that lands hardest on legal environments is CVE-2026-55040, a weak-authentication flaw in Microsoft SharePoint allowing a security-feature bypass: SharePoint and its document libraries are the beating heart of most firms' document management, matter workspaces and client extranets, so an authentication bypass there is a bypass of the control that keeps one client's material separate from another's. The other three are CVE-2026-33824, a double free in the Microsoft Windows IKE service extensions (IKEEXT) leading to remote code execution; CVE-2026-59310, a path-traversal flaw in Broadcom VMware vCenter enabling code execution (covered separately below, and under active mass exploitation); and CVE-2026-65400, an improper-authentication issue in Apple macOS Screen Sharing, which matters for the Mac-heavy partner and litigation-support populations common in law firms. All four are in KEV because exploitation has been observed, not predicted. CVSS scores are not restated here where they could not be confirmed against a primary source — treat the KEV listing itself as the trigger.

Critical Advisory

VMware vCenter flaw under mass exploitation — 361 victim IPs in 47 countries, reverse-SSH persistence, Babuk-derived ransomware

CVE-2026-59310 is a directory-traversal flaw in the VMware vCenter syslog service, CVSS 9.8, allowing unauthenticated code execution. Broadcom published VMSA-2026-0006 on 29 July 2026 with no workaround available — patching is the only remedy. Researchers observed the first victims connecting to attacker infrastructure on 3 August, and by 5 August roughly 95% of an eventual 361 victim IP addresses across 47 countries had already appeared, concentrated in Germany, the United States, Turkey, Iran and France. The chain gives the actor root on vCenter, plants a cron job for reverse-SSH persistence, and in some cases deploys ransomware derived from leaked Babuk source. CISA added the flaw to the KEV catalog on 18 August with a 21 August federal deadline. For a law firm or legal-services provider running its own virtualised estate, vCenter is the management plane over case-management, document-management and e-discovery workloads — and frequently over backup infrastructure too, which is precisely what makes root there worth having. If your vCenter was internet-reachable and unpatched at any point since late July, patching alone is not enough: hunt for unexpected cron entries and reverse-SSH connections.

High Advisory

Windows Task Host privilege-escalation flaw now being abused by ransomware crews

CVE-2025-60710 is a link-following privilege-escalation flaw in the Windows Task Host that lets a low-privileged local user reach SYSTEM on Windows 11 and Windows Server 2025. Microsoft patched it in November 2025 and it entered the CISA KEV catalog on 13 April 2026; the KEV entry was updated on 18 August 2026 to reflect that ransomware operators are now using it. This is not a new patch cycle — it is a hunt-for-stragglers signal. The machines that matter are the ones that miss routine patching: long-lived servers with change-freeze exemptions, laptops belonging to partners who defer reboots for weeks, and workstations in litigation-support or e-discovery roles that are rebuilt infrequently. A local escalation to SYSTEM is the step that converts a single phished or vished user — exactly the foothold the Silent Ransom Group campaign above is designed to obtain — into domain-wide movement and encryption.

Policy Regulation

UK Solicitors Regulation Authority issues an AI warning notice after fabricated case law and client data in public AI tools

The Solicitors Regulation Authority issued a warning notice on 17 August 2026, reported 18 August, after finding two distinct failures among solicitors: fabricated case law generated by AI tools being submitted to courts, and confidential client information being entered into publicly available AI services. The SRA's position is that solicitors remain fully accountable for AI output regardless of how it was produced, and that firms must be able to demonstrate human oversight and risk-based governance over how these tools are used. It is easy to file this as a professional-conduct story, but the second half lands directly on firm IT. Client confidences typed into a public model is a data-confidentiality and acceptable-use problem, and the evidence the regulator expects — that oversight and governance exist and are working — is evidence IT is usually the only function able to produce: which AI services are reachable from firm devices, which are sanctioned and under what contractual data terms, what is logged, and what data-loss controls stop client material leaving through a browser tab. Firms outside England and Wales should read the notice as a preview rather than someone else's problem; regulators elsewhere are moving in the same direction.

New Jersey federal judge dismisses a data-breach class action against a background-check company

A federal judge in New Jersey dismissed a data-breach class action brought against a background-check company, reported 16 August 2026. The ruling turns on the familiar threshold questions in US breach litigation — standing, and whether plaintiffs pleaded a sufficiently concrete injury rather than a speculative risk of future harm. For in-house counsel and firm risk teams the value here is calibration rather than doctrine: the litigation exposure baked into breach-cost models is only as good as the assumptions about which claims actually survive a motion to dismiss, and rulings in this line keep moving that number. It cuts both ways — a dismissal on standing is not a finding that the security was adequate, and it does not touch regulatory exposure, contractual notification duties, or the professional-conduct obligations that attach when the exposed material is privileged.
