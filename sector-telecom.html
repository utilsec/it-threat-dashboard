AOMEI Backupper’s own update servers were tampered with for minutes at a time to plant backdoors — Google counts telecom firms among the victims

Google Threat Intelligence and Mandiant, with ManageEngine, reported in mid-October 2026 that an actor Google tracks as UNC7017 repeatedly altered two configuration files on AOMEI’s update and notification servers for windows of five to ten minutes, on dates including 28 May, 16 July and 20 July. AOMEI first suggested the traffic was intercepted in transit, which ManageEngine disputes, and says it has since secured its servers, but it has not explained how they were altered. A secured server does not clean a machine that was already infected. For operators, backup agents run with high privilege on the servers that matter most, so a vendor’s update channel is part of the attack surface (editorial assessment).

High Campaign

The FBI and Secret Service say the FortiBleed credential campaign against FortiGate firewalls is still active — no CVE, and targets span every US critical-infrastructure sector

In a joint advisory dated 6 October 2026 (JCSA-20261006-01), the FBI and US Secret Service warned that the FortiBleed campaign against internet-facing Fortinet FortiGate firewalls and SSL VPN gateways remains an active threat. There is no CVE: the agencies describe abuse of reused or leaked credentials and of legacy SHA-256 password storage, and researchers describe credential stuffing and spraying from leak dumps, offline cracking of extracted hashes and a Go-based sniffer that passively captures authentication traffic. Some owners were locked out after attackers changed administrator passwords or deleted accounts. The advisory cites SOCRadar’s count of more than 86,644 compromised devices in 194 countries — a figure from 19 June, not a new count — and the agencies say targets span all 16 US critical-infrastructure sectors, communications among them. Access is resold, and authorities say compromised FortiGates have served as ransomware entry points; SOCRadar says it has confirmed at least 12 ransomware deployments. Researchers describe the operator as a Russian-speaking access broker; that is their assessment, not the agencies’. For carriers and managed-security providers that run FortiGate fleets for customers, the same credential questions apply to every tenant.

Rapid7 finds BPFDoor and a new SMTP-mimicking implant, AVERAT, built to blend into telecom environments in South Korea and Taiwan — relayed through devices on a carrier’s network, not through the carrier

Rapid7 published research on 2 October 2026 on Linux implants that, in its description, blend into the software and device conventions of the telecom environments they target. In South Korea it found a new BPFDoor variant that can tunnel over HTTPS POST and a BPF-based Rekoobe variant that filters on port 25; mail-security appliances such as SpamSniper are among the device types it describes as targeted. In Taiwan it found six builds of a new modular implant it calls AVERAT, delivered by an AES-encrypted dropper. AVERAT connects out on TCP 25 and behaves like a mail client — EHLO, then STARTTLS, then its own encryption — and checks in every 600 to 699 seconds, so on the wire it looks like an appliance sending mail. The relay infrastructure is the part to read carefully. Rapid7 says the command-and-control relays were compromised customer equipment belonging to third-party victims — NAS boxes, ADSL and FTTH customer premises equipment and CCTV recorders — sitting in Chunghwa Telecom’s HiNet address space in three Taiwanese cities. That is end-user equipment on a carrier’s network repurposed as relays; Rapid7 does not report a compromise of the carrier itself. It is equally careful on attribution: it places the activity in the pattern of China-nexus covert relay (ORB) networks but found no infrastructure or indicator overlap with any named ORB network, and names no actor. Rapid7 publishes hashes for the dropper, the AVERAT builds and the Korean cluster, plus domains and IP addresses. Its detection cues travel well into any operator estate: processes whose executable has been deleted from disk, unexpected state files and scripts on appliances, and outbound SMTP from an appliance to mail-role hostnames that resolve to consumer or embedded devices.

Medium Threat Intel

Nozomi’s Cling botnet hides its command channel in STUN traffic dressed up as Google, recruits routers and DVRs through old flaws, and has aimed DDoS at a South Korean ISP

Nozomi Networks Labs published research on 1 October 2026 on a botnet it calls Cling. Its main way in is CVE-2021-35394, a remote code-execution flaw in the UDPServer component of the Realtek Jungle SDK, and it carries exploits for a string of other old router and DVR flaws, among them CVE-2014-8361, CVE-2016-10372 (Eir D1000), CVE-2023-41011 (FiberHome SR1041F and China Mobile HG6543C4), CVE-2024-3721 (TBK DVR) and CVE-2025-34037 (Linksys). The distinctive part is the command channel: Cling encodes commands in STUN-style exchanges, carrying them in transaction IDs and spoofing source addresses to look like stun.l.google.com, and infected devices contact 13 hard-coded STUN servers. Nozomi confirmed the channel by registering its own decoy bots with each server and receiving operator commands hours later on ports it had advertised to only one of them. Fortinet’s PSIRT advisory FG-IR-26-175, published 1 October 2026, covers CVE-2026-104286, a path-traversal flaw combined with improper NULL-byte handling in FortiMail that lets an unauthenticated attacker write arbitrary files to the underlying system with crafted HTTP or HTTPS requests. India orders operators to show callers’ registered names on 4G and newer networks from 1 November — and not to keep the name data after the call

India’s Department of Telecommunications issued a circular dated 13 October 2026 requiring telecom service providers to display a caller’s registered name with the number on incoming calls from 1 November 2026, The Hacker News reported on 15 October. The rule, Calling Name Presentation (CNAP), covers mainly wireless networks on 4G and newer technologies; 2G, 3G and fixed-line rollout will be examined separately. A name saved in the recipient’s contacts takes precedence; otherwise the originating operator’s subscriber database supplies the name for domestic calls, and calls from abroad are labelled as international. Originating operators must maintain the name-to-number database that receiving operators query, must offer calling line identification restriction so users can withhold their identity, and must not retain the calling name after the call ends. Business and bulk connections may present a preferred name only if it is a registered trademark or GST-registered trade name. The stated aim is to help people identify unknown callers and to curb spam and fraud. For operators the security work is in the new inter-operator lookup: a database every network queries on every call is both a privacy asset and an availability dependency, and the circular asks for a secure-by-design approach.
