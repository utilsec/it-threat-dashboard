A fraud network is running more than 119,000 fake storefronts to harvest shoppers’ card details

Researchers reported on 8 September 2026 a fraud operation named DoppelCart that runs over 119,000 domains hosting fake online shops whose purpose is to collect payment card details at checkout. The scale is the point: at that volume the operators can rotate through domains faster than blocklists and takedown processes work, so any individual site being killed costs them nothing. For legitimate merchants the exposure runs in two directions. The first is direct fraud pressure — cards harvested from these sites are used elsewhere, which shows up in your chargeback and authorisation-decline rates without anything having gone wrong on your own platform. The second is brand impersonation, and it is the one merchants usually discover late. Storefronts of this kind routinely copy real product imagery, descriptions, logos and even policy pages, so customers who are defrauded believe they bought from you, complain to you, and post about you. Practically, that makes brand monitoring a security function rather than a marketing one, and it makes your customer-service inbox a threat-intelligence feed: complaints about an order you have no record of are usually the first signal that your brand is being cloned.

Carhartt’s customer database was taken out of its Databricks tenant, not its storefront — 12.9 million records are now public

The ShinyHunters extortion crew claimed an intrusion at Carhartt on 13 August, demanded roughly $3.3 million, and published the data after the workwear brand declined to negotiate. Reporting on 27 August put the exposure at about 12.9 million accounts containing names, email addresses, phone numbers and postal addresses, together with internal corporate material and more than 15,000 carhartt.com employee addresses. Carhartt’s statement was blunt: after review with leadership it would not move forward with negotiations. No payment card data has been reported as involved, and no CVE applies. The mechanism is what retail security teams should read closely. The data did not come out of the e-commerce platform, the store network or the ERP — it came out of a Databricks cloud analytics workspace, the place where marketing and merchandising teams assemble the full customer record precisely so it can be queried. That tier is usually provisioned by a data team rather than by IT security, is frequently reached with long-lived personal access tokens or OAuth app credentials rather than SSO with conditional access, and rarely carries the same egress monitoring as the transactional systems it copies from. ShinyHunters has spent 2026 harvesting exactly this class of target across SaaS and cloud data platforms. If your customer 360 lives in a lakehouse, the controls that matter are token lifetime, IP allow-listing, per-table access review and alerting on bulk export — not the WAF in front of checkout.

Wordfence blocked over 440,000 exploit attempts against two WordPress upload flaws — the Elementor Pro bug this page flagged as unexploited is now under mass attack

The August edition carried Elementor Pro’s file-upload bypass, CVE-2026-32475, as a patched issue with no observed exploitation. That has changed. Wordfence telemetry published in early September records more than 440,000 blocked attempts across two vulnerabilities: roughly 250,000 against Super Forms, CVE-2026-14894 at CVSS 9.8, fixed in 6.3.314, and around 190,000 against Elementor Pro, CVE-2026-32475 rated 9.0 to 9.8 and fixed in 4.2.2. Both are missing or bypassable file-type validation that lets an unauthenticated attacker write a PHP file into a web-reachable directory and then call it. The Elementor technique submits the form field as an array with an empty first element and PHP in the second, dropping a randomly named .php file into /wp-content/uploads/elementor/forms/; Super Forms attacks POST base64-encoded PHP to admin-ajax.php disguised as an image and then use the resulting uploader shell to stage more files. Super Forms attacks began on 14 July and peaked on 18 August; Elementor Pro exploitation started on 19 August, the day after details circulated, from about twenty source addresses. For retail this is the broadest live exposure of the fortnight. WooCommerce storefronts, campaign microsites and agency-built brand pages run on exactly these components, licence-gated plugins stop updating quietly when a licence lapses, and a shell on a shop yields session cookies, admin accounts and the ability to modify checkout pages.

More than 5,400 hacked sites — WordPress and PrestaShop among them — are serving fake verification prompts whose payload lives on a blockchain

Netskope reported on 5 September that it had identified over 5,400 compromised websites, predominantly WordPress and PrestaShop installations, injected with script that serves ClickFix lures: a fake CAPTCHA or verification prompt that persuades the visitor to copy a PowerShell command and run it themselves, so no exploit and no download prompt is involved. What makes the campaign awkward to disrupt is where the payload is kept. The operators use the EtherHiding technique, storing the malicious code in smart contracts on the BNB Smart Chain testnet, so there is no host to suspend and no domain to seize — the compromised site simply reads the contract. Netskope recorded activity climbing steadily since spring, peaking at 536 sites contacting BSC endpoints in a single day, and describes a newer variant that replaces the PowerShell stage with a WebRTC data-channel stager: it creates a peer connection and data channel that bypasses the standard handshake, giving the operator an encrypted path for dynamic code execution with no file written to disk. PrestaShop’s presence in that population is the retail angle, and there are two consequences for merchants. A compromised storefront is not only a card-skimming risk; it can be turned into a delivery mechanism against your own shoppers, and the reputational damage from a customer who was told to paste a command by your site is comparable to a skimmer. Second, the identical lure works on staff — store managers, help-desk users and marketing teams are the population most likely to comply with an on-screen instruction to fix a page that appears broken.

Talos now ties the exploited CVSS 10.0 Secure Firewall Management Center bypass to three actor clusters — one deploying a Sandworm-linked implant, one deploying Qilin ransomware

Cisco has confirmed active exploitation of CVE-2026-20079, an authentication bypass in Secure Firewall Management Center and Security Cloud Control scored CVSS 10.0. An unauthenticated remote attacker can bypass authentication and execute commands as root. CISA added it to the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog on 9 September with a federal remediation deadline of 12 September. There is no workaround: the patched software has to be installed, and Cisco’s guidance is that an already-compromised device needs remediation separate from the upgrade. Update, 11 September: Cisco Talos has published attribution that materially changes the shape of this. Exploitation of CVE-2026-20079, chained in places with CVE-2026-20316 (CVSS 5.3, an unauthenticated low-privilege login), is now attributed to at least three distinct clusters. UAT-12197 planted JSP web shells and Java command executors to harvest credentials. UAT-11823 deployed Netcat reverse shells and, more significantly, a variant of Cyclops Blink — a modular ELF implant previously attributed to the Russian state-sponsored group Sandworm. UAT-11988 used the management centre’s own built-in tooling for reconnaissance, credential theft and security-tool termination, then deployed Qilin ransomware on selected systems. Cisco has confirmed the findings and released hotfixes.
