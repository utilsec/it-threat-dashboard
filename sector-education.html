Top Story

The PaperCut campaign is now running at scale against schools and universities — 440-plus compromised instances, education named as the primary target

The chained exploitation of CVE-2026-81578 (an improper access control flaw letting an unauthenticated attacker change system configuration) and CVE-2026-82078 (unsafe dynamic class loading in the database connection utilities, allowing arbitrary Java bytecode execution) in PaperCut NG and MF has stopped being a handful of intrusions and become a sector-wide campaign. The Hacker News reported on 10 September 2026 that the same operator scaled the attack using hundreds of automated AI agents, reaching more than 440 compromised instances across the United States, United Kingdom, France, Spain, Canada, Belgium, Portugal, Australia, Germany and Switzerland, and that the campaign is “mainly targeting the education sector”. That matches what Arctic Wolf’s Adversary Research Team published on 5 September from intrusions running from K-12 districts to major universities in the US and Europe: after the exploit the activity is unglamorous and consistent — basic reconnaissance, creation of a privileged local account such as “Administrator17”, credential-harvesting tooling (lsa_collect.exe), Metasploit and Meterpreter Java payloads, and systematic searches through PaperCut’s own configuration files for passwords, secrets and LDAP bind credentials. Read the target selection plainly. Print management is one of the few software categories whose core market is schools; PaperCut servers are usually domain-joined because they enforce per-student quotas against directory services; and they are often owned by a print or desktop team rather than by security. That is a credential store in a low-attention corner of the estate, and it is now being worked through by automation rather than by hand. Both CVEs were added to CISA’s Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog on 31 August, and PaperCut’s first emergency build was bypassed and replaced — the final hotfix shipped 1 September. One thing has improved since yesterday: PaperCut has now replaced those emergency hotfixes with fully QA-tested maintenance releases, so the patch you apply is no longer an out-of-band build. One caution on the headline number — reporting differs slightly on scale, with BleepingComputer citing 395 confirmed compromised organisations and The Hacker News 440-plus; either figure describes the same campaign and neither is a ceiling, because the scanning is automated and ongoing.

Critical Incident

Springfield, Massachusetts escalates — 23,000-plus students affected, nurses locked out of student medical records, and officials call the attacker “very sophisticated”

This incident got materially worse at a public briefing on 10 September 2026. Springfield Public Schools — one of Massachusetts’s largest districts, with more than 23,000 students — closed its school buildings on Tuesday 8 September after unauthorised actors accessed the district network. Officials now describe it as a “Level 4” cybersecurity incident, with breach activity reportedly beginning around 1 September and escalating over the following weekend, blocking email, phone and computer systems. The detail that should stop a district technology leader is a health-and-safety one: access to students’ medical records was disrupted, leaving school nurses unable to dispense medications or manage allergy information. Superintendent Dr. Sonia Dinnall told the briefing the attacker group was “very sophisticated”. State and federal agencies are investigating; no ransomware group has been named, and officials say it is not yet determined whether student or staff data was compromised. Phone systems were expected back by Monday 14 September, when students return, and the school calendar is being revised without adding days. The same week, the city of Everett, Massachusetts closed City Hall following a separate incident detected around 6:30 on Sunday evening; officials have not said whether the two are related. The lesson is the dependency map rather than the malware: transport routing, food service, attendance, building access, emergency notification — and, as Springfield has now shown, the nurse’s medication and allergy record — all sit on ordinary IT, and losing them closes a school as effectively as a snowstorm.

High Incident

Westfield, New Jersey walks back its “equipment failure” account after INC Ransom posts proof screenshots — and Salida, Colorado says damaged files cannot be recovered

DataBreaches.net reported both on 9 September 2026. The Salida School District in Colorado has disclosed a cyberattack that began around 6:30 a.m. on 29 June 2026 and was discovered roughly two hours later; the district shut down its network and brought in outside specialists. Multiple network-connected devices suffered file damage on their hard drives, and the district states that “some damaged files will not be able to be recovered”. It initially said no information appeared to have been accessed or exfiltrated, has not released final forensic conclusions, and has not disclosed whether ransomware or extortion was involved or how the attackers got in. Separately, Westfield Public Schools in New Jersey lost internet, Wi-Fi, instructional tools, phones, clocks and its student information system district-wide during the first week of the school year. The superintendent first attributed the outage to “equipment failure”. The INC Ransom group then posted the district on its leak site with two screenshots offered as proof of access and claimed it had encrypted district files and servers — and that forced a public correction: by 9–10 September Superintendent Raymond González had acknowledged that the cause “remained under investigation”, a notable shift from the district’s original explanation. Schools stayed open using non-digital instruction. Hold the line on what is established: the outage and INC Ransom’s posting with screenshots are confirmed; the claim that district files and servers were encrypted is INC Ransom’s alone and the district has not confirmed ransomware. Two lessons sit on top of each other here: destructive impact can outlast the incident by months, and an early “it’s just equipment” statement is very hard to walk back once an extortion group publishes evidence.

High Breach

Mathspace discloses a breach affecting more than a million students, teachers, staff and parents through a self-hosted reporting tool

The education technology provider Mathspace disclosed on 7 September 2026 a data breach affecting more than one million students, teachers, school staff and parents or guardians. The company says attackers reached a self-hosted Metabase instance it used for internal reporting. That detail is the whole lesson. Metabase is a business-intelligence front end, not a product feature; it exists so staff can query the production data without touching it, which means it is routinely pointed at a database containing everything and is routinely stood up by a data or analytics team rather than by whoever owns the application’s security. It is also the kind of internal tool that quietly acquires an internet-facing address so people can use it from home. For schools and districts the exposure is one you did not configure and cannot patch: a supplier’s internal analytics stack now sits in your data-protection scope, and it holds records on children. Notification duties, parent communication and any state student-privacy obligations fall to the district as much as to the vendor.

Critical Active Exploitation

The critical Citrix NetScaler authentication bypass is now in KEV with 56 documented exploitation attempts — and NetScaler is the campus VPN and virtual-lab gateway

CVE-2026-19490 is a critical authentication bypass in NetScaler ADC and NetScaler Gateway that affects appliances configured as an AAA virtual server or as a Gateway — that is, SSL VPN, ICA Proxy, CVPN or RDP Proxy, which between them describe nearly every way a university publishes remote desktops, virtual labs and off-campus access. Citrix disclosed and patched it on 19 August without flagging exploitation. That changed at the start of this month. On 3 September, sensors operated by Previdian recorded requests matching the public proof-of-concept from three distinct source addresses geolocated to Australia, the United States and Germany, per researcher Ryan Dewhurst; Belgium’s national cybersecurity centre, NCC-BE, issued its own warning. The tally has since grown: CISA added CVE-2026-19490 to the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog on 10 September alongside Cisco Secure FMC and Fortinet, with a 12 September federal remediation deadline, and reporting on that action documents 56 exploitation attempts since early September. Shadowserver tracks more than 22,000 NetScaler ADC appliances and close to 1,700 Gateway instances exposed to the internet, though it cannot see which of those are patched or in a vulnerable configuration. For education the exposure profile is unusually bad in one specific way: a campus NetScaler is deliberately open to the whole world because students and staff connect from anywhere, it fronts single sign-on, and an authentication bypass in front of an SSO estate is an identity problem rather than a network problem. If your appliance was reachable and unpatched at any point since the proof-of-concept became public, the honest posture is to assume session and credential compromise and work backwards from there.

Medium Research

Education is the world’s most-attacked sector by weekly volume — 4,696 attacks per organisation, more than double the cross-industry average

Check Point Research found that education organisations absorbed an average of 4,696 cyberattacks per week between January and July 2026, an 8 per cent year-on-year increase and more than twice the 2,150-attack global cross-industry average. The attack types behind the number are not exotic: fake education-themed websites and phishing campaigns aimed at students, parents and educators to harvest personal and financial information, with activity intensifying ahead of the back-to-school period. The figure is useful for two things and misleading for a third. It is useful as a budget argument — education is not being hit at the same rate as everyone else and being under-resourced — and useful as a case for prioritising identity and domain-abuse controls over yet another endpoint tool, because impersonation of the institution is the dominant technique. It is misleading if read as a measure of breaches; a weekly attack count is telemetry volume, not successful compromises. The operational takeaway is that your brand and your enrolment, payment and financial-aid workflows are the attack surface as much as your servers are.

High Threat Actor

PREY-0058 is calling IT help desks to reach senior staff, then stealing the session instead of the password

Arctic Wolf published research on 7 September 2026 on a data-theft and extortion cluster it tracks as PREY-0058. The route in is the IT service desk: the actor telephones posing as a senior employee, persuades an agent to run a credential or multi-factor reset, and then uses adversary-in-the-middle infrastructure to capture the authenticated session token rather than the password itself. Sign-ins come through residential proxy addresses so the location looks unremarkable, and the group then works through Microsoft 365 and other connected SaaS collecting data to extort. Arctic Wolf notes overlap with the cluster Mandiant tracks as UNC6671. Schools, colleges and universities are unusually exposed to this shape of attack. Service desks handle enormous reset volumes at term start, often with student workers on the phones, and the caller claiming to be a dean, a principal or a director of finance is exactly the caller an agent is least inclined to challenge. The fix is a verification step nobody is allowed to skip, not a training reminder.

High Phishing

BigBear 2.0 phishing service bypassed MFA at 258 organisations and took over 5,000 Microsoft 365 credentials

Researchers reported on 7 September 2026 that a phishing-as-a-service platform called BigBear 2.0 defeated multi-factor authentication at 258 organisations and harvested more than 5,000 Microsoft 365 credentials. It works by adversary-in-the-middle: the service proxies the genuine Microsoft sign-in, relays whatever challenge the real site issues, and keeps the authenticated session cookie once the user completes it. The victim sees a normal, successful login and has nothing to report. For schools and universities the significance is that defeating MFA is now a purchased service rather than a specialist skill, arriving in the same weeks as term-start credential churn. Codes by text or email and push approvals all sit inside what these kits handle; origin-bound FIDO2 credentials do not, because the browser refuses to release them to a look-alike domain. A stolen staff session in education typically reaches student records, payroll and the finance system, which is why the accounts to harden first are administrative rather than academic.

Medium Research

Unit 42 ran a full enterprise breach in under ten hours using autonomous AI agents and no zero-days

Palo Alto Networks’ Unit 42 published research describing an end-to-end enterprise compromise completed in under ten hours, in which AI agents automated more than 50 MITRE ATT&CK techniques from initial access through to objective — without a single zero-day. Everything used was ordinary: known vulnerabilities, credential reuse, misconfiguration and living-off-the-land tooling. The finding worth carrying into an education context is about tempo, not about artificial intelligence as such. A school district or a mid-sized university typically detects an intrusion in days and responds over a working week, on a team whose on-call rota is one person and whose out-of-hours escalation is a mobile number. An adversary operating at machine speed compresses the window between initial access and impact to less than a single shift, which means controls that fail closed matter far more than controls that page a human. In practice that favours enforced MFA over alerting on suspicious logins, immutable and tested backups over backup monitoring, and automatic containment of an endpoint over a triage queue. It also raises the value of the boring inventory work, because everything in this chain was a known exposure that somebody had not got to yet.

High Threat Intel

Session-cookie theft against authenticated AI-platform sessions — the MFA bypass aimed at the tools students and staff adopted fastest

Researchers this week described attackers stealing session cookies from authenticated AI-platform sessions in order to ride an existing login rather than defeat the login itself. The mechanism is not new — a stolen session token reproduces an already-authenticated state, so multi-factor authentication has already been satisfied and is never re-tested — but the target selection is. AI assistants and chat platforms went from novelty to daily use across teaching, administration and research faster than almost any category of software in recent memory, usually without a procurement review, frequently signed in with an institutional Google or Microsoft account, and often from a personal browser profile that also holds the user’s school session. That last detail is the education-specific risk: an infostealer on a staff member’s home laptop, or a student’s, does not distinguish between the cookie for an AI tool and the cookie for the SIS. The exposure is also worse in higher education, where departmental adoption of AI tooling is genuinely uncontrolled and the same browser is used for research data, email and administrative systems. Session-token theft is why “we have MFA” is no longer a complete answer.

Education is a soft fit for this by design. The BYOD step is the clever part: routing the victim to an unmanaged personal device sidesteps conditional-access device compliance and endpoint controls entirely, and the Graph API traffic that follows looks like ordinary Microsoft 365 usage.
