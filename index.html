Top Story

The Shai-Hulud npm worm is still spreading and now hunts 469 separate credential locations — 400+ packages, 1,300+ versions and roughly 2 billion monthly downloads in its blast radius

The self-propagating npm worm known as Shai-Hulud remains the single broadest live exposure in enterprise IT. The current wave began at 09:00 UTC on 4 August 2026, when a compromised maintainer identity was used to publish malicious versions of keyv 6.0.0, cacheable-request 13.0.20, cache-manager 7.2.10, flat-cache, file-entry-cache and hundreds of others. Wiz counted over 400 distinct packages; Singapore’s CSA put the figure at more than 1,300 affected versions representing roughly two billion monthly downloads, and JFrog later tracked the campaign across 1,700+ versions. The worm is not a vulnerability — nothing was flawed, packages were simply replaced — and that is why there is no CVE and no patch. It runs from a preinstall lifecycle hook, executes an obfuscated payload under the Bun runtime, harvests npm tokens, GitHub PATs, AWS/Azure/GCP keys, Kubernetes secrets and AI service keys, then uses any writable publishing token to push infected patch releases of every package that identity can reach. It also plants execution hooks in repository files such as .vscode/tasks.json and Claude Code hook configurations, and injects workflows to steal GitHub Actions secrets. Command-and-control addresses are pulled from an Ethereum smart contract rather than hardcoded, so takedowns of individual domains achieve little. On 3 September GitGuardian reported that the credential-scanning logic had grown from 189 paths to 469 — .env files, shell history, package-manager configs, CLI caches, CI/CD systems, IDE settings and AI tool configs. That is the real lesson: the operators are not breaking trust relationships, they are collecting the credentials already lying around in build environments and riding them onward.

Wordfence blocked more than 440,000 exploit attempts against two WordPress plugins in a file-upload campaign now running at scale

Two unauthenticated file-upload flaws in widely installed WordPress plugins are under sustained attack. CVE-2026-14894 in Super Forms, rated CVSS 9.8, is a missing file-type validation issue: attackers post Base64-encoded PHP to /wp-admin/admin-ajax.php through the super_submit_form endpoint and drop a web shell. Exploitation began on 14 July 2026 and peaked on 18 August at more than 40,000 requests a day, with Wordfence blocking over 250,000 attempts in total; the fix is Super Forms 6.3.314. CVE-2026-32475 in Elementor Pro, disclosed through Patchstack in August and fixed in 4.2.2, is a validation bypass reached by submitting a form’s File Upload field as an array, which lands PHP files under /wp-content/uploads/elementor/forms/. This matters beyond marketing sites: WordPress instances are frequently run by a business unit rather than central IT, on infrastructure that still sits inside the corporate network or shares an identity provider, and a PHP web shell on such a host is a foothold like any other. The two campaigns are opportunistic and internet-wide, so exposure is a function of patch latency alone.

High Incident — Social engineering

Attackers are impersonating IT support over Microsoft Teams external chat, then relaying credentials to domain controllers

A coordinated social-engineering operation tracked as Spring Ring used external Microsoft Teams accounts to contact more than 150 employees across at least ten companies, working from 26 distinct attacker identities. The pretext is the oldest one in the book — an IT support contact about a problem with the user’s account — but the channel is what makes it work. Teams external collaboration is enabled by default in many tenants, the message arrives inside a sanctioned corporate tool rather than in email, and there is no attachment or link for a gateway to inspect. In the observed intrusions the operator talks the user into granting a remote session, then silently installs a malicious MSI package that carries Node.js and JavaScript implants for persistent command execution, and moves laterally toward domain controllers. Some chains escalated from voice phishing into NTLM relay attacks against the domain controller directly. There is no vulnerability here and nothing to patch: the whole sequence is legitimate software driven by a person who was persuaded. The practical controls are configuration and process rather than detection — restricting who can initiate external Teams chat, publishing a verification procedure staff can use when someone claims to be IT, and alerting on unexpected MSI installation and new remote-access tooling on user endpoints.

High Intel

A backdoor called “ted” hides inside the victim’s own recompiled HAProxy binary and reports the same version string as a clean build

Rapid7 Labs documented a previously undescribed Linux toolkit it calls ted, found embedded directly into trojanised HAProxy load balancers at two South Korean organisations in the automotive and media sectors. Rather than running as a separate process or module, the implant is compiled into HAProxy itself. It watches for requests to specific image paths to switch into command-and-control mode, erases its own commands from HAProxy’s connection counters, and filters visitors on User-Agent, URL pattern, referer and an operator key — only requests that clear all four checks receive a modified page. Everything else gets normal traffic. Rapid7 attributes the toolkit with medium confidence to North Korean state-sponsored activity based on overlaps with APT37, Lazarus and Kimsuky tradecraft, and suspects initial access through exposed Korean groupware portals. The detection problem is the reason this belongs on every enterprise’s radar regardless of geography: a recompiled HAProxy reports the same version string as a clean build, so package version checks, vulnerability scanners and inventory tools all report a healthy system. Rapid7 recommends independent network correlation, memory behavioural analysis and binary integrity checking, and has published six domains, file paths and two SHA-256 hashes for retrospective log review.

Sophos’ Counter Threat Unit released an analysis of the ransomware-as-a-service operation known as The Gentlemen, which it tracks as GOLD SHERWOOD. EU Cyber Resilience Act reporting obligations start on 11 September — 24 hours to tell ENISA about an actively exploited vulnerability in your product

Article 14 of the EU Cyber Resilience Act takes effect on 11 September 2026, and it applies to any manufacturer placing a product with digital elements on the EU market, whether or not they are established in the EU. Two triggers start the clock: becoming aware that a vulnerability in your product is being actively exploited, or discovering a severe incident affecting the product’s security. The deadlines are an early warning to ENISA and the relevant national CSIRT within 24 hours, a fuller notification within 72 hours, and a final report within 14 days for vulnerabilities or one month for severe incidents. Reporting goes through the ENISA Single Reporting Platform, which currently requires manual form completion with no API. Several scope details matter in practice: the obligation continues after a product is no longer supported, vulnerabilities in third-party components must be reported if they are exploitable within your product, there is no retroactive reporting for anything discovered before 11 September, and users must be notified with disclosure proportional to risk. Exemptions cover medical devices, defence products and SaaS. Penalties reach €15 million or 2.5% of global annual turnover, whichever is higher, plus possible recalls. If your organisation ships software, firmware, an app or a connected device into Europe, the practical question this week is who holds the pager for a 24-hour regulatory clock.

CISA still expects to finalise the CIRCIA incident-reporting rule this month — 72 hours for incidents, 24 hours for ransom payments

CISA has targeted September 2026 for publication of the final rule implementing the Cyber Incident Reporting for Critical Infrastructure Act of 2022. As proposed, covered entities in critical-infrastructure sectors would have to report substantial cyber incidents to CISA within 72 hours and any ransom payment within 24 hours. The rule has slipped repeatedly: the statutory deadline was October 2025, CISA’s own prior internal target was May 2026, and the agency held town-hall consultations in June 2026 with more than 1,200 participants before settling on the current timeline. Final thresholds for who counts as a covered entity — based on sector and size criteria set out in the April 2024 proposed rule — remain pending, which is precisely the detail most organisations need in order to know whether they are in scope. Carried here as still-current rather than new: the September target was set in July and had not been superseded as of this edition. The reason to prepare regardless of the publication date is that a 72-hour clock is short if nobody has decided in advance who declares an incident reportable, who writes the report and who signs it. Organisations that already report under NIS2, DORA, SEC disclosure rules or state regulations should be mapping CIRCIA against those obligations now rather than building a separate process.
