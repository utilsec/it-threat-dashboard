Top Story

Arctic Wolf confirms the PaperCut intrusions are landing on schools and universities — and the attackers are there for credentials, not printing

The PaperCut story stopped being a generic advisory this week. On 5 September, Arctic Wolf’s Adversary Research team published an analysis of intrusions it observed against education organisations in the United States and Europe, ranging from K-12 schools to major universities, in which attackers chained CVE-2026-81578 (an improper access control flaw that lets an unauthenticated attacker change system configuration) with CVE-2026-82078 (unsafe dynamic class loading in the database connection utilities, allowing arbitrary Java bytecode execution) in PaperCut NG and MF. Huntress researcher John Hammond described the chain as a “point and shoot” full compromise. What happened after the exploit is the part that matters. Arctic Wolf describes ordinary reconnaissance first — whoami, tasklist, ver, uname — then the creation of a privileged local account named Administrator17, then credential harvesting: lsa_collect.exe and lsa_collect_small.exe variants and save_hives.exe dumping Windows registry hives, with registry key extraction aimed at reconstructing the BootKey and reaching the SAM database. The attackers also ran findstr against PaperCut’s own configuration files looking for the strings password, secret, ldap, bind and token, and deployed a Meterpreter Java payload. Two addresses were named in the reporting: 45.142.193[.]132 for tool delivery and 194.180.48[.]134 for the Meterpreter session. The timeline is tight. PaperCut published its bulletin on 27 August covering versions 24, 25 and 26; an emergency patch followed on 28 August and had to be followed the same day by a second build after watchTowr found multiple patch bypasses and a further authentication bypass. CISA added the two flaws to the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog in the last days of August, with federal remediation deadlines of 11 September for CVE-2026-81578 and 14 September for CVE-2026-82078. Shadowserver counted around 204 exposed instances at risk of remote code execution at the start of this week, roughly 60 of them in the United States, with confirmed cases reported in Denmark and Ireland alongside the US activity; Cybersecurity Dive named education, healthcare and construction as the sectors seen so far. Read the target selection plainly rather than as coincidence. Print management is one of the few software categories where schools, colleges and universities are the core market rather than an afterthought; PaperCut servers are usually domain-joined because they enforce per-student quotas and charge accounts against directory services; and they are frequently owned by a print or desktop team rather than by security. That is a credential store sitting in a low-attention part of the estate, and the LDAP bind details in its configuration files are the reason someone bothered.

Patched 28 Aug 2026 — the first emergency build was bypassed and replaced the same day; confirm you are on the second build, not the first Action: Treat every PaperCut NG/MF server as a credential-exposure incident until you have checked it, not as a patching ticket. Education leak-site postings did not pause for the start of term — three more in the first days of September, and not one of them is confirmed

Breach-monitoring firm Breachsense logged three education victims posted to extortion sites in the opening days of September: Newton County School System in Georgia (Qilin, 1 September), Westfield Public School District in New Jersey (INC Ransom, 3 September) and Heilbronn University of Applied Sciences in Germany (an actor listed as Panzer, 4 September). They follow a late-August run that included Nottingham Trent University in the UK, posted on 26 August by an actor calling itself ShadowByt3$. Read every one of them the same way. Each is a claim published by the attacker on its own leak site, not a disclosure by the institution: none has published a breach notice, all four list the leak size as unknown, and none has drawn national coverage. Leak-site claims are sometimes inflated, occasionally recycled from an older intrusion, and very occasionally invented outright. For scale, Breachsense recorded 225 organisations posted across all sectors during September so far, so education is not being singled out in the raw counts. What the cluster does say is directional: mid-tier districts and small-to-mid public universities — the ones with a two- or three-person IT team and no dedicated security staff — are being worked steadily by mid-tier affiliates on both sides of the Atlantic, and the rate did not dip for the start of the academic year. One detail worth noting is that Panzer, the actor listed against Heilbronn, is the same double-extortion crew researchers described this week as having posted 16 victims across 11 countries — a new brand, not a rebrand of a household name, which is consistent with the pattern of newer affiliates finding education an easy first market. The actionable half is credential exposure: Breachsense indexes several hundred institutional accounts per school appearing in third-party breaches and infostealer logs, and institutional logins with plaintext passwords in circulation remain the ordinary way these intrusions start.

The critical Citrix NetScaler authentication bypass is now being attacked — and NetScaler is the campus VPN and virtual-lab gateway

CVE-2026-19490 is a critical authentication bypass in NetScaler ADC and NetScaler Gateway that affects appliances configured as an AAA virtual server or as a Gateway — that is, SSL VPN, ICA Proxy, CVPN or RDP Proxy, which between them describe nearly every way a university publishes remote desktops, virtual labs and off-campus access. Citrix disclosed and patched it on 19 August without flagging exploitation. That changed at the start of this month. On 3 September, sensors operated by Previdian recorded requests matching the public proof-of-concept from three distinct source addresses geolocated to Australia, the United States and Germany, per researcher Ryan Dewhurst; Belgium’s national cybersecurity centre, NCC-BE, issued its own warning. Shadowserver tracks more than 22,000 NetScaler ADC appliances and close to 1,700 Gateway instances exposed to the internet, though it cannot see which of those are patched or in a vulnerable configuration. For education the exposure profile is unusually bad in one specific way: a campus NetScaler is deliberately open to the whole world because students and staff connect from anywhere, it fronts single sign-on, and an authentication bypass in front of an SSO estate is an identity problem rather than a network problem. If your appliance was reachable and unpatched at any point since the proof-of-concept became public, the honest posture is to assume session and credential compromise and work backwards from there.

High Active Exploitation

MikroTik routers are being taken over through exposed SSH — the budget edge device behind a lot of small districts and student housing

CERT Polska warned that MikroTik RouterOS devices have been under active takeover since 2 September through their exposed SSH service, with reporting through 5 September describing continuing activity. MikroTik hardware occupies a specific niche in education: it is what a small district, a rural school site, a charter network or a student-housing block buys when the budget will not stretch to an enterprise firewall, and it is frequently installed by a contractor, configured once, and then never logged into again. That combination — internet-reachable management, default or reused credentials, and firmware years behind current — is exactly the profile being harvested. Compromised edge routers are rarely the end goal. They become relay infrastructure, traffic interception points and a persistent foothold inside a network segment that nobody monitors, which is a particularly poor thing to have sitting between a school site and the district core. Treat this as an inventory problem first: most organisations that own MikroTik devices do not have a complete list of them, because they arrived through site projects rather than through the network refresh.

Medium Incident

More than 14,500 Dahua cameras compromised in a campaign building attack infrastructure — and campus camera fleets are counted in the thousands

Researchers reported a large-scale campaign that has compromised over 14,500 Dahua network cameras, using them to build attack infrastructure rather than to spy on what the cameras see. That distinction matters for how you triage it. The value of a compromised camera to this kind of operation is its address, its uptime and its position inside a network, not its video; the devices become relays, scanners and staging points. Education carries an outsized share of this exposure because a single district or campus commonly runs hundreds to thousands of cameras across school buildings, car parks, athletic facilities and residence halls, purchased over a decade through different capital projects, often installed and maintained by a physical-security contractor rather than by IT, and frequently sitting on a flat VLAN with routable access to administrative systems. The firmware is rarely patched because nobody owns patching it, and the web interfaces are sometimes deliberately published so that a school resource officer or an athletics director can view a feed from home. No education organisation has been named in this campaign; the point is the asset class, and the fact that most institutions cannot currently produce an accurate count of the cameras on their own network.

K-12 ransomware fell in the first half of 2026 while higher education rose — and two thirds of the attacks were never confirmed by anyone

Comparitech’s education ransomware report for the first half of 2026, the most recent sector-wide dataset available, found K-12 attacks down 26 per cent worldwide against the second half of 2025 while higher education rose by more than 8 per cent. In the United States, 34 attacks were recorded on educational institutions, a 44 per cent decline from 61 in the preceding six months; the US accounted for 33 per cent of the 104 education-sector attacks recorded globally. Elsewhere the direction reversed: the United Kingdom recorded 13 attacks, a 225 per cent increase, with Brazil up 33 per cent to eight and Thailand up 150 per cent to five. The number to sit with is that of those 104 global attacks, only 36 were confirmed by the institution itself — the rest rest on attacker claims. That ratio is the single most useful thing in the report for anyone reading leak-site counts, including the ones on this page: roughly two thirds of what circulates as an education ransomware statistic has never been acknowledged by the organisation named. Read the trend lines with that caveat attached, and read the divergence between K-12 and higher education as a shift in where affiliates believe the payment is, not as evidence that schools have become harder targets.

Medium Research

Unit 42 ran a full enterprise breach in under ten hours using autonomous AI agents and no zero-days

Palo Alto Networks’ Unit 42 published research describing an end-to-end enterprise compromise completed in under ten hours, in which AI agents automated more than 50 MITRE ATT&CK techniques from initial access through to objective — without a single zero-day. Everything used was ordinary: known vulnerabilities, credential reuse, misconfiguration and living-off-the-land tooling. The finding worth carrying into an education context is about tempo, not about artificial intelligence as such. A school district or a mid-sized university typically detects an intrusion in days and responds over a working week, on a team whose on-call rota is one person and whose out-of-hours escalation is a mobile number. An adversary operating at machine speed compresses the window between initial access and impact to less than a single shift, which means controls that fail closed matter far more than controls that page a human. In practice that favours enforced MFA over alerting on suspicious logins, immutable and tested backups over backup monitoring, and automatic containment of an endpoint over a triage queue. It also raises the value of the boring inventory work, because everything in this chain was a known exposure that somebody had not got to yet.

High Threat Intel

Session-cookie theft against authenticated AI-platform sessions — the MFA bypass aimed at the tools students and staff adopted fastest

Researchers this week described attackers stealing session cookies from authenticated AI-platform sessions in order to ride an existing login rather than defeat the login itself. The mechanism is not new — a stolen session token reproduces an already-authenticated state, so multi-factor authentication has already been satisfied and is never re-tested — but the target selection is. AI assistants and chat platforms went from novelty to daily use across teaching, administration and research faster than almost any category of software in recent memory, usually without a procurement review, frequently signed in with an institutional Google or Microsoft account, and often from a personal browser profile that also holds the user’s school session. That last detail is the education-specific risk: an infostealer on a staff member’s home laptop, or a student’s, does not distinguish between the cookie for an AI tool and the cookie for the SIS. The exposure is also worse in higher education, where departmental adoption of AI tooling is genuinely uncontrolled and the same browser is used for research data, email and administrative systems. Session-token theft is why “we have MFA” is no longer a complete answer.

Medium Research

Rapid7 puts exploited high and critical vulnerabilities up 105 per cent — and the interval from disclosure to attack keeps shrinking

Rapid7’s 2026 Global Threat Landscape report records a 105 per cent increase in high and critical vulnerabilities observed being exploited, alongside a continuing collapse in the time between public disclosure and the first attacks. Both figures describe a change in the operating assumption rather than a new threat. Education patching cadence was built around a different world: firmware and server maintenance clustered into the summer break and the winter holiday, curriculum systems frozen during term, and change control designed to protect teaching continuity rather than to move quickly. Three items on this page — PaperCut, NetScaler and MikroTik — went from disclosure to observed exploitation in days or weeks, and all three landed inside the first fortnight of the academic year, when nobody in school or campus IT has spare capacity. The practical conclusion is not to patch everything faster, which no education IT team can staff. It is to define a small, explicitly named set of internet-facing and identity-adjacent systems — the VPN and gateway, the identity provider, the mail edge, the SIS and LMS front doors, the print and file servers reachable from outside — that are exempt from the term-time change freeze, and to agree that exemption with academic leadership in advance rather than during an incident.

HPE patched an unauthenticated RCE in ArubaOS-CX — the switch operating system running a large share of campus networks

CVE-2026-73749 is a buffer overflow in ArubaOS-CX that allows an unauthenticated remote attacker to execute code on the switch. HPE published fixed builds on 3 September: upgrade 10.18.0001 to 10.18.1002 or later, 10.17.1021 and earlier to 10.17.1030 or later, 10.16.1051 and earlier to 10.16.1060 or later, 10.13.1180 and earlier to 10.13.1190 or later, and 10.10.1180 and earlier to 10.10.1181 or later. The same bulletin carries a further 23 issues rated between 8.1 and 8.8, tracked as CVE-2026-73750 through CVE-2026-73753 and CVE-2026-73777 through CVE-2026-73782 among others. HPE stated that at publication it was not aware of active exploitation or of a public proof-of-concept, and urges upgrading. Two things make this awkward for education specifically. AOS-CX is core and access switching for a very large number of universities and school districts, and switch firmware in this sector typically moves on an annual or summer-break cadence — a window that closed a fortnight ago, which means the honest answer to “when can we take a maintenance outage on the core?” is often May. The second is that campus switch management interfaces are frequently reachable from the general staff network rather than from an isolated out-of-band VLAN, because that was easier when the network was built and nobody has revisited it since. Unauthenticated remote code execution on a switch is control of the segment and of the traffic crossing it, so the compensating control worth acting on today — before the firmware plan — is the reachability of the management plane.

Chrome V8 zero-day exploited in the wild — the sixth this year, on the browser that is the whole endpoint in most classrooms

Google patched CVE-2026-85046, a type-confusion flaw in the V8 JavaScript and WebAssembly engine, and acknowledged that an exploit for it exists in the wild. Type confusion means the engine treats one object type as another, corrupting memory in a way that a crafted page can steer toward code execution. Fixed builds are 152.0.7977.82 and .83; Google withheld technical detail to buy patching time, as it usually does while an exploit is circulating. This is the sixth actively exploited Chrome flaw fixed during 2026. The education angle is scale rather than novelty. In K-12 in particular the browser is not one application among many — it is the entire student computing environment, and a managed Chromebook fleet numbering in the thousands per district updates on a channel and a reboot cadence that IT controls only loosely, because the device belongs to a student who may not restart it for weeks. Verify enforcement rather than assuming it: set the minimum version policy in the admin console, pull the reported version distribution across the fleet, and identify the tail of devices that has not taken the update. On the staff side the same check applies to Chrome and to Chromium-based Edge on managed laptops, and to the personal machines that reach institutional systems through a browser session.

An internet-facing SSRF rated 10.0 on an appliance nobody owns is close to a worst case. The check is quick: confirm whether an SMA 1000 exists in your estate at all, and whether its address is still resolving and reachable.

Seven additions to the KEV catalog on 2 September — including the phone system and the artifact repository

CISA added seven entries to the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog on 2 September. Alongside the two SonicWall SMA 1000 flaws, the batch covers CVE-2026-9586, SQL injection in Sangoma Switchvox rated 9.3; CVE-2026-82329, improper authentication in JFrog Artifactory rated 9.8, reported in use to obtain reverse shells and administrative tokens; CVE-2026-48710, request smuggling in Kludex Starlette rated 6.5 and chained with CVE-2026-42271; CVE-2026-49869, command injection in Kestra OSS rated 10.0 and used to deploy reverse shells and cryptocurrency mining; and CVE-2026-59822, improper authentication in Berri LiteLLM rated 8.8 in its Model Context Protocol handling. Three of those land in education more often than the vendor names suggest. Switchvox is an unglamorous but common choice for school and small-college telephony, and a phone system holds directory data and sits on a network segment that predates most of your controls. Artifactory and Kestra belong to university research computing, computer science departments and central development teams — environments that are frequently self-managed by academic staff, outside the central patch process, and treated as non-production right up until someone lands an administrative token in them. LiteLLM is part of the AI tooling that departments have been standing up on their own this year. The uncomfortable question for a campus is not whether these are patched but whether central IT even knows they exist.

High Advisory

An Exchange authentication bypass with roughly 22,000 unpatched servers still on the internet — and hybrid Exchange is an education speciality

Microsoft addressed CVE-2026-62911, an authentication bypass in Exchange Server rated CVSS 8.0, with reporting on 3 September putting roughly 22,000 unpatched internet-facing servers still online. No confirmed in-the-wild exploitation had been reported at the time of writing, which is the one piece of good news here. The reason this belongs on an education page rather than only on the main dashboard is the shape of the estate. Schools, colleges and universities moved to cloud mail earlier and more completely than most sectors, but a very large number kept a hybrid Exchange server running to manage recipients, to relay from the SIS, the library system, the bell scheduler, the print server and the assorted appliances that can only speak unauthenticated SMTP, or simply because the migration was declared finished before the last server was retired. That server is domain-joined, internet-facing, has extensive Active Directory permissions by design, and is very often on an update cadence that nobody has reviewed since the migration project closed. An authentication bypass on a box like that is a directory problem, not a mail problem. If you cannot immediately say which build your hybrid server is on, that is the finding.

The Education Department’s annual FERPA and PPRA letter shifts survey collection from opt-out to written parental consent

The U.S. Department of Education issued its annual Dear Colleague letter on FERPA and the Protection of Pupil Rights Amendment on 31 August, alongside new non-binding guidance on the responsible use of education technology in the classroom. This year’s letter leans considerably harder on PPRA than previous editions. The change districts will feel is the consent model: schools are directed to obtain written parental permission before students complete surveys that request information in the eight protected categories, rather than relying on the opt-out notice most districts currently send at the start of the year. The letter also addresses behavioural health screening instruments, required notification of parental rights, third-party edtech applications and policies on personal device use. The companion ed-tech guidance asks districts to judge tools by whether they address a specific learning challenge and produce evidence of improved outcomes, and to discontinue those that do not — a framing that gives IT and data-privacy staff an unusually direct lever for retiring applications. This is a privacy and process obligation rather than a security control, but it lands on the same people: the data-privacy officer, the assessment team and whoever maintains the district’s edtech application inventory. Read the letter before the first survey or screener of the year goes out, because the opt-out notice you already sent may not carry.

CISA’s free K-12 guides are the baseline to measure against — and they arrived while districts were losing other free support

CISA released two free K-12 cybersecurity resources: one aimed at school leaders building a cybersecurity programme from a standing start, and one for cybersecurity leaders trying to sustain a programme already in place. The recommendations are deliberately unexotic — protect student and staff login credentials, safeguard devices, perform and test backups, write and rehearse an incident response plan, run training and awareness, protect sensitive data, adopt CISA’s Cross-Sector Cybersecurity Performance Goals and build a longer-term plan against the NIST Cybersecurity Framework. CISA frames schools as attractive targets partly because so much about them is a matter of public record. The supporting figures are worth keeping: more than 1,300 disclosed school cybersecurity incidents between 2018 and 2021, and 34 ransomware attacks on US K-12 and higher education institutions in the first half of 2026 of which only 12 were confirmed. One cited example, a Texas district breached in March 2026, affected 26,629 individuals and took the district’s systems down for five days. The value of these guides for a small district is less the content than the citation: they give a technology director something authoritative to put in front of a board that has been asked for money.

Medium Policy

A year on from the MS-ISAC defunding, the replacement bill is still pending — and K12 SIX has stepped into part of the gap

Federal support for the Multi-State Information Sharing and Analysis Center ended in September 2025, when CISA declined to renew its cooperative agreement with the Center for Internet Security. MS-ISAC had served roughly 18,000 state, local, tribal and territorial organisations, school districts prominently among them, and the services that disappeared — free monitoring, advisories and incident support — were in many cases the entire security programme of a small district. The organisation now runs on a fee-based membership model backed by around $1 million a month from CIS. Senator Mark Warner’s Guaranteeing Universal Access to Cybersecurity Act, introduced on 5 June, would direct CISA to re-enter the agreement, require outreach to win back lost members, and authorise $50 million a year from fiscal 2027 — roughly double the $27 million Congress appropriated in 2025. It has not passed. In the meantime the sector-specific gap narrowed slightly from another direction: K12 SIX was admitted to membership of the National Council of ISACs on 30 July, which formally connects K-12 to critical-infrastructure threat intelligence sharing. Neither development changes what a district should do this term, which is to stop assuming the free feed is coming back and name a replacement in the budget.
