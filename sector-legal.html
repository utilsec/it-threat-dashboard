Top Story

The Thomson Reuters C-Track court breach moves from vendor disclosure to court-by-court confirmation — Kentucky joins North Dakota, and the states are now coordinating a shared notification problem

Nothing published in the past few days displaces this as the story legal IT has to work. Thomson Reuters disclosed on 2–3 September 2026 that an unauthorised party obtained files belonging to C-Track, the court case-management platform it supplies to appellate and trial courts. Access began around March 2026 and was not discovered until 30 June; affected courts and Ontario’s Ministry of the Attorney General were notified in late July, with public disclosure following more than two months later. Reporting names courts across roughly a dozen US jurisdictions — Alabama, Kentucky, Montana, Nevada, New Hampshire, North Dakota, Ohio, Pennsylvania, South Carolina, Tennessee and Wyoming, plus the US Virgin Islands — alongside Ontario’s Court of Appeal, Superior Court of Justice and Court of Justice. Individual outlets differ on the exact jurisdiction list, so treat any single roll-call as provisional until your own court confirms. The exposed material is names combined with one or more of Social Security numbers, driver’s licence numbers, dates of birth, medical information and health-insurance information. Thomson Reuters has also said that confidential, redacted or sealed court information may have been affected at some courts, which is the detail separating this from an ordinary vendor breach: sealed records exist because a court ordered them withheld, and juvenile matters, protective orders, victim identities and cooperating-witness material all sit behind that seal. What changed in the last 48 hours is the shape of the response. On 4 September the Kentucky Administrative Office of the Courts confirmed that data from the Kentucky Supreme Court and Court of Appeals was involved, said its appellate courts were not operationally disrupted, and stated it has no indication so far that the intruder shared or distributed Kentucky’s data — while noting that Thomson Reuters is still reviewing systems court by court to establish whose records were exposed and that Kentucky is coordinating with other affected states through the National Center for State Courts. Kentucky also made the useful distinction that its trial court e-filing does not run through a third-party vendor and was unaffected. North Dakota’s Supreme Court had confirmed its own exposure a day earlier, with a criminal investigation under way. Still undisclosed by anyone: an affected-individual count, how the intruder got in, and who was responsible. Twelve months of credit monitoring is on offer. For every firm that litigates in these jurisdictions, the question this week is not whether your network held, but which of your clients’ identifiers and sealed filings sat in a vendor database you never contracted with directly — and the answer is arriving court by court rather than all at once, so the docket query you run today will need repeating.

Critical Breach

The courts, not the vendor, now own the notification problem — and they are working it one jurisdiction at a time through the National Center for State Courts

The downstream half of the Thomson Reuters disclosure is the part legal IT has to work, and it is now visibly under way. The North Dakota Supreme Court acknowledged on 3 September that data associated with the court was affected through C-Track and that a criminal investigation is running. On 4 September the Kentucky Administrative Office of the Courts confirmed exposure covering the state’s Supreme Court and Court of Appeals, said appellate operations continued normally, and disclosed that it is coordinating with other affected states through the National Center for State Courts while Thomson Reuters reviews systems court by court. Kentucky added that no individual count is yet established and that its trial court e-filing, which does not use a third-party vendor, was untouched. The shape of this incident is the awkward one. The intrusion happened inside the vendor’s cloud environment, the data taken was backup database copies handed over for troubleshooting, and the courts had no visibility into any of it — yet the notification duty, the public statements and the standing with litigants all sit with the courts. Roughly six months passed between the files being taken and the public learning of it. For firms, three things follow. First, matter-level exposure: pull the docket for every case filed in the named jurisdictions before 30 June 2026 and identify which clients’ identifiers went into the case-management system — and expect to repeat it, because the jurisdiction list has already shifted between reports. Second, sealed material: if a court tells you sealed or redacted content may have been affected, that changes what you can represent to a client about the confidentiality of a filing and may warrant a motion. Third, third-party risk generally: the diligence question that would have caught this is not “is the vendor certified” but “where do production copies of our data go when support needs to reproduce a bug, and who is watching those copies.”

IDScan faces class actions over a claimed 153 million driver’s licence scans, with the FBI investigating and plaintiffs’ firms already filing

Identity-verification vendor IDScan is being sued over an alleged breach that reporting describes as covering roughly 153 million US and Canadian driver’s licence scans, about 10 million ID cards, 3 million travel documents and some 579,000 medical cards. The data surfaced through a dark-web service calling itself Nexus, which advertised lookups against the database; journalist Brian Krebs verified the material traced back to IDScan. Nexus is no longer reachable, but the underlying database is understood to remain in criminal hands. The FBI’s New Orleans field office is investigating, and complaints and class-action investigations have been announced by plaintiffs’ firms including Markovits, Stock & DeMarco and Hall Attorneys, with Louisiana — where IDScan is based — as the venue. IDScan had not commented publicly at the time of reporting. There are two distinct reasons this belongs on a professional-services page. The obvious one is defence-side and plaintiff-side workload: a claimed victim pool of this size is a mass-tort-shaped event, and firms on either side of it should expect intake volume, consolidation motions and preservation demands. The less obvious one is that identity-scanning vendors are embedded in visitor management, client onboarding and know-your-client workflows at plenty of firms and their clients, often through a reseller. If your reception desk scans a driver’s licence to issue a visitor badge, you should establish which vendor sits behind that scanner, what it retains and for how long. The allegations against IDScan are unproven.

High Supply Chain

A software vendor left its own TeamCity unpatched and lost customer AWS credentials and source code — the cleanest current example of the vendor-side risk C-Track also illustrates

JetBrains disclosed that attackers breached Cadence, its cloud service for running machine-learning workloads from PyCharm, by exploiting CVE-2026-63077 — a deserialization flaw in JetBrains TeamCity, rated CVSS 9.8, permitting unauthenticated remote code execution. The company’s own TeamCity server had not been patched. The intrusion ran from 8 to 24 August 2026 and was discovered on 23 August. Taken were usernames, real names, email addresses, login timestamps and IP addresses; a full 2024 server backup containing credentials and configurations; AWS IAM credentials belonging to both JetBrains staff and users; contents of S3 buckets in JetBrains AWS accounts; and source code synchronised from users’ PyCharm projects. JetBrains told users to revoke or rotate every credential and secret immediately and to treat all Cadence executions as potentially untrusted. It acknowledged the server should have been patched and has not explained the delay. Most law firms do not use Cadence. The reason this sits on a professional-services page is the pattern rather than the product, and it is the same pattern as the top story: a vendor holds a copy of your material — a backup, a support export, a synchronised project — and your exposure is decided entirely by that vendor’s patching and retention discipline, which you cannot see. Consultancies, legal-technology teams and e-discovery groups inside larger professional-services organisations do run build and CI systems of exactly this kind, and the 2024 backup detail is the one to sit with: a credential you rotated in 2025 may still be live inside a backup someone else is holding.

High Campaign

More than 5,400 hacked WordPress and PrestaShop sites are serving fake-CAPTCHA lures whose payloads live in a blockchain smart contract

Netskope published analysis on 5 September 2026 of a large ClickFix operation running from over 5,400 compromised websites, mostly WordPress and PrestaShop installations. The delivery mechanism is the notable part. Rather than hosting the malicious script on infrastructure that can be seized, the operators use a technique known as EtherHiding: the payload is stored in a smart contract on the BNB Smart Chain testnet, and an injected script on the hacked site fetches it at page load. There is no server to take down and no domain to sinkhole. Netskope observed roughly 300 to 400 infected sites contacting BNB Smart Chain endpoints daily through August, peaking at 536 in a single day. The lure itself is the now-familiar fake CAPTCHA: the visitor is told to prove they are human by pasting a command into the Windows Run box, which executes PowerShell under their own account. A later variant drops a WebRTC data-channel stager that establishes an encrypted covert channel to the operators’ command-and-control. Two things make this a legal-sector item rather than general noise. The first is that fee earners spend their day on unfamiliar third-party websites — court sites, county recorders, expert directories, opposing-party sites, foreign registries — and a compromised WordPress site is indistinguishable from a working one. The second is that many firms run their own marketing site on WordPress, so the same weakness that makes a site a delivery vehicle for someone else applies to yours. ClickFix defeats email security entirely because nothing arrives by email; the user is talked into running the code themselves.

Medium Research

Microsoft documents a phishing operation hiding invisible Unicode inside financial words to slip past keyword filters — a direct problem for billing and finance inboxes

Microsoft Security Research published analysis on 4 September 2026 of a high-volume phishing operation using ASCII smuggling: invisible Unicode Tag characters in the U+E0000 to U+E007F range inserted inside otherwise ordinary words. A message about “funding” is written with a hidden code point between the letters, so it renders normally to a human reader but no longer matches a filter looking for the literal string. Fortra researchers described the underlying technique in September 2025; what Microsoft adds is the scale. Between February and May 2026 the campaign sent between roughly one and 2.37 million messages on weekday peaks, dropping to near silence at weekends — a business-hours rhythm chosen to blend into normal mail flow. The lures were finance-themed, imitating business loans, credit lines and funding advances, with sender domains including guardiangrowthfunding[.]com, digitalcapitalboost[.]com and thebusinessloanexpress[.]com, and a substantial part of the targeting aimed at Small Business Administration loan applicants. The professional-services relevance is not exotic. Finance-themed lures land in the accounts, billing and practice-management inboxes that already process payment instructions daily, and those are the same inboxes involved in most invoice-fraud and completion-monies incidents in this sector. If your mail security depends meaningfully on keyword or phrase matching — and many DLP and business-email-compromise rules do — this technique defeats it silently and produces no alert to tell you so. The fix is normalisation: strip or flag invisible Unicode before rules run, and treat unusual density of these characters as suspicious in itself.

High Campaign

Still the sector’s defining campaign: Silent Ransom Group works law firms by phone, and sometimes by walking through the front door

Carried forward and re-checked this run, because nothing published in the past fortnight supersedes it — and in a week with no new law-firm incident disclosed, this remains the most likely way your firm actually gets hit. The group tracked as Silent Ransom Group, Luna Moth, Chatty Spider and UNC3753 has been active since 2022 and targeting law firms since spring 2023, and both an FBI FLASH alert and Google Mandiant reporting describe the campaign as still running. The tradecraft is almost entirely human. Operators call or email posing as IT support, talk a partner, paralegal or billing clerk into a remote-support session, and install a legitimate remote monitoring tool. Exfiltration runs over WinSCP or a hidden or renamed copy of Rclone, with data pushed to Google Drive, Microsoft OneDrive, external hard drives or USB storage. There is frequently no encryption stage at all — the leverage is the client material itself, which in a firm means privileged files that cannot be restored away. The outlier detail, and the one that breaks most legal threat models, is that operators have turned up in person, claiming they need to image a device or take a backup to deal with the effects of a phishing email. Halcyon’s research centre ranked law firms fourth most-targeted industry in early 2026, attributing it to the sensitivity of client data, regulatory pressure to resolve incidents quickly and a perceived willingness to pay. The FBI’s own recommendations are unglamorous and correct: verify visitor identity against ID, deploy phishing-resistant multi-factor authentication, restrict remote access and external-drive permissions on sensitive systems, and train staff to recognise the pretext.

A phishing operation across 46 countries is delivering remote-monitoring installers instead of malware — the same access route already used against law firms

ANY.RUN published analysis on 3 September 2026 of a phishing operation spanning 46 countries with the United States accounting for around 45 per cent of observed activity, tracked across 601 connected cases. The payload is not a novel implant. It is a legitimate remote monitoring and management installer, which gives the operator interactive control of the machine while looking to most endpoint tooling like ordinary IT administration. The lures are chosen to make a document click feel routine: Canada Revenue Agency tax forms, shipping and UPS notices, Adobe PDF prompts, US Social Security Administration letters, tax notices and invoices. Infrastructure discipline is the notable part — 425 kit URLs across 240 hosts, 94 per cent of them observed for only a single day, staged across Vercel, GitHub Pages, Netlify, compromised sites, Amazon S3, Cloudflare R2, DigitalOcean Spaces, Dropbox and GoFile. What holds constant is the fingerprint: shared assets such as a font1.woff2 file and a stable secure.html to project/*.zip delivery structure. The named target sectors are education, technology, government, banking, finance and manufacturing rather than legal specifically, but the technique is the one Silent Ransom Group has been running against law firms by phone for three years, now industrialised over email at scale. Tax-notice and invoice lures land naturally in an accounting or billing inbox. The defence is the same either way: application-control the RMM estate so only the tool you actually deploy can run, and alert on the first execution of anything else.

Critical Advisory

PaperCut attackers are now harvesting credentials and creating their own admin accounts — and print management is one of the few servers almost every firm runs

PaperCut warned on 27 August 2026 that two flaws in its NG and MF print-management products were being exploited, and CISA added both to the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog on 31 August with a federal remediation deadline of 14 September. CVE-2026-82078 and CVE-2026-81578 chain an authentication bypass into remote code execution for an unauthenticated attacker. The escalation this window is in what the intruders do once they are in. Arctic Wolf’s adversary research team published detail on 5 September describing hands-on activity against vulnerable PaperCut servers: discovery commands such as uname, whoami, ver and tasklist; creation of privileged accounts, including one named “Administrator17”; credential harvesting with tooling identified as lsa_collect.exe and save_hives.exe; deployment of Meterpreter Java payloads; and grepping PaperCut configuration files for the strings “password”, “secret”, “ldap” and “token”. The victims Arctic Wolf names are K-12 schools and universities in the US and Europe rather than law firms, but the target selection is opportunistic — internet-exposed PaperCut — not sectoral, and ShadowServer has put more than a thousand instances directly on the internet. PaperCut shipped emergency patches, then a second patch after the first was bypassed, with a consolidated release still pending, so patching once is not evidence of safety. For law and accounting firms the exposure is unusually direct. Print management holds the cost-recovery data binding documents to matters and clients, it sits on the internal network with service accounts that touch directories and file shares, and secure print release means the server has seen a copy of what people printed. That last point is now the operative one: the observed behaviour is specifically credential theft and LDAP-secret hunting, so an exposed PaperCut server is a directory-credential problem, not just a print outage.

Two separate NetScaler flaws are now being exploited, and NetScaler is the remote-access front door for a large share of firms

The Citrix NetScaler situation changed twice inside this window. CVE-2026-8452, a memory-overflow issue that Citrix patched on 30 June and described in terms of unpredictable behaviour and denial of service, turned out to be worse than advertised: watchTowr published proof-of-concept detail on 14 August showing unauthenticated remote code execution, in-the-wild exploitation followed shortly afterwards with web shells and discovery commands observed by Previdian and Defused, and CISA added it to KEV on 26 August with a remediation deadline of 29 August. For a firm, an authentication bypass on the remote-access appliance hands an attacker the same interactive session that Silent Ransom Group spends weeks on the phone trying to talk someone into granting. This is the patch to do first if you have not.

Critical Advisory

SonicWall SMA1000 zero-days are being exploited and are already in KEV — check whether your remote-access box is one of the 400 exposed

SonicWall warned on 2 September 2026 that two flaws in its SMA1000 secure remote-access appliances are under active exploitation, and CISA added both to the KEV catalog the same day. CVE-2026-83548 is the serious one: a command-injection flaw in the SMA1000 Appliance WorkPlace interface stemming from a server-side request forgery weakness, rated critical, and confirmed exploited in the wild. CVE-2026-83549 is a command-injection issue in the Appliance Management Console that requires administrative privileges and permits arbitrary operating-system command execution — useful to an attacker who has already reached the console by other means, including through the first flaw. Affected models are the 6210, 7210 and 8200v. SSL-VPN on SonicWall firewalls and the SMA 100 series are not affected, which matters because those are the products most small firms actually run. SonicWall says its PSIRT investigated a case indicating active exploitation and is urging customers onto the hotfix release without waiting. ShadowServer tracks over 400 internet-exposed SMA1000 appliances worldwide, some of which will already be patched. It also pairs badly with the ClickFix campaign elsewhere on this page — the same compromised third-party sites that ask a user to paste a command can equally serve an exploit that needs no interaction at all. The G7 tells every sector to start the post-quantum migration now — and no sector holds secrets that must stay secret for longer than this one

On 3 September 2026 the national cybersecurity agencies of the G7 states, with the French agency ANSSI chairing the G7 Cybersecurity Working Group and alongside the European Commission and ENISA, issued a joint call to accelerate the transition to post-quantum cryptography. The framing is the notable change: the group explicitly reframes the quantum threat from a distant future problem to a near-term one demanding action across all sectors, not only critical infrastructure. Two dates give it teeth. ANSSI has said it will stop vetting products that lack quantum-safe encryption from 2027, and post-quantum security becomes mandatory for procurement of certain security products by 2030. The statement sets out five priority areas — awareness, national transition strategies and supply chains, research and development, public-private partnership, and building post-quantum cryptography into cybersecurity requirements — and advises organisations to build a cryptographic asset inventory, identify systems holding critical data, map dependencies and take up integrated post-quantum products during normal renewal cycles rather than as a separate programme. This is a policy signal rather than a rule that binds a law firm today, and it is easy to file under “not yet”. The reason not to is specific to legal and professional services: the harvest-now-decrypt-later problem bites hardest where confidentiality obligations run longest. Privilege does not expire. Sealed material stays sealed. Deal documents, trust structures, patent prosecution files, arbitration records and personal data in long-running matters all carry confidentiality duties measured in decades, which is the same horizon the G7 is now telling everyone to plan against. The practical first step costs almost nothing and is useful regardless: know what cryptography you are actually using and where.

A €500,000 CNIL fine sets out, in order, the four control failures a regulator will fault after a breach

France’s data-protection authority CNIL announced a €500,000 fine on 3 September 2026 against Hôpital privé de la Loire in Saint-Étienne, part of the Ramsay Santé group, over a summer 2025 breach affecting 524,867 patients and 202,246 trusted third parties — 727,113 people in total. A single compromised doctor’s account gave the intruder system-wide access to the electronic patient record. The findings are worth reading as a checklist rather than as healthcare news, because they are the same four things any regulator or opposing counsel will ask about: external users were not required to connect over VPN or to use multi-factor authentication; access controls did not limit how many records one account could reach; there was no real-time monitoring or alerting, so the intruder operated undetected for days; and the organisation failed to notify the 202,246 third parties directly. The cited breaches were of Article 32 on security of processing and Article 34 on communication to data subjects. For advisory and privacy practices, this is a usefully concrete data point on how a European regulator weighs a breach where the attacker — a teenager using the alias “Marak” — claimed the data was neither sold nor published: the penalty attached to the controls and the notification failure, not to downstream harm. For firms as controllers in their own right, the second and fourth findings are the uncomfortable ones. Broad standing access to a document management system, and a habit of notifying the client but not the individuals in the file, are both common in this sector — and the second of those is precisely the question the C-Track courts are working through this week.
