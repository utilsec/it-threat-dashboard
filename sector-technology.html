Microsoft ties the ConnectWise Cloud Backup compromise to China-aligned Storm-0940, which used the vendor’s own app identities and an AI agent framework to reach customers’ Microsoft 365 tenants

Microsoft said on 21 October that it assesses Storm-0940, an actor it describes as China-state aligned, was responsible for the compromise of ConnectWise Cloud Backup, the Microsoft 365 backup service formerly called SkyKick. The attacker gained control of several ConnectWise cloud-application identities and signed in to customers’ tenants as those apps, using app credentials rather than user logons, so MFA never came into play; a separate password spray on 10–14 October did not succeed. Microsoft says sign-in attempts reached more than 2,250 tenants and that in several cases privileged access followed within three hours of first targeting, although most of the targeting failed. Storm-0940 ran the campaign with ARTEX, an open-source multi-agent penetration-testing framework that can use any AI model. ConnectWise took Cloud Backup, SaaS Security and Cloud Management offline on 17 October and began a phased restoration on 21 October. It leads because a service MSPs connect to their clients’ tenants became the way in.

Critical Zero-day

Suspected Chinese state hackers used a BeyondTrust Remote Support zero-day from 29 August, stealing credentials and internal files — CISA added it to KEV on 16 October

BeyondTrust and Google’s Mandiant and Threat Intelligence Group disclosed on 15 October that CVE-2026-102764 (CVSS v4 9.3), a flaw in how Remote Support validates the origin of internal messages, was exploited as a zero-day from 29 August against one BeyondTrust-hosted and one self-hosted appliance. Mandiant assesses the group, Armory Castle (also tracked as UNC5221 and Warp Panda), as Chinese and state-sponsored, and BeyondTrust calls it an espionage operation. The intruders planted a PHP web shell called Sawfly and a SOCKS5 tunneller called Bareclaw, gained root, collected the credentials the appliance stored and deleted logs, then used the support tool itself to reach endpoints and copy out documents such as software inventories. The fix shipped on 8 October and all SaaS instances were patched by 9 October; self-hosted customers without automatic updates must patch themselves. CISA added CVE-2026-102764 to its Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog on 16 October with a 19 October federal deadline, which has now passed. Remote-support appliances hold privileged reach into customers’ and employees’ machines, so MSPs and help desks that run one should treat this as an incident review, not only a patch.

Critical Zero-day

Ivanti Connect Secure: a three-flaw chain gave a new actor root on fully patched VPN appliances — one CVE is fixed and in KEV, the privilege-escalation link has no patch, and Ivanti’s integrity checker may miss it

Ivanti disclosed on 13 October that CVE-2026-104935 (CVSS v3.1 8.6), which covers an authentication bypass in welcome.cgi and a file write in wu.cgi, lets an unauthenticated attacker run commands on Connect Secure, and that it had been exploited against a limited number of customers. Volexity, which found it during an incident response opened on 5 October, says the attacker chained a third flaw — a privilege escalation in the dsinvoked broker — to reach root on an appliance running a fully patched 22.8R2.4; that third flaw has no CVE and no fix yet, and Ivanti expects to ship one in the coming weeks. Volexity dates its case to 3 October and Defused saw activity from 1 October, so exploitation goes back to at least early October. The actor, which Volexity tracks as UTA0576, left no web shell or backdoor: it ran commands through the exploit’s own HTTPS requests, stole LDAP and domain credentials, queried Active Directory for LAPS passwords and BitLocker keys, Kerberoasted, sprayed passwords, sniffed LDAP traffic and wiped Ivanti debug and Linux audit logs. Because the chain patches library code in memory and restores configuration data, Volexity and CISA warn that Ivanti’s Integrity Checker Tool may not detect it. CISA added CVE-2026-104935 to KEV on 13 October with a 16 October deadline, and watchTowr expects widespread, indiscriminate exploitation. Volexity has only low confidence that the motive is criminal rather than espionage. For technology companies Connect Secure is often the remote-access front door for engineers and administrators, and this actor went straight for the directory.

Citrix NetScaler: after three exploited zero-days, a fourth critical SAML flaw means some appliances patched in late September must be upgraded again

Citrix published bulletin CTX697191 on 8 October for CVE-2026-107406, a memory overflow in NetScaler ADC and Gateway rated CVSS v4 9.5 that Citrix says may lead to remote code execution or denial of service when the appliance acts as a SAML service provider or identity provider. Citrix says it is not aware of exploitation, Rapid7 says no successful exploitation has been publicly reported, and the flaw was not in KEV as of the 15 October catalog. The catch is the build history: appliances that took the late-September fixes (14.1-73.37 to 73.41, 13.1-64.23 to 64.28) are still exposed if they act as a SAML IdP, and older builds are exposed as SP or IdP, so many customers need a second upgrade in a fortnight; there is no workaround. CISA has urged Citrix customers to patch again and warns that attackers use CVE-2026-88779, the third zero-day (CVSS v4 8.7, in KEV since 4 October), to force reboots so that code planted through CVE-2026-88771 runs. That earlier pair, CVE-2026-88771 and CVE-2026-88772 (both CVSS v4 9.5, in KEV since 27 September), was exploited as zero-days from August: Google Threat Intelligence Group counts dozens of affected organisations across several industries in North America and Europe and attributes the activity only to suspected state-nexus actors, Arctic Wolf counts at least 78 organisations targeted, and Mandiant found two new malware families, the WHIPSHOT web shell and the SLAPSHOT tunneller, in attacks on CVE-2026-88772. Shadowserver saw more than 20,000 potentially vulnerable appliances when the attacks began, and Dutch hospitals briefly suspended patient portals as a precaution. For technology firms NetScaler is often the single sign-on and remote-access front door, and an appliance patched in October can still hold an intruder from August.

LiteLLM 1.107.0 shipped a credential stealer to PyPI, Docker Hub and GHCR for up to about two and a half hours after attackers used maintainers’ stolen GitHub tokens to run the release

BerriAI, which maintains the open-source LiteLLM AI gateway, says its release pipeline was compromised on 21 October and that version 1.107.0 “contained compiled malware which steals credentials and loads remote-code execution tools”. Its security director says the release was run from three engineers’ GitHub accounts with stolen GitHub CLI OAuth tokens — one account triggered the release workflow and another approved it — and that how the tokens were taken is not known. The malicious package was on PyPI from 11:39 to 13:23 UTC, the Docker Hub tags v1.107.0, latest, main-latest and main-stable from 11:46 to 14:20 UTC, and the GHCR images until 13:51 UTC. Socket and Endor Labs report that the application code was unchanged; instead the package added cook.pth, cook.py and sitecustomize.py, which run whenever Python starts, collect cloud, Kubernetes and Docker credentials, AI-provider keys including those stored in LiteLLM, SSH keys, browser data and environment variables, send them encrypted to litellm[.]dev/healthcheck, and leave a loader that polls a GitHub-hosted pointer every hour. The last clean release is 1.106.2; private enterprise builds were not affected, and no download count has been published. An AI gateway holds every model-provider key a company uses. This is a different issue from the configuration flaw CVE-2026-35029 covered under Critical Advisories.

Railway, the application-hosting platform with about 4.8 million users, disclosed on 21 October that it had detected anomalous activity that morning on systems that store customer environment variables. It says an unauthorised third party “may have accessed encrypted environment variables” and, for some, their decrypted contents; initial analysis points to a subset of users, and no number has been given. Railway paused all deployments while it investigated; BleepingComputer reports builds were restored for Pro and Enterprise customers on the morning of 22 October, with other plans following. No attacker has been named, and no other company has linked its own incident to Railway’s. Environment variables are where most deployed apps keep their production keys.

Zeabur, a deployment platform for developers, says an attacker used leaked internal credentials on 14–15 October to break into its shared cluster and some dedicated servers, then read the platform database. By its count, quoted by BleepingComputer, more than 3.2 million environment-variable records and data for 321,810 accounts were exposed across shared clusters in nine regions; the attacker also signed in as root on 101 Google Cloud servers, added SSH keys to 50 servers and attached copies of 16 customer disks to its own virtual machines. It is winding the service down: renewals stop and shared-cluster services pause from 1 December, and dedicated servers and shared clusters end by 31 December, with a possible migration extension not yet scheduled. Customers face both secret rotation and a migration.

What makes it more than another poisoned package is that sckit carries templates to install itself into npm packages, Python packages and GitHub Actions workflows, so every publishing credential it steals is a route to the next project: it is built to spread as a worm. Download counts have not been published, the affected packages sit in a fairly niche corner of the AI-agent tooling ecosystem, and no one has attributed the activity. It is the technique that matters to every software company: the same trick as the GHAPPIER case on this page, where a CI workflow rather than a person did the publishing, but this time with a payload designed to take the next maintainer’s tokens and repeat the process. A release workflow that can be steered by a commit is a publishing credential in its own right.
