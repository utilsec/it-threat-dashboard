Top Story

Luminis Health is running on paper and diverting patients as an unresolved cyberattack keeps its records systems offline

Luminis Health, the Annapolis-based system that operates Luminis Health Anne Arundel Medical Center, Luminis Health Doctors Community Medical Center in Lanham and roughly 100 ambulatory sites across central Maryland, has been operating in downtime procedures since widespread outages began on 31 August 2026. The Maryland Department of Health was alerted the same day and patients began being routed to other facilities; the system confirmed a cybersecurity incident publicly on 1 September and posted a further statement on 2 September. Electronic health records and the MyChart patient portal were taken offline, clinicians reverted to paper charting, and the portal was still unavailable days later. Anne Arundel County Fire Department has reported little effect on ambulance response times because of coordination with hospital staff, but the patient-facing consequences are real: local reporting documents at least one oncology patient whose radiation treatment was postponed with no rescheduled date. Luminis has engaged outside counsel and third-party forensic specialists, stood up a dedicated incident webpage and a patient helpline, and says its priority is safe care while the investigation runs. As of this edition no ransomware group has claimed the attack, no ransom demand has been confirmed, and Luminis has not said whether patient data was accessed or exfiltrated — questions that in comparable intrusions have taken months to answer. Local outlets also note that both hospitals had been placed in lockdown after a bomb threat roughly two weeks earlier, a reminder that physical-security and cyber events increasingly land on the same incident-command structure. Two things make this the week’s most instructive story for hospital readers. First, duration is the harm: an outage measured in days, not hours, exhausts the 24-to-48-hour downtime kits most organisations actually stock, and the failure points shift from charting to results reporting, medication reconciliation, scheduling and revenue cycle. Second, diversion is a regional problem, not a local one — every neighbouring emergency department absorbs volume it did not plan for, which is why a peer system’s incident belongs on your own operations agenda.

Legacy-data archiving vendor Aesto Health posts a 9.54 million-person breach to HHS, nine months after the intrusion

Aesto Health, which provides data migration, EHR-to-EHR exchange and legacy clinical data archiving to provider organisations, appeared on the HHS Office for Civil Rights breach portal on 31 August 2026 with 9,540,000 individuals affected. The unauthorised activity occurred in its Amazon Web Services infrastructure between 2 and 18 December 2025 and was detected on 18 December. Aesto did not confirm a breach until 26 May 2026, began notifying its covered-entity customers on 26 June, and reported to HHS on 31 July. The exposed set is close to a worst case for identity fraud: names, dates of birth, medical information, driver’s licence numbers, financial account numbers, health insurance information, taxpayer identification numbers, government identification numbers and Social Security numbers. No actor has claimed the intrusion and Aesto says it has no evidence of resulting identity theft. The structural lesson is about archives specifically. When a health system retires an EHR, the historical record does not disappear — it is copied to an archiving vendor and then largely forgotten, outside the scope of the access reviews, logging and segmentation that protect the live system. The archive is often broader than the production database because it spans every predecessor system the organisation ever ran. Note also the interval: more than five months between detection and the vendor telling its customers, and eight months before the count reached HHS. If your incident-response clock starts when a business associate tells you, it starts far too late.

A dark-web service offering 153 million driver’s licence scans — and more than half a million medical cards — draws an FBI investigation

On 31 August 2026 a service calling itself Nexus was advertised on the Russian-language cybercrime forum Exploit, selling searchable images of identity documents: more than 153 million United States and Canadian driver’s licences, over 10 million identification cards, more than three million travel and international documents, upwards of 579,000 medical cards, and assorted dispensary and government access credentials. Reporting by Krebs on Security traced the collection to data handled by IDScan.net, a New Orleans identity-verification vendor that says it processes more than 21 million verifications a month at over 20,000 locations. The listing appeared to be fed continuously — roughly 400,000 new records added within a day — suggesting an exfiltration running for about a year rather than a single theft. The FBI’s New Orleans field office opened an investigation on 1 September; IDScan.net has not publicly confirmed a breach, and the Nexus service disappeared from the dark web shortly after the reporting appeared. Treat the attribution as reported rather than confirmed. Healthcare should not read this as someone else’s problem. Registration and admitting desks scan driver’s licences and insurance cards as routine identity proofing, pharmacy counters capture licences for controlled substances, and the set explicitly includes medical cards. High-resolution images of a licence plus an insurance card are precisely what is needed to open a fraudulent episode of care in someone else’s name — and medical identity theft corrupts a clinical record rather than merely a credit file. The related control question is where your own captured images go: how long registration systems retain them, whether they are encrypted at rest, and which third parties receive them.

Medium Phishing

The “MyChart Medicare Kit” phishing wave keeps growing — more than 40 health systems have now warned patients

The count of health systems publicly warning patients about fraudulent MyChart-branded email has climbed from seven in mid-August to 21, and to more than 40 by early September 2026. The warnings span academic medical centres and rural critical-access hospitals alike, which is the tell: this is not an attack on any one organisation but on a brand that thousands of hospitals and clinics share. The lure offers a free “Senior Health Package” under subject lines such as “Your MyChart Medicare Kit Awaits!” and directs recipients to a counterfeit site at mychart-epic[.]com, built by copying the legitimate site’s code and signed off as the “MyChart Health Network” using Epic’s real Verona, Wisconsin address. The page harvests portal credentials and personal information. Epic has been explicit that MyChart itself was not compromised, that no data leaked from the platform, and that the fraudsters are exploiting the brand’s familiarity rather than any flaw in it; a company representative described it as taking advantage of the popularity of the MyChart name. That distinction is the hard part of the response. You cannot patch this, and the organisation whose brand is being abused is not your vendor’s alone — patients see your logo on the portal, so the reputational and support-desk cost lands on you. The Medicare framing also narrows the target: an older population, more likely to hold a portal account they rarely use, and a credential that increasingly unlocks the full longitudinal record rather than just appointment times.

A phishing operation across 46 countries tricks users into installing legitimate remote-management software

ANY.RUN researchers have connected 601 cases into a single campaign that spans 46 countries, with 45% of observed activity aimed at the United States. The method avoids malware entirely at the first stage: victims receive a document lure — Canada Revenue Agency tax forms, shipping and UPS notices, Adobe PDF prompts, US Social Security Administration themes, invoices — and are led through a fake document page to download and install a genuine, signed remote monitoring and management agent. Once it runs, the attacker has interactive remote control using a tool that is code-signed, frequently allow-listed, and indistinguishable from what an IT department legitimately deploys. The infrastructure is deliberately disposable: 425 kit URLs across 240 hosts, 94% of them observed for only a single day, hosted on Vercel, GitHub Pages, Netlify and compromised websites, which defeats blocklists built on reputation. Researchers name education, technology and government as the most-targeted sectors, with banking, finance and manufacturing also prominent; healthcare is not called out in the reporting. It belongs on this page anyway, because the technique is sector-agnostic and healthcare estates are unusually exposed to it — large distributed workforces, high volumes of legitimate external correspondence about claims and invoices, and an existing tolerance for RMM agents installed by managed service providers across affiliated practices.

Critical Advisory

Two PaperCut NG/MF flaws are being exploited and can be chained for unauthenticated code execution on your print servers

CISA added two PaperCut NG/MF vulnerabilities to the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog on 31 August 2026 with a federal remediation deadline of 14 September. CVE-2026-81578 is a missing-authentication flaw that lets an unauthenticated remote attacker change certain system configuration settings. CVE-2026-82078 is an unsafe-reflection flaw that allows execution of arbitrary Java bytecode already present on the application classpath. Individually each is serious; chained, they produce unauthenticated remote code execution on the print management server. CISA records both as exploited in real-world attacks, with ransomware involvement listed as unknown — a status that in previous PaperCut episodes changed quickly once ransomware affiliates picked up the technique. Healthcare should treat this as the most consequential patch of the week alongside SonicWall, because of where print servers sit. PaperCut is the release-and-follow-me layer for the printers that produce patient wristbands, specimen and medication labels, discharge instructions and after-visit summaries, so an outage there is an immediate clinical workflow problem. Worse, the server is almost always domain-joined, integrated with Active Directory for user lookup, and frequently running with a service account far more privileged than the job requires — and print jobs in the spool are documents containing patient data. It is also a system that tends to be owned by a desktop or print team rather than by the group that tracks CISA deadlines, which is exactly how an internet-reachable or flatly-networked instance survives a patch cycle.

Critical Advisory

SonicWall SMA1000 remote-access appliances hit by two chained zero-days, one scoring a maximum 10.0

SonicWall disclosed on 1 September 2026 that two flaws in its SMA1000 secure access appliances were exploited in the wild before any patch existed. CVE-2026-83548 (CVSS 10.0) is a pre-authentication server-side request forgery in the Appliance Work Place interface that lets an unauthenticated remote attacker reach sensitive internal functionality. CVE-2026-83549 (CVSS 7.8) is an OS command injection in the Appliance Management Console that normally requires administrative access — but chained behind the SSRF, that requirement falls away and the pair yields unauthenticated command execution on the appliance. SonicWall found the flaws internally and says it has evidence of both being chained in attacks, without publishing indicators of compromise or attributing the activity. Affected models are the SMA1000 series 6210, 7210 and 8200v; fixed builds are hotfix 12.4.3-03526 or higher and 12.5.0-02952 or higher. SSL-VPN on SonicWall firewalls and the SMA100 series are not affected. CISA added both CVEs to the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog on 2 September with a federal remediation deadline of 5 September. For healthcare this is the week’s most urgent patch. Remote access concentrators are how clinicians, coders, transcription staff and third-party support reach the clinical network, they sit outside the firewall by design, and a compromise there is a session-level position inside the estate rather than a foothold on one workstation.

Critical Advisory

A single unauthenticated request takes over Sangoma Switchvox phone systems, and attackers started using it on 30 August

CVE-2026-9586 is an unauthenticated SQL injection in Sangoma Switchvox, scored CVSS 9.3. An XML-processing endpoint that requires no authentication takes a user-controlled PhoneIP value and concatenates it into queries against the PostgreSQL back end, so a single crafted HTTP POST lets an unauthenticated attacker execute arbitrary SQL and, from there, code on the appliance. Horizon3 and Security Risk Advisors identified the flaw in April 2026 and Sangoma fixed it on 14 July 2026 in Switchvox 8.4.0.2, with SMB Edition 8.3 the affected line. Exploitation in the wild began on 30 August 2026, first from a single source address and then from dozens, with attackers installing reverse shells and following up with second-stage payloads including cryptocurrency miners. Researchers count roughly 4,000 internet-exposed Switchvox instances, predominantly in the United States. CISA added the CVE to the KEV catalog on 4 September 2026, and SecurityWeek reports federal agencies were given a three-day remediation window under BOD 26-04 — an unusually short clock. The healthcare relevance is the customer base. Switchvox is a small-and-medium-business PBX, which in this sector means physician practices, dental and behavioural-health groups, imaging centres, home-health agencies and the affiliated clinics hanging off a larger system’s network. A compromised PBX is not just a telephony outage: voicemail boxes and call recordings routinely contain appointment details, test results and medication requests, so this is a PHI system that almost never appears on a PHI inventory. It is also an excellent platform for internal vishing, since calls placed from your own PBX display your own caller ID.

Critical Advisory

A NetScaler flaw Citrix described as denial of service turns out to allow unauthenticated code execution, and attackers moved within days of the write-up

Citrix patched CVE-2026-8452 in NetScaler ADC and Gateway on 30 June 2026, characterising it as an improper restriction of operations within a memory buffer with denial-of-service impact. On 14 August watchTowr Labs published a technical analysis and proof-of-concept showing the flaw could be driven to full unauthenticated remote code execution. Exploitation followed within days: defenders observed attackers deploying web shells named x.php and z.php, with 36 exploitation attempts from 12 source addresses across several countries logged over a twelve-day span, and activity seen from three countries. CISA added the CVE to the KEV catalog on 26 August 2026 with a federal remediation deadline of 29 August. Fixed builds are 14.1-72.61, 13.1-63.18 and 13.1-37.272. The sequence is the lesson as much as the bug. An organisation that triaged this in July on the vendor’s stated impact would reasonably have queued a denial-of-service fix behind more urgent work, and would have been six weeks behind when the severity was corrected in public. NetScaler is heavily deployed in healthcare as the front door to Citrix-published clinical applications and virtual desktops, so an unauthenticated foothold there sits directly in front of the EHR presentation layer.

High Advisory

Google patches a sixth actively exploited Chrome zero-day of 2026, a type confusion bug in V8

Google shipped an emergency Chrome update on 4 September 2026 fixing CVE-2026-85046, a type confusion vulnerability in the V8 JavaScript and WebAssembly engine rated high severity at CVSS 8.8. The flaw allows arbitrary code execution inside the renderer sandbox by way of a crafted HTML page; the reporting researcher describes an array holding PACKED_ELEMENTS receiving a PACKED_SMI_ELEMENTS map, which can be converted into arbitrary read and write on the JavaScript heap. Google confirmed that an exploit exists in the wild and, as is now standard, withheld details while users update. Fixed versions are 152.0.7977.82/.83 on Windows and macOS and 152.0.7977.82 on Linux. It was reported on 4 August 2026 by researcher Salvatore Gulizia. This is the sixth actively exploited Chrome zero-day patched in 2026. Browser zero-days deserve more weight in healthcare than they usually get. The browser is the client for the patient portal, the payer portal, the imaging viewer, the e-prescribing tool and increasingly for the EHR itself, and it runs on shared clinical workstations where the logged-in session has direct access to clinical applications. Chromium-derived browsers — Edge, Brave, Opera, Electron-based desktop clients — carry the same engine and need the same attention, and clinical workstations are precisely where update deferral policies tend to be longest.

High Advisory

HPE patches an unauthenticated 9.8 remote code execution flaw in Aruba AOS-CX switches, alongside 33 other CVEs

Hewlett Packard Enterprise has released updates for Aruba Networking AOS-CX addressing 34 CVEs. The most severe, CVE-2026-73749 at CVSS 9.8, covers a group of flaws caused by improper processing of malformed input sent to a service on the switch; an unauthenticated attacker who can reach that service with crafted packets gets remote code execution with elevated privileges. The remainder of the set includes 22 high-severity issues spanning denial of service, further remote code and command execution, authentication bypass, privilege escalation and information disclosure, and 11 medium-severity issues. Fixed builds are 10.18.1002, 10.17.1030, 10.16.1060, 10.13.1190 and 10.10.1181. HPE says it is not aware of any of these being exploited in the wild, and no CVE from this batch is in the KEV catalog, which is why this sits below the SonicWall, PaperCut and Switchvox items rather than alongside them. It still deserves a place on a healthcare page because of what Aruba switching underpins in a hospital: the wired and wireless fabric carrying clinical workstations, medication dispensing cabinets, infusion pumps, nurse-call and telemetry, and the access layer for medical devices that cannot host an agent. Code execution on a switch sits underneath every endpoint control you own — it is a position from which to intercept or redirect traffic, and it is invisible to the EDR estate. The mitigating factor is reachability, and that is the control worth verifying: HPE’s own guidance is to keep management interfaces on a dedicated segment with firewall policy and logging, which for most hospitals is a documented intention more often than a verified reality.

Medium Advisory

A twelve-year-old PostgreSQL flaw lets a replication account take over the database server

Researchers at Cyera have disclosed CVE-2026-6471, nicknamed PostGREShell, a missing-authorisation flaw in PostgreSQL’s logical decoding feature. The plugin name supplied when creating a logical replication slot is passed straight to the loader without validation or sanitisation, so an account holding only the REPLICATION privilege can cause arbitrary files to be loaded and code to be executed as the postgres operating-system user. From there the path to full superuser rights, access to every database on the instance and persistent backdoor installation is straightforward. The flaw is rated CVSS 7.2 and has been present since PostgreSQL 9.4, roughly twelve years, affecting every release through 18.2; fixes are in 18.6, 17.11, 16.15, 15.19 and 14.24. There are no public reports of exploitation. The reason this belongs on a healthcare page is that PostgreSQL is everywhere underneath health IT without appearing on anyone’s application list: it backs imaging and PACS components, interface engines and HIE middleware, analytics and research warehouses, scheduling and practice-management products, and the internal databases of appliances you administer as black boxes — the Switchvox flaw elsewhere on this page attacks a PostgreSQL back end too. The privilege at issue is one organisations hand out casually. Replication roles get created for backup agents, read replicas, reporting mirrors and vendor data feeds, are documented as low-risk because they cannot write application data, and are then never reviewed. This flaw makes that assumption wrong, and the practical exposure is a vendor integration account nobody has looked at in years.

Patched: PostgreSQL 18.6, 17.11, 16.15, 15.19 and 14.24 Action: Start with discovery rather than patching, because most healthcare PostgreSQL instances are embedded in something else. Ask application owners and your major vendors, in writing, whether their product ships or requires PostgreSQL and what version, and add the answers to your asset inventory. Every replication role should map to a named backup, replica or integration purpose with an owner; delete the rest, and restrict connections for the survivors by source address in pg_hba.conf. Info Policy

OCR settles its 55th right-of-access case, over records a patient waited nearly two years to receive

The HHS Office for Civil Rights announced a $50,000 settlement with Azul Vision, Inc., a California optometry and ophthalmology practice, on 27 August 2026. A patient requested their medical records in April 2023 and filed a complaint with OCR the same month; the records were not provided until January 2025, nearly two years later against a HIPAA requirement of 30 days with one permitted 30-day extension. The corrective action plan requires Azul Vision to revise its policies and procedures, train its workforce on the right of access, and report to OCR on every access request received, including the dates of receipt and completion. This is the 55th enforcement action under OCR’s Right of Access Initiative and the ninth financial penalty the office has announced in 2026. The signal for compliance and IT leaders is that this line of enforcement has not slowed, and that it is triggered by a single complaint from one patient rather than by a breach. Right-of-access failures are usually process failures rather than security failures — requests arriving by several channels with no single queue, no clock, and no owner — which makes them cheap to fix and conspicuous when they are not.

Info Litigation

DaVita agrees to $15 million to settle litigation over the Interlock ransomware attack on its dialysis network

DaVita, which operates more than 3,000 dialysis centres worldwide, has agreed to pay $15 million to resolve consolidated class litigation over the 12 April 2025 ransomware attack attributed to the Interlock group. The breach affected roughly 2.7 million individuals — 2,689,826 by the documented count — exposing names, contact information, Social Security numbers, health insurance information, clinical information and tax information. Interlock claimed to have taken more than 20 terabytes and published about 1.5 terabytes on its leak site. Under the settlement, $10 million goes to class member relief, with documented out-of-pocket losses reimbursable up to $2,500 per person and a pro rata cash payment anticipated at around $50 for roughly 2.3 million eligible individuals; the remainder covers fees, administration and service awards. DaVita admits no liability. The case, brought in Colorado federal court, alleged negligence and inadequate cybersecurity. Two points are worth noting for boards. The reported settlement does not carry the injunctive security-improvement commitments that some healthcare breach settlements now include, so the cost here is purely financial and does not create ongoing court-supervised obligations. And the ratio is instructive: roughly $5.50 per affected individual in total settlement value, which is the sort of figure that belongs in a risk quantification alongside notification, credit monitoring, forensic and downtime costs.
