Top Story

Telus tells customers that attackers were inside subscriber accounts for sixteen months — and the way in was credentials, not a vulnerability

Telus disclosed on 14 September 2026 that a campaign running from February 2025 to June 2026 used compromised credentials to log into customer accounts, view and in some cases modify billing, subscription and contact data, and attempt to move subscribers to competing providers. The exposed fields named in the disclosure are names, account numbers, phone numbers, billing addresses, partial card numbers, and subscription and payment history. Two things Telus has not said matter as much as what it has: the number of affected customers is not disclosed, and neither is the origin of the credentials — whether they came from an internal breach or were harvested elsewhere and replayed. No software flaw has been named, and none is implied. What is confirmed is the shape of the intrusion and its duration. Sixteen months of account access that produced port-out attempts and subscription changes is a detection failure on the customer-facing estate rather than on the network, and that estate is the one part of a carrier that is deliberately exposed to the whole internet and defended mostly by a password. The operational point for other operators is that the self-service portal is the control plane for the phone number itself: an attacker holding a subscriber session can attempt a port, change the contact address a confirmation is sent to, and alter a plan, which is the same sequence that precedes SIM-swap and account-takeover fraud downstream. Telus has forced credential resets on affected accounts, increased account monitoring, notified Vancouver Police and is offering identity-theft protection. The controls that generalise are credential-stuffing defences on the login path, mandatory multi-factor authentication on self-service rather than optional, a port-out lock or PIN that is not derivable from the account record, and alerting on the sequence of contact-detail change followed by a port or plan change — because it is the sequence, not any single request, that is the signal.

High Incident

An intruder held long-term access inside Thailand’s largest broadband provider by running MeshCentral as a hidden backdoor

Reporting published on 14 September 2026 describes an intruder who maintained persistent access inside the network of 3BB, Thailand’s largest broadband provider, by configuring MeshCentral — a legitimate, widely used open-source remote-management platform — as a covert backdoor, with subscriber credentials as the objective. No CVE is involved and no exploit was needed for the persistence itself: the tool is the implant. That is the detail worth carrying into your own environment, because a remote-management server is exactly the kind of asset that survives a hunt. It is signed, it is expected to talk to large numbers of hosts on odd ports, its agent runs as a service with administrative rights, and an unauthorised instance looks identical in process and network telemetry to an authorised one. Carrier and ISP estates are unusually exposed to this pattern because they already run several overlapping remote-management and provisioning systems — field-technician tooling, CPE and ACS management, NOC jump infrastructure, managed-service platforms for business customers — often inherited through acquisitions and rarely inventoried end to end. The control is an authoritative list of which remote-management platforms are sanctioned, which servers are authorised to run them, and which accounts are permitted to enrol agents, followed by a sweep for instances that appear on none of those lists. Nothing here tells you how the intruder first got in; 3BB has not published that, and it should not be assumed.

Critical Incident

A critical NetScaler authentication bypass went from public proof-of-concept to live exploitation in about a day

CVE-2026-19490 is a critical authentication bypass in Citrix NetScaler ADC and Gateway, disclosed in a Citrix advisory on 19 August 2026. It affects appliances configured as an AAA virtual server or as a Gateway — SSL VPN, ICA Proxy, CVPN or RDP Proxy — with the precise impact varying by firmware and SAML Action configuration. SecurityWeek reported at disclosure that exploitation was expected; it arrived quickly. After a proof-of-concept appeared publicly, Ryan Dewhurst of Previdian recorded exploitation attempts from three countries on 3 September, and Belgium’s national cybersecurity centre issued its own warning the following day; reporting around the KEV listing puts the documented total at 56 exploitation attempts since early September. Shadowserver counts more than 22,000 exposed NetScaler ADC appliances and close to 1,700 Gateway instances worldwide, though how many are patched is unknown. Authentication bypass is the worst failure mode for a device in this role, because it strips the control everything behind the Gateway assumes is holding — MFA, conditional access, session identity — without generating a failed-login signal for anyone to notice. For carriers and ISPs, NetScaler typically fronts customer self-service portals, provisioning front ends and staff application access, so a bypass there is an identity problem before it is an appliance problem.

High Campaign

Attackers are planting a Linux rootkit on F5 BIG-IP APM appliances that leaves no malicious file on disk

Reporting on 8 September 2026 describes intrusions in which attackers deploy a Linux rootkit on F5 BIG-IP Access Policy Manager devices. The rootkit hooks the loading of PHP files and injects a web shell directly into memory, so nothing malicious is written to the filesystem — a file-integrity check of the appliance comes back clean while the attacker retains interactive access. BIG-IP APM sits at the remote-access edge of a great many enterprise, carrier and government networks, terminating VPN and application-access sessions and holding the authentication decisions for the users behind them. That position is the whole point of the target selection: an implant there sees credentials and session traffic in the clear, and it survives the checks most teams would run. For carriers and service providers there is a second dimension. Appliances of this class are frequently operated on behalf of enterprise customers, so a compromise is a multi-tenant problem rather than a single-device one, and the tenant is unlikely to be able to inspect the box themselves. The detection approach has to shift accordingly: on-box antivirus and file hashing will not find a fileless implant, so look outward at what the appliance does — unexpected outbound connections, administrative sessions from unfamiliar sources, and changes in process behaviour — and treat vendor guidance on integrity verification as the authoritative check.

Critical Incident

Attackers are exploiting an unauthenticated SQL injection in Sangoma Switchvox to reach code execution on enterprise phone systems

CVE-2026-9586 is an unauthenticated SQL injection in Sangoma Switchvox, the VoIP and unified-communications platform used to run enterprise PBX estates, rated CVSS 9.3 and exploited in the wild. The defect sits in an XML-processing endpoint that concatenates an unauthenticated PhoneIP parameter straight into PostgreSQL queries with no parameterisation, so an attacker who can reach the interface can run arbitrary SQL and pivot from there to code execution. Horizon3 confirmed live exploitation and published indicators of compromise; CISA added the CVE to the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog on 2 September with a 5 September federal remediation date, and reporting on that KEV batch describes post-exploitation activity including reverse shells and credential harvesting. It matters more on this page than on a general IT one because Switchvox is telephony infrastructure — deployed by carriers, MVNOs, hosted-voice providers and the channel partners who resell them, and typically sitting close to SIP trunks and provisioning data rather than in a hardened data-centre tier. A database compromise on a PBX is not just a server; it is extension mappings, SIP registration credentials, call detail records and voicemail — the raw materials for toll fraud, for caller-ID spoofing that survives downstream trust checks, and for quiet call-metadata collection. The federal deadline has now passed. Treat any internet-reachable Switchvox as compromised until proven otherwise, and start with the SIP and administrative credentials it holds rather than with the patch alone.

High Incident

A BGP hijack let attackers pass a certificate authority’s domain validation and sign a malicious software update with a genuine TLS certificate

Softaculous confirmed that from roughly 20:57 UTC on 28 August until 30 August 2026 a threat actor hijacked its IP address space at the routing layer. The attacker announced a more-specific route through AS62390 (NexonHost) while keeping the legitimate AS24940 (Hetzner) on the AS path, so the announcement looked plausible to route filters and to anyone glancing at origin data. The traffic pulled in by that hijack included the certificate authority’s automated domain-validation checks, which meant the attacker could satisfy Let’s Encrypt’s validation for a domain it did not control and obtain a genuine, publicly trusted TLS certificate. Holding that certificate, it served malicious Virtualizor control-panel updates to the small number of installations that happened to poll for updates during the roughly 22 hours of diversion, which included an 11-hour partial gap. The victim count is not the story here. This is a worked demonstration that route manipulation defeats domain-validated certificate issuance, and it lands squarely on network operators, because the people positioned to stop it are the ones whose filtering and RPKI policy decide whether a more-specific hijack propagates at all — and the people positioned to detect it are the ones watching announcements for their own prefixes.

Red Heron scanned 1,386 self-hosted Gitea servers and got into thirteen organisations — telecom among the sectors named

Researchers disclosed on 14 September 2026 that a group tracked as Red Heron, attributed to China at moderate confidence, automated a public proof-of-concept for CVE-2026-60004 — a critical remote code execution flaw in the self-hosted Gitea code platform, patched back in July 2026 — into a Python framework that registers accounts, exploits unpatched servers, steals repositories and cleans up after itself. The framework was pointed at 1,386 Gitea instances across seven countries and produced confirmed compromises at thirteen organisations in the United States, Canada, Taiwan, Argentina, Qatar and Sri Lanka, spanning defence, elections, energy, aerospace, government and telecommunications. At one Taiwanese victim the group reached root on a three-node Proxmox cluster and installed a custom Linux rootkit, SIXZUT, that patches fifteen kernel functions to hide itself. The telecom relevance is not that carriers run Gitea more than anyone else; it is that a self-hosted code platform inside an operator holds network automation, device configuration templates and the credentials that drive them, and it is the kind of internal service that gets stood up by an engineering team and then falls outside the patch programme. This is exploitation of unpatched instances of a two-month-old flaw, not a zero-day, which makes it a question about your asset inventory rather than about your patch speed. Confirm every Gitea instance you own is on a fixed release, then audit for accounts you did not create and repository clones you did not authorise — patching does not evict an attacker who already holds a valid account.

High Threat Intel

A China-nexus group is living inside Cisco IOS XR routers and TACACS servers, turning carrier-class network gear into a listening post

Sygnia has published incident-response research documenting activity by Fire Ant, a China-nexus intrusion set whose operations the firm assesses as strongly overlapping with UNC3886 while noting differences in filenames, paths and implementation that stop short of conclusive attribution. What makes it matter for this sector is that it is not a CVE. The group moved off the VMware hypervisor estates it was known for and onto the network layer itself: Cisco IOS XR routers, TACACS authentication servers and Linux management hosts. On the routers a custom implant ran behind a fake system service on an alternating schedule to limit its exposure to observation, while a second backdoor, BridgeAgent, presented itself as a legitimate Zabbix monitoring agent and supplied tunnelling and persistence. The operators created GRE tunnel interfaces that left no entry in configuration history and suppressed the syslog messages that would have described them, took interactive shell access without logging, and manipulated file timestamps to frustrate forensics. The routers then became collection platforms, with packet captures written on the device and uploaded to external FTP servers — exposing internal topology, administrative sessions, authentication flows and routing relationships. Sygnia describes a “target behind the target” pattern, in which trusted infrastructure is used to reach the high-value networks that connect to it.

For a carrier or ISP this lands on exactly the assets that usually sit outside the security programme rather than inside it. IOS XR is the operating system of the service-provider core; TACACS decides who may touch it. Compromise both and an adversary holds traffic visibility, credential flow and the authorisation layer at once. The second half of the finding is worse than the first: because the actor suppressed logging, the telemetry an operator would reach for to answer “has this happened to us” is the same telemetry the actor was editing. Sygnia’s guidance is to treat routers, TACACS servers, hypervisors and jump hosts as first-class forensic and Tier-0 assets — export their telemetry to independently administered systems, validate logs against more than one source of evidence, keep out-of-band access that does not traverse the equipment being investigated, and limit the blast radius of trust between connected networks. Sygnia declined to name the victim’s sector or location and has published indicators of compromise, YARA rules and hunting guidance. There is no patch, because no vulnerability was named: this is a hunting exercise, and the absence of a fix to apply is the reason to start it rather than defer it.

High Threat Intel

Microsoft documents two initial-access crews phoning helpdesks, enrolling personal devices, then draining Microsoft 365 through the Graph API

Microsoft researchers have detailed at least two initial-access-broker clusters, tracked as Storm-3032 and Storm-3121, running voice-phishing campaigns since May 2026. The pattern reported on 10 September 2026 is consistent across both: a caller impersonates the IT helpdesk, walks the target to a convincing Microsoft sign-in page on a domain built to look procedural — the example given is company.add-passkey.com — and captures credentials and session tokens from the victim’s own personal device rather than a managed endpoint. From there the operators abuse the Microsoft Graph API to enumerate and pull data out of SharePoint, OneDrive and Exchange in small, periodic transfers sized to look like ordinary synchronisation rather than exfiltration. Access is reported to be sold on to extortion groups including ShinyHunters. Microsoft says it has not yet tied the campaigns to a confirmed corporate breach, and no victim organisation has been named.

The reason this belongs on a telecom page is structural rather than sectoral. Carriers, ISPs and cable operators run some of the largest helpdesk and retail-channel workforces in any industry, with high turnover, shift coverage, outsourced tiers and a culture of resetting things quickly for colleagues and customers alike — which is exactly the ground a helpdesk-impersonation call is designed to work. The BYOD step is what defeats the usual controls: nothing touches a managed device, so endpoint detection never sees it, and the subsequent activity is a legitimately-issued token calling a legitimate API. The detections that matter are identity-side, not endpoint-side: new device registrations and passkey or MFA-method additions that follow a helpdesk contact, sign-ins from unmanaged devices, consent grants and unusual Graph API read volume against SharePoint and Exchange, and any authentication against look-alike sign-in domains. The corresponding process control is verification of the caller on the helpdesk side, not just verification of the user.

A crafted email is enough to get root on Cisco Secure Email Gateway, and Cisco confirms the flaw is already being exploited

CVE-2026-76461 is an unauthenticated remote code execution flaw in Cisco Secure Email Gateway and Secure Email and Web Manager, rated CVSS 9.8, and Cisco has confirmed exploitation in the wild. The attack is delivered through the appliance’s own function: a message containing SQL injection payloads is parsed during email processing, and the result is command execution with root privileges on the underlying operating system. Every configuration is in scope and there is no workaround — the fixed AsyncOS releases are 15.5.5-0141, 16.0.4-302 and 16.5.0-780. The exposure profile is what makes this urgent rather than merely severe. An email gateway accepts connections from anyone by design, sits at the perimeter, and holds the credentials and trust relationships needed to read, rewrite and release mail for the whole organisation; it is also frequently a shared platform, because operators and service providers commonly run mail hygiene for business customers on the same estate they run their own on. A root shell there yields message content, routing configuration, directory integration and a plausible platform from which to send. Treat an unpatched appliance that has been internet-reachable during the exploitation window as a compromise investigation, not a patch task: apply the AsyncOS fix, then rotate the appliance’s credentials, LDAP or Active Directory bind accounts and API keys, and review mail-flow policies, message-filter rules and administrator accounts for changes you did not make.
