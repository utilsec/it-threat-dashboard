Top Story

FBI opens an investigation after a dark-web service begins selling scans of more than 153 million US and Canadian driving licences

On 31 August 2026 an actor using the name “Nexus” advertised a new dark-web search service on a Russian-language cybercrime forum, offering images of what it claimed were more than 153 million US and Canadian driving licences, upwards of 10 million other identification cards, roughly 3 million travel and international identity documents, and several hundred thousand medical cards. The listings reportedly include front and back scans and, in some cases, the infrared and ultraviolet captures that scanning hardware produces, each carrying a timestamp. Reporting by Krebs on Security on 2 September pointed to IDScan.net, a New Orleans-based identity-verification vendor whose scanning software and hardware are used at car-hire counters, hotels, dispensaries and other age- and identity-checking points; the timestamps on sampled records lined up with occasions when the named individuals had handed over an identity document. The FBI’s New Orleans field office opened an investigation on 1 September. The company has acknowledged that it is investigating but has not confirmed a breach or issued a detailed statement, so at this stage the scale is a seller’s claim rather than a vendor-confirmed figure. Two things make this a public-sector story rather than a retail one. First, the documents are government-issued credentials: states and provinces produce them, and the entire chain of identity proofing for benefits, licensing, tax and unemployment portals assumes an image of one is reasonably hard to obtain. A searchable market in high-fidelity scans, including the security-feature captures that automated checks look at, weakens document-image verification as a control across every government service that relies on it. Second, records said to belong to serving federal officials, including a senior FBI executive, appear in the data — which turns this into a targeting and impersonation problem for agencies as well as a fraud problem for citizens. Driving licence numbers can be reissued, but only slowly and at the holder’s initiative, and a face image cannot be reissued at all.

High Ransomware

A Minnesota county paid a $128,000 ransom in January — and a different group hit it again three months later

Winona County, Minnesota has confirmed that it paid $128,539 to recover from a ransomware attack in January 2026, and that a second, separate ransomware attack struck the county in April. County Administrator Maureen Holte said roughly $50,000 of the payment came from insurance and about $78,000 from the county levy — that is, from local taxpayers — and described the decision as taken after careful consideration and on the advice of the county’s cybersecurity advisers, to restore services and protect residents’ personal information. County officials have said the two incidents were the work of different criminal groups. Emergency services continued to operate through both, but other county functions fell back to paper processes. The January investigation is still open and affected individuals have been notified; the scope of the April incident was still being assessed as of early September. The uncomfortable detail is the sequence. Paying a ransom buys a decryption key and, sometimes, a deletion promise; it does not remediate the access path, and it does not make an organisation a less attractive target. A county that pays is visible as a county that pays. Nothing in the public record suggests the second group used the first group’s access, but the second attack landed inside the window in which the county was still recovering from the first — which is exactly when detective controls, monitoring and change management are weakest and staff are most stretched. For small counties and municipalities the transferable lesson is about the recovery period rather than the payment decision: the months after an incident are the months you are most likely to be hit again, and that is when hardening and monitoring investment has to land, not when the budget cycle allows.

A New Hampshire county spends a week on paper after a network security incident cuts dispatch access to criminal records

Merrimack County, New Hampshire was still running degraded a week after a network security incident that first surfaced in late August, when county dispatchers lost access to criminal records held in the state’s IMC records system. By 1 September the Register of Deeds had stopped accepting electronic submissions altogether and was requiring paper filings delivered in person or by mail. County officials said certain systems remained offline out of an abundance of caution, that this might limit services, and that third-party experts were assisting the investigation. They stressed that emergency services were not compromised. No attribution has been made public and no group has claimed the incident. Two features of this one generalise. The first is the dependency chain: a county dispatch function lost access to a state-operated records platform, which means the incident boundary and the service boundary were not the same, and the county could not restore the service on its own timetable. Any agency that consumes a shared state or regional system should know in advance which of its own services stop when that link is cut, and whether a manual alternative exists that dispatchers and clerks have actually practised. The second is the deeds registry. Land records are a statutory function with recording deadlines and downstream conveyancing consequences; a week of paper-only filing is a real backlog with legal effects, not an inconvenience. Continuity plans for record-keeping offices should specify how filings received on paper are queued, timestamped and reconciled once the electronic system returns.

Thomson Reuters disclosed in the first days of September that attackers had unauthorised access to its environment from roughly 1 March to 29 June 2026, affecting C-Track, the court case-management platform its West Publishing unit operates for judiciaries. Second, the vendor stated the compromise occurred in its environment, not in court systems, which means no court could have detected or prevented it with its own controls. Sealed records are the part that cannot be remediated: credit monitoring, offered here for 12 months, does nothing for confidentiality that has already been lost.

High Research

A criminal crew is renting out the trust in government domains: malicious Apache modules turn .gov.br sites into SEO relays

Check Point Research published analysis on 3 September of a Chinese-speaking cybercrime operation it calls Gambling Goblin, active since mid-2025 and overlapping with activity Trend Micro documented in 2022 as Earth Berberoka. The technique is the interesting part for anyone who runs a government web estate. Rather than defacing sites or hosting malware on them, the operators install a malicious Apache module on the compromised web server that acts as a selective reverse proxy: it fetches gambling and sports-betting content from attacker infrastructure and serves it under the government domain, stripping the site’s own security headers so the injected content runs unimpeded. The behaviour is conditional — related tooling documented in this space altered the server response only when the request came from a search-engine crawler — so an administrator browsing the site sees nothing wrong while the search index fills with betting pages that carry the credibility of a state domain. The scale reported by other researchers in the same campaign space is substantial: ANY.RUN documented at least twenty .gov.br portals used in related activity in July 2026, and Hunt.io counted more than 630,000 URLs generated on hijacked gov.br subdomains. Check Point notes that compromised .gov.br and .jus.br hosts should be handled differently from attacker-owned infrastructure, since blanket blocking would cut citizens off from legitimate government services. The lesson travels to any .gov, .gov.uk or municipal domain: the asset being stolen is the domain’s reputation, and the compromise is designed to be invisible to the owner.

Medium Espionage

A new APT28-linked backdoor hides its command channel inside a legitimate webhook service, targeting European government and diplomatic bodies

Recorded Future’s Insikt Group documented a backdoor it calls HOOKEDGE, used against government and diplomatic organisations in Romania, Spain and Türkiye between late September 2025 and early April 2026, and linked with moderate confidence to the Russian state-sponsored group tracked as APT28, also known as BlueDelta, Fancy Bear and Forest Blizzard. Initial access is unglamorous: macro-enabled Word documents with diplomatic-themed lures, some early samples impersonating Spanish government material. Enabling content writes six files to the user profile directory and launches the installer chain; persistence is a scheduled task that runs every thirty minutes. What makes HOOKEDGE worth a defender’s attention is the command-and-control design. Rather than standing up dedicated infrastructure, it abuses webhook.site — a legitimate, widely used developer testing service — for tasking, staging and exfiltration, fetching arbitrary .cmd payloads from a staging endpoint, running them, and posting the output back. Traffic therefore goes to a real service with a valid certificate and an unremarkable reputation, which defeats domain-reputation blocking and blends into ordinary developer noise. A second-stage variant reserved for higher-value targets beacons as often as every five minutes. Insikt attributes on code and tradecraft overlap with HEADLACE, an APT28 backdoor in use since 2023. For public-sector defenders the practical takeaway is that macro-enabled documents are still landing, and that “the destination is a legitimate SaaS service” is not evidence of benign traffic.

High Research

CISA red-teamed a government-sector organisation to full domain compromise and its SOC noticed nothing

CISA released advisory AA26-237A, “A Tale of Two SOCs”, on 25 August 2026, publishing two red team assessments run with comparable tradecraft against two critical-infrastructure organisations. Organisation A is a government services and facilities entity; Organisation B is a water and wastewater utility. Both were compromised at the domain level. The difference was detection: Organisation B’s SOC identified and isolated phishing payloads within two to twenty minutes and stopped the intrusion spreading, while at Organisation A the entire operation went unnoticed. The path into Organisation A is depressingly ordinary. Default credentials on a web application gave initial access; Active Directory Certificate Services misconfiguration provided privilege escalation; cleartext credentials and static, non-expiring AWS access keys carried the team into sensitive systems. The advisory also flags default Machine Account Quota settings and over-permissioned Entra ID applications. There is no CVE here and nothing to patch, which is precisely why the advisory is worth an hour of a government security team’s time: every finding is a configuration or process decision that an audit would likely mark compliant. CISA’s own conclusion is the line to take to leadership — detection tools are only as effective as the people, processes and procedures behind them. Two organisations with similar tooling produced opposite outcomes because one had a SOC that was practised at acting on what the tooling said.

Phishing campaign spanning 46 countries installs legitimate remote-management software, using government-themed lures

ANY.RUN published analysis on 3 September 2026 of a phishing campaign that reached 46 countries and 601 observed cases, with the United States accounting for about 45 per cent of activity. The payload is not malware in the conventional sense: victims are led to install genuine remote monitoring and management software, which then gives the operator interactive remote access under a signed, legitimate binary that endpoint tooling is often configured to permit. Government is named among the targeted sectors alongside education, technology, banking and finance, and manufacturing — and the lures lean heavily on public-sector identity, impersonating Canada Revenue Agency tax forms and US Social Security Administration notices as well as shipping and invoice themes. The delivery infrastructure is deliberately disposable: 425 kit URLs across 240 hosts, 94 per cent of which were seen for a single day only, rotated through Vercel, GitHub Pages, Netlify and compromised sites. Blocking domains is therefore close to useless. The researchers’ recommendation is to detect the chain instead — recurring assets such as a shared font file and images, and the consistent secure.html to project archive structure — and to build detections that survive the operators switching RMM vendors, which they do routinely. For agencies the defensive question is narrow and answerable: which RMM products are approved in your estate, and does anything stop an unprivileged user installing one that is not?

Critical Advisory

Two PaperCut NG/MF flaws are being exploited as a chain, with a 14 September federal deadline — and print servers are everywhere in government

CISA added two PaperCut NG/MF vulnerabilities to the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog on 31 August 2026, directing federal civilian agencies to remediate by 14 September. CVE-2026-81578, rated CVSS 8.8, is a missing-authentication-for-critical-function flaw that allows pre-authentication remote code execution. CVE-2026-82078, rated CVSS 9.4, is an unsafe reflection issue that permits loading of unsafe Java classes to execute arbitrary code. BOD 26-04 is the reason federal deadlines in this window were three days, not thirty

The compressed remediation dates attached to this edition’s KEV entries — 5 September for flaws added on 2 September, 29 August for flaws added on 26 August — are not ad-hoc urgency. They are the mechanical output of Binding Operational Directive 26-04, “Prioritizing Security Updates Based on Risk”, issued on 10 June 2026, which replaced fixed 15-day and 30-day windows with timelines derived from four questions: is the asset publicly exposed, is the vulnerability on KEV, can exploitation be automated, and does exploitation grant control of the system. Where the answer to all four is yes, the remediation window is three calendar days and a forensic triage step is required as well. Mid-tier combinations get 14 days, lower-risk internal cases 60 days, and the lowest tier is fixed on the next upgrade. The directive also requires exposure status to be maintained continuously in Continuous Diagnostics and Mitigation dashboards, with a 60-day process deadline and a 180-day deadline for meeting the full timeline table. Non-federal government bodies are not bound by it, but the framework travels well and is worth adopting on its logic rather than its authority: the four questions are a better triage rubric than a CVSS threshold, and they explain to non-technical leadership why an internet-facing appliance on KEV outranks a higher-scored flaw on an internal server.
