Luminis Health names a criminal actor and enters a second week on paper, with MyChart and phone systems still down

Luminis Health, the Annapolis-based system that runs Luminis Health Anne Arundel Medical Center, Luminis Health Doctors Community Medical Center in Lanham and roughly 100 ambulatory sites across central Maryland, has been operating in downtime procedures since widespread outages began on 31 August 2026. The Maryland Department of Health was alerted the same day and patients began being routed to other facilities; the system confirmed a cybersecurity incident on 1 September, took MyChart and its CareConnectNow platform offline, and reverted clinicians to paper charting. The most substantive change since our last edition is in the language. Hospitals remain open. As of this edition no ransomware group has publicly claimed the attack, no ransom demand has been confirmed, and Luminis has not said whether patient data was accessed or exfiltrated — questions that in comparable intrusions have taken months to answer. Local reporting has documented real patient-facing consequences, including at least one oncology patient whose radiation treatment was postponed. Two things make this the week’s most instructive story for hospital readers. First, duration is the harm: an outage now running into a second week exhausts the 24-to-48-hour downtime kits most organisations actually stock, and the failure points migrate from charting to results reporting, medication reconciliation, scheduling and revenue cycle. Second, diversion is a regional problem, not a local one — every neighbouring emergency department absorbs volume it did not plan for, which is why a peer system’s incident belongs on your own operations agenda rather than only on your news feed.

The FBI is investigating a dark-web service selling 153 million driver’s licence scans allegedly taken from an ID-verification vendor

A new dark-web marketplace has been offering more than 153 million driver’s licence images and millions of additional identity documents, with reporting linking the trove to an alleged breach at IDScan.net, a vendor whose scanning and age-verification technology is embedded in point-of-sale, access-control and patient-registration workflows. Krebs on Security, SecurityWeek, Cybernews, Malwarebytes and Time have all covered the offering, the FBI has confirmed it is investigating, and IDScan has already been sued in a putative class action over the alleged breach. Attribution and the exact provenance of the images are not settled — the seller’s claims are claims, and the vendor’s own account of what happened is still developing — but the volume and the document type are what matter here. This is not a password dump. Driver’s licence images are the single most useful artefact for defeating the identity checks that healthcare organisations rely on at exactly the points where fraud is most expensive: patient registration and identity proofing, portal account recovery, insurance eligibility and enrolment, and the help-desk workflows that reset clinician credentials. A convincing licence image plus a name, date of birth and address is enough to open a portal account in someone else’s name, and medical identity theft is far harder for a patient to unwind than a fraudulent card charge. Any process in your organisation whose strongest control is “the caller sent a photo of their ID” should be assumed compromised as a control, not merely at elevated risk.

Legacy-data archiving vendor Aesto Health reports a breach affecting more than 9.5 million people

Aesto Health, which provides legacy clinical data archiving and release-of-information services to healthcare providers, has reported a data breach affecting more than 9.5 million individuals — one of the largest healthcare breach filings of the year. Reporting from SecurityWeek, BleepingComputer, HIPAA Journal, Becker’s Hospital Review and others describes exposure spanning names, Social Security numbers, financial details and health records. The structural point matters more than the headline number. Aesto’s business is holding data that provider organisations have retired from their live systems: records from decommissioned EHRs, imaging archives and practices that were acquired or closed. That data is subject to the same retention obligations as live data, is rarely covered by the same monitoring, and is often held under contracts signed by a department that no longer exists. For most affected organisations, the first they knew of their exposure was a notification from a vendor they may not have thought of as current. Legacy archives concentrate decades of records in one place while attracting a fraction of the security attention that production systems get, and they are exactly the kind of third party that never appears on a tier-one vendor list because nothing clinical breaks when they go down.

High Breach

Baylor Genetics confirms the protected health information of 2.8 million people was exposed in a cybersecurity incident

Baylor Genetics, the Houston-based clinical genomics laboratory, has confirmed that the electronic protected health information of approximately 2.8 million patients was exposed in a cybersecurity incident. The company has posted a security update, and the breach has been covered by HIPAA Journal, Cybersecurity Dive and Cybernews. Diagnostic laboratories are a high-value target for a reason that is easy to under-weight: a laboratory holds identified clinical data for the patients of many provider organisations at once, so a single lab breach produces notification obligations across dozens of unrelated health systems and practices, none of which had any visibility into the lab’s controls. Genetic and genomic testing raises the stakes further. Unlike a payment card or even a Social Security number, genomic data cannot be reissued, it implicates biological relatives who never consented to anything, and its sensitivity does not decay — a record exposed today is as damaging in twenty years as it is now. That permanence is why breach-response measures built around credit monitoring are a poor fit for this category of data, and why the harm framing in the litigation that typically follows tends to look different from an ordinary PHI case.

Portal credentials also matter more than patients assume — a compromised MyChart account exposes the full clinical record, messaging with clinicians, billing detail and often family members’ proxy access, and it can be used to redirect refunds or request prescription changes.

Medium Sector Intel

Unit 42 reports an enterprise breach completed in under ten hours by autonomous AI agents, using no zero-days at all

Palo Alto Networks’ Unit 42 has published research describing an enterprise compromise carried out end to end in under ten hours by autonomous AI agents, automating more than 50 MITRE ATT&CK techniques without using a single zero-day. Unit 42 published the investigation on 2 September 2026, and its own framing is that the attacker compressed roughly two weeks of coordinated human red-team effort into an automated loop of monitoring, evaluation and re-planning, driving multiple frontier AI agents in parallel. The finding that matters is the absence of anything exotic. The agents used known techniques against known weaknesses; what changed was the speed and the removal of the human bottleneck between reconnaissance, credential access, lateral movement and exfiltration. Separate reporting in the same period is consistent with the direction of travel, including Rapid7’s 2026 global threat landscape work showing exploited high and critical vulnerabilities up sharply with the interval between disclosure and exploitation collapsing. For healthcare the implication is uncomfortable but specific. Most hospital security programmes are built around human-speed response: a patch window measured in weeks, an alert triaged in hours, an out-of-hours escalation that reaches a person eventually. Detection and containment that depend on a human noticing within a shift do not work against an intrusion that finishes inside one. This is not a reason to buy an AI product; it is a reason to shorten the paths that already exist — automatic isolation on high-confidence detections, credential-tiering that stops lateral movement without a human decision, and patch timelines for internet-facing systems measured in days.
