Top Story

Thomson Reuters discloses a breach of its C-Track court case-management platform that reached Social Security numbers and sealed records across a dozen jurisdictions

On 2 September 2026 Thomson Reuters disclosed that an unauthorised party obtained files belonging to C-Track, the court case-management platform it supplies to appellate and trial courts. The access window ran from roughly 1 March to 29 June 2026 and was not discovered until 30 June; affected courts and Ontario’s Ministry of the Attorney General were notified between 23 and 27 July, with public disclosure following more than two months later. Reporting names courts in eleven US states — Alabama, Kentucky, Montana, Nevada, New Hampshire, North Dakota, Ohio, Pennsylvania, South Carolina, Tennessee and Wyoming — plus the US Virgin Islands and Ontario, Canada. The exposed material is names combined with one or more of Social Security numbers, driver’s licence numbers, dates of birth, medical information and health-insurance information, alongside case numbers, party addresses, phone numbers and charge descriptions. Thomson Reuters has also said that confidential, redacted or sealed court information may have been affected at some courts, which is the detail that separates this from an ordinary vendor breach: sealed records exist precisely because a court ordered them withheld, and juvenile matters, protective orders, victim identities and cooperating-witness material all live behind that seal. The company says the incident happened inside its own cloud environment and was not caused by the courts’ networks or systems, and points to backup database copies supplied to it for troubleshooting as what was accessed. It reports no evidence to date of fraud or misuse, considers C-Track safe to continue using, and is offering twelve months of credit monitoring through Experian and TransUnion. Nobody has yet disclosed an affected-individual count, how the intruder got in, or who was responsible; a criminal investigation is under way. For every firm that litigates in these jurisdictions, the practical question this week is not whether your own network held, but which of your clients’ identifiers and sealed filings sat in a vendor database you never contracted with directly.

The C-Track breach lands on the courts, not the vendor — North Dakota’s Supreme Court confirms a criminal investigation and every affected jurisdiction now owns a notification problem

The downstream half of the Thomson Reuters disclosure is the part legal IT has to work. Individual court systems began confirming their own exposure on 3 September: the North Dakota Supreme Court acknowledged that data associated with the court was affected through C-Track and that a criminal investigation is under way. The shape of this incident is the awkward one. The intrusion happened inside the vendor’s cloud environment, the data taken was backup database copies handed over for troubleshooting, and the courts had no visibility into any of it — yet the notification duty, the public statements and the standing with litigants all sit with the courts. Five months passed between the files being taken in March and the public learning of it in September, and more than two months passed between the courts being told in late July and disclosure. For firms, three things follow. First, matter-level exposure: pull the docket for every case filed in the named jurisdictions during and before the access window and identify which clients’ identifiers went into the case-management system. Second, sealed material: if a court has told you sealed or redacted content may have been affected, that is not something to wait out — it changes what you can represent to a client about the confidentiality of a filing, and it may warrant a motion. Third, third-party risk generally: the diligence question that would have caught this is not “is the vendor certified” but “where do production copies of our data go when support needs to reproduce a bug, and who is watching those copies.”

IDScan faces class actions over a claimed 153 million driver’s licence scans, with the FBI investigating and plaintiffs’ firms already filing

Identity-verification vendor IDScan is being sued over an alleged breach that reporting describes as covering roughly 153 million US and Canadian driver’s licence scans, about 10 million ID cards, 3 million travel documents and some 579,000 medical cards. The data surfaced through a dark-web service calling itself Nexus, which advertised lookups against the database; journalist Brian Krebs verified the material traced back to IDScan. Nexus is no longer reachable, but the underlying database is understood to remain in criminal hands. The FBI’s New Orleans field office is investigating, and complaints and class-action investigations have been announced by plaintiffs’ firms including Markovits, Stock & DeMarco and Hall Attorneys, with Louisiana — where IDScan is based — as the venue. IDScan had not commented publicly at the time of reporting. There are two distinct reasons this belongs on a professional-services page. The obvious one is defence-side and plaintiff-side workload: a claimed victim pool of this size is a mass-tort-shaped event, and firms on either side of it should expect intake volume, consolidation motions and preservation demands. The less obvious one is that identity-scanning vendors are embedded in visitor management, client onboarding and know-your-client workflows at plenty of firms and their clients, often through a reseller. If your reception desk scans a driver’s licence to issue a visitor badge, you should establish which vendor sits behind that scanner, what it retains and for how long. The allegations against IDScan are unproven.

Medium Supply Chain

A fourteen-hour hijack of Coder’s module registry pushed credential-stealing Terraform modules into developer workspaces at named consultancies and investors

Attackers compromised Coder’s Cloudflare infrastructure and inserted their own servers into the IP pool behind registry.coder.com, so that for a window on 31 August 2026 between roughly 07:35 and 21:45 UTC some requests for Terraform modules were served from attacker-controlled hosts instead. The substituted modules behaved as infostealers, harvesting provisioner environment variables and secrets, cloud and AI API keys, CI/CD credentials, SSH keys, OIDC tokens, database passwords and configuration secrets, and sending them to the lookalike domain coder-infra[.]com. Coder disclosed the incident in a GitHub security advisory, GHSA-vx42-ghc9-gw65, and named affected organisations including Dropbox, Palantir, Square, Mercedes-Benz, KKR, EnBW, US government bodies and defence contractors — while noting it cannot definitively identify every compromised deployment, because the attackers controlled the delivery path. Most law firms do not run Terraform. Plenty of consultancies, advisory practices and legal-technology teams inside larger professional-services groups do, and so do the managed providers that build client environments on their behalf. The relevant control here is not patching — nothing was unpatched. It is whether your build pipeline pins module versions and checksums, whether it can tell you what it pulled and from where during a given fourteen-hour window, and whether the credentials it holds can be rotated quickly. If your organisation consumed Coder registry modules that day, treat every secret reachable by the provisioner as disclosed.

High Campaign

A phishing operation across 46 countries is delivering remote-monitoring installers instead of malware — the same access route already used against law firms

ANY.RUN published analysis on 3 September 2026 of a phishing operation spanning 46 countries with the United States accounting for around 45 per cent of observed activity, tracked across 601 connected cases. The payload is not a novel implant. It is a legitimate remote monitoring and management installer, which gives the operator interactive control of the machine while looking to most endpoint tooling like ordinary IT administration. The lures are chosen to make a document click feel routine: Canada Revenue Agency tax forms, shipping and UPS notices, Adobe PDF prompts, US Social Security Administration letters, tax notices and invoices. Infrastructure discipline is the notable part — 425 kit URLs across 240 hosts, 94 per cent of them observed for only a single day, staged across Vercel, GitHub Pages, Netlify, compromised sites, Amazon S3, Cloudflare R2, DigitalOcean Spaces, Dropbox and GoFile. What holds constant is the fingerprint: shared assets such as a font1.woff2 file and a stable secure.html to project/*.zip delivery structure. The named target sectors are education, technology, government, banking, finance and manufacturing rather than legal specifically, but the technique is the one Silent Ransom Group has been running against law firms by phone for three years, now industrialised over email at scale. Tax-notice and invoice lures land naturally in an accounting or billing inbox. The defence is the same either way: application-control the RMM estate so only the tool you actually deploy can run, and alert on the first execution of anything else.

High Campaign

Still the sector’s defining campaign: Silent Ransom Group works law firms by phone, and sometimes by walking through the front door

Carried forward and re-checked this run, because nothing published in the past fortnight supersedes it. The group tracked as Silent Ransom Group, Luna Moth, Chatty Spider and UNC3753 has been active since 2022 and targeting law firms since spring 2023, and both an FBI FLASH alert and Google Mandiant reporting describe the campaign as still running. The tradecraft is almost entirely human. Operators call or email posing as IT support, talk a partner, paralegal or billing clerk into a remote-support session, and install a legitimate remote monitoring tool. Exfiltration runs over WinSCP or a hidden or renamed copy of Rclone, with data pushed to Google Drive, Microsoft OneDrive, external hard drives or USB storage. There is frequently no encryption stage at all — the leverage is the client material itself, which in a firm means privileged files that cannot be restored away. The outlier detail, and the one that breaks most legal threat models, is that operators have turned up in person, claiming they need to image a device or take a backup to deal with the effects of a phishing email. Halcyon’s research centre ranked law firms fourth most-targeted industry in early 2026, attributing it to the sensitivity of client data, regulatory pressure to resolve incidents quickly and a perceived willingness to pay. The FBI’s own recommendations are unglamorous and correct: verify visitor identity against ID, deploy phishing-resistant multi-factor authentication, restrict remote access and external-drive permissions on sensitive systems, and train staff to recognise the pretext.

Researchers tie an extortion crew’s haul to misconfigured client portals rather than any exploit — a direct read-across to firm extranets

Fortra published analysis on 14 August 2026, reported more widely on 26 August, corroborating data-theft claims made by the extortion group ExfilSquad from late July. The researchers concluded the exfiltrated material appears to relate to misconfiguration of Microsoft Power Pages portals, where public read permissions had been left in place over data held in Microsoft Dynamics 365 behind them. The group claimed material from roughly fifteen entities, with named claims including the City of Atlanta, Allstate, the UK Department for Education, Frontier Airlines and Microsoft; individual claims should be treated as claims rather than confirmed. The finding that matters is the negative one: investigators found no evidence of vulnerability exploitation and no ransomware deployment. Someone simply read what the portal was configured to let anyone read. Professional-services firms run exactly this pattern under a different name — client extranets, deal rooms, matter portals, secure-upload pages and audit-request sites, frequently built quickly for one engagement and then left running. They are usually stood up outside the change process by a practice group with a deadline, they are indexed by search engines, and permissions on them are rarely re-reviewed after go-live. An enumeration exercise against your own portal estate, checking what an unauthenticated visitor can retrieve, is cheaper than the alternative and needs no vendor patch to carry out.

PaperCut exploitation has moved from scanning to hands-on-keyboard intrusion — and print management is one of the few servers almost every firm runs

PaperCut warned on 27 August 2026 that two flaws in its NG and MF print-management products were being exploited, and CISA added both to the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog on 31 August with a federal remediation deadline of 14 September. CVE-2026-82078 and CVE-2026-81578 both allow an unauthenticated attacker to bypass authentication and reach remote code execution. watchTowr, which has been tracking the activity, described the escalation plainly: what began as exploratory probing to find vulnerable systems is now real-world exploitation with hands-on-keyboard interaction. ShadowServer data puts more than a thousand PaperCut NG/MF instances directly on the internet. PaperCut shipped emergency patches, then issued a second patch after the first was bypassed, with a consolidated official release still in development — so patching once is not sufficient evidence of safety. Any internet-exposed instance that was unpatched during this period should be assumed compromised rather than merely at risk. For law and accounting firms the exposure is unusually direct. Print management is not a peripheral system in this sector: it holds the cost-recovery data that binds documents to matters and clients, it sits on the internal network with service accounts that touch directories and file shares, and secure print release means the server has seen a copy of what people printed. A print server is also the last box anyone thinks to check when hunting, which is precisely why an attacker with code execution on it can sit quietly.

Two separate NetScaler flaws are now being exploited, and NetScaler is the remote-access front door for a large share of firms

The Citrix NetScaler situation changed twice inside this window. CVE-2026-8452, a memory-overflow issue that Citrix patched on 30 June and described in terms of unpredictable behaviour and denial of service, turned out to be worse than advertised: watchTowr published proof-of-concept detail on 14 August showing unauthenticated remote code execution, in-the-wild exploitation followed shortly afterwards with web shells and discovery commands observed by Previdian and Defused, and CISA added it to KEV on 26 August with a remediation deadline of 29 August. For a firm, an authentication bypass on the remote-access appliance hands an attacker the same interactive session that Silent Ransom Group spends weeks on the phone trying to talk someone into granting. This is the patch to do first if you have not.

High Advisory

Google patches a sixth actively-exploited Chrome zero-day this year — a browser update is the cheapest control on this page

Google shipped a Chrome update on 4 September 2026 fixing CVE-2026-85046, a type-confusion vulnerability in the V8 JavaScript and WebAssembly engine, and confirmed that an exploit for it exists in the wild. Type confusion means the engine can be tricked into treating one kind of object as another, which is a reliable route to memory corruption and, from there, to code execution inside the renderer — typically triggered by nothing more than visiting a page carrying crafted JavaScript. The fix is in 152.0.7977.82/.83 on Windows and macOS and 152.0.7977.82 on Linux, and the release also addresses nine other high-severity issues including use-after-free and out-of-bounds memory bugs. The flaw was reported by researcher Salvatore Gulizia. This is the sixth Chrome bug fixed this year after evidence of exploitation. Nothing about it is legal-specific, which is exactly the point: the browser is the single application every fee earner, analyst and consultant in the firm uses all day, it is where client portals, court filing systems, research platforms and webmail all live, and it is the one piece of software where a forced restart is a minor inconvenience rather than a change-control exercise. Renderer-sandbox escape usually requires a second bug, so this alone is not automatic full compromise — but browser zero-days are routinely chained, and firms running Chrome or Chromium-derived browsers should push the update rather than wait for users to relaunch.

High Advisory

The rest of the early-September KEV traffic: a forged-admin-token flaw in JFrog Artifactory and an unauthenticated SQL injection in Switchvox phone systems

Two more exploited flaws from the same few days deserve a line each, because both hit systems professional-services organisations run without thinking of them as security-critical. CVE-2026-82329 is a critical authentication bypass in JFrog Artifactory’s default configuration: watchTowr observed attackers minting themselves administrative tokens, unauthenticated and over the network. An attacker with admin on Artifactory can enumerate users and groups, read artifacts, change security settings and poison packages that build systems downstream then consume — and JFrog notes that upgrading the binary does not by itself invalidate a token already issued, so patching without revocation leaves the door open. Fixed versions released on 28 August include 7.111.21, 7.117.28, 7.125.20, 7.133.29, 7.146.38 and 7.161.20; JFrog Cloud environments were already protected. CVE-2026-9586 is an unauthenticated SQL injection in Sangoma Switchvox SMB Edition 8.3, found by Horizon3 and Security Risk Advisors in April and patched in 8.4.0.2 on 14 July. Active exploitation was observed from 30 August, with attackers deploying reverse shells, enumerating processes and installing second-stage malware including apparent cryptominers; roughly 4,000 instances are internet-exposed per Shodan. Switchvox is a small-business phone system, which is to say it is exactly the kind of thing a mid-sized firm inherited from a telecoms reseller years ago and has never patched. Policy Regulation

A €500,000 CNIL fine sets out, in order, the four control failures a regulator will fault after a breach

France’s data-protection authority CNIL announced a €500,000 fine on 3 September 2026 against Hôpital privé de la Loire in Saint-Étienne, part of the Ramsay Santé group, over a summer 2025 breach affecting 524,867 patients and 202,246 trusted third parties — 727,113 people in total. A single compromised doctor’s account gave the intruder system-wide access to the electronic patient record. The findings are worth reading as a checklist rather than as healthcare news, because they are the same four things any regulator or opposing counsel will ask about: external users were not required to connect over VPN or to use multi-factor authentication; access controls did not limit how many records one account could reach; there was no real-time monitoring or alerting, so the intruder operated undetected for days; and the organisation failed to notify the 202,246 third parties directly. The cited breaches were of Article 32 on security of processing and Article 34 on communication to data subjects. For advisory and privacy practices, this is a usefully concrete data point on how a European regulator weighs a breach where the attacker — a teenager using the alias “Marak” — claimed the data was neither sold nor published: the penalty attached to the controls and the notification failure, not to downstream harm. For firms as controllers in their own right, the second and fourth findings are the uncomfortable ones. Broad standing access to a document management system, and a habit of notifying the client but not the individuals in the file, are both common in this sector.

Enforcement decision — fine issued, not a proposal No CVE — regulatory finding on security of processing and notification
No patch — access control, MFA, monitoring and notification practice Action: Test your own position against the four findings: is remote access by external users gated behind MFA and a managed tunnel; can any single account reach far more client material than its role needs; would anyone notice days of unusual bulk access; and does your incident plan identify the individuals in a file rather than only the client organisation. Fix the access-scope question first — it is the one that turns a single compromised account into a firm-wide breach.

The SRA’s AI warning notice still stands as the clearest statement of what a regulator expects firms to be able to evidence

Carried forward from the previous edition and still current: the UK Solicitors Regulation Authority issued a warning notice on 17 August 2026 after identifying two distinct failures among solicitors — fabricated case law generated by AI tools being put before courts, and confidential client information being entered into publicly available AI services. The SRA’s position is that solicitors remain fully accountable for AI output however it was produced, and that firms must be able to demonstrate human oversight and risk-based governance over how these tools are used. It is easy to file this as a professional-conduct matter, but the second half lands on firm IT. Client confidences typed into a public model is a data-confidentiality and acceptable-use problem, and the evidence a regulator expects — that oversight and governance exist and are working — is evidence IT is usually the only function able to produce: which AI services are reachable from firm devices, which are sanctioned and under what contractual data terms, what is logged, and what data-loss controls stop client material leaving through a browser tab. Firms outside England and Wales should read the notice as a preview rather than someone else’s problem. Nothing published in the current window supersedes it, and the direction of travel across regulators has not changed.

A Russian national is indicted in California over a decade-old campaign that infected 80,000 freelancers through macro-laden spreadsheets

A federal grand jury in California indicted Searzhudin Tamirlanovich Aktulaev, a 40-year-old Russian national, over a campaign that reporting dates to June 2016 through November 2017. Prosecutors allege he created 255 fake accounts on a freelance employment platform based in the Northern District of California and sent Microsoft Excel attachments carrying malicious macros to roughly 80,000 freelancers, installing the TVRAT remote access trojan — also tracked as TeamSpy and TVSPY — and DarkVNC, then using that access to take e-commerce credentials and personal information. About half the infected victims were in the United States. Aktulaev was arrested at Larnaca Airport in Cyprus in May 2025, was later extradited, and is in federal custody with a court appearance scheduled for 5 October. The charges are allegations and he has not been convicted. Two points carry over to professional-services risk management. The first is the timeline: nine years from campaign to indictment, and more than a year from arrest to public charging, which is a realistic picture of what enforcement means for a firm weighing whether deterrence belongs in a threat model. The second is the target selection. Contract lawyers, document reviewers, forensic accountants and interim consultants engaged through freelance platforms sit outside the firm’s managed estate but inside its matters, and a compromised freelancer’s device is a route into client material that no endpoint policy of yours covers.
