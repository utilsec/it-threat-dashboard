PHP2Shell is now exploited and in CISA’s KEV with a 29 October federal deadline — Palo Alto reports attacks on internet-exposed PAN-OS management interfaces, and a Nextgov review counted about 900 government websites showing signs of PHP

CISA added both PHP2Shell flaws, CVE-2026-109618 and CVE-2026-109619, to its Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog on 26 October with a 29 October deadline for federal civilian agencies, a reversal of its position the previous week that it had not observed exploitation. Researchers at Perplexity (Adel Karimi), with Expel’s Marcus Hutchins concurring, confirmed working remote code execution against a honeypot over 24–25 October. No victim organisation has been named, and CISA did not describe the activity behind the listing. Palo Alto Networks’ advisory for CVE-2026-109618, raised to Critical on 27 October and updated on 28 October, reports active exploitation targeting PAN-OS management interfaces exposed to the internet on PA-Series, VM-Series and Panorama, through the web interface and XML API (GlobalProtect is not affected). Palo Alto scores the flaw CVSS v4.0 9.2, or 5.2 where management access is restricted, names no victims, and is rolling out hotfixes between 21 and 30 October, with some due on 29–30 October. Sophos and Palo Alto’s Unit 42 withdrew earlier reports of successful exploitation; Unit 42 said it could not confirm PHP2Shell was the entry point in an attack on a European government agency, so this page does not count that case.

The fixes. The PHP Group rates both flaws CVSS v4.0 9.2. PHP 8.2.35, 8.3.36, 8.4.27 and 8.5.12 fix them (21 October), and the Windows builds were re-released on 26 October to bundle Brotli 1.2.1, whose own exploited decoder flaw entered KEV on 27 October (covered in Critical Advisories). Amazon says attempts began on 17 October, four days before the fixes. CISA published a SIGMA detection rule on 23 October, and its alert says systems that cannot be updated promptly should be taken offline. A Nextgov/FCW review published on 27 October found roughly 900 Shodan results tied to federal, state and local government websites showing signs of PHP use, and noted that many may already be patched. A third flaw, CVE-2026-115560, affects only an unusual session configuration, has no reported exploitation and has a mitigation but no fix: set session.upload_progress.enabled=0.

Why it leads. It is the one issue this week that reaches almost every agency at once: PHP sits behind council, court and agency websites, often on hosting a contractor runs, and the PAN-OS route puts the same bug on firewalls many agencies use as their perimeter (editorial assessment). It replaces the Bankruptcy Noticing Center outage, which was restored on 27 October (covered in Incidents), because exploitation is confirmed, a federal deadline falls on 29 October and the fix is in agencies’ own hands. No government compromise through PHP2Shell has been confirmed.

The Bankruptcy Noticing Center that BAE Systems runs for US courts is back online after about two and a half weeks — the judiciary declared a “Bankruptcy Rules emergency” on 22 October, and the cause and data impact remain undisclosed

Update, 27 October: The Administrative Office of the US Courts updated its notice to say BAE Systems has told it the cybersecurity issues are resolved and that the BNC system “is now back online and available for use.” The same notice records that on 22 October the Judicial Conference declared a “Bankruptcy Rules emergency” under Federal Rule of Bankruptcy Procedure 9038, giving bankruptcy judges wider authority to extend deadlines, toll time periods and issue related orders to deal with the outage and the return to normal operations. The update gives no restoration time, backlog figure, cause or statement on data.

What happened. The BNC, which produces and sends notices and orders to parties in bankruptcy cases, stopped doing so after BAE told the courts of a cybersecurity incident late on 9 October; bnc.uscourts.gov, the notices websites and the National Creditor Registration Service were also unavailable. Courts worked around it with their own administrative orders — the Northern District of Florida entered its second on 20 October — and courts in at least 20 states posted notices, Cybernews reported. BAE has said the incident was isolated to the BNC system. It has not said what happened, whether data was taken or who was responsible, and this page does not call it ransomware.

Why it matters. One contractor-run platform stopped a statutory court function nationwide for about two and a half weeks, and it took an emergency rules declaration to protect parties from missed deadlines. Agencies that are creditors, trustees or parties in bankruptcy cases should expect delayed notices to arrive and check what each district has extended or tolled. It led the previous two editions and moves here now that service is restored.

Columbia, South Carolina, shuts down city systems after its IT team finds suspicious activity — police, fire, 911 and water keep running, and state police are leading the investigation

The City of Columbia told Recorded Future News, in a report published on 22 October, that its IT department had identified suspicious activity and called in the South Carolina Law Enforcement Division (SLED), which is leading the investigation. Why it matters. Columbia is South Carolina’s capital, and the pattern matches Vicksburg earlier this month (covered below): the resident-facing portal goes offline first while emergency and water operations keep running. Keeping those services separate from a city’s general IT is what lets it shut systems down quickly without stopping 911 or water (editorial assessment).

The UK Legal Aid Agency breach is wider than first thought: records back to 1978, probation data and the names of judges and lawyers in Crown Court trials were taken, the Ministry of Justice says

What is confirmed. In a written statement to Parliament, published with a Legal Aid Agency update on Wednesday 14 October and reported on 15 October, Justice Minister Sarah Sackman said forensic work on the April–May 2025 intrusion found more data had been stolen than first believed. Most records relate to applications, claims or payments from January 2007 to 16 May 2025, but about 250,000 records are older, mostly from the 1990s and 2000s, the earliest verified one from 1978. Newly listed data includes probation records (names, dates of birth, nationalities and case references), financial assessments of defendants and their partners, bank and property details, some partially redacted payment-card details, and the names of judges, lawyers and court reporters in Crown Court trials. The ministry says it has no indication the data has been published or shared, and that it has strengthened authentication and access controls, improved monitoring and begun retiring or isolating higher-risk legacy systems, working with the National Crime Agency, the NCSC and the ICO. Claimed: the extortionists say they hold data on more than 2 million people; the government has given no count and has a long-standing policy of not paying.

Why it matters. The scope grew because old data was still there: records nearly five decades old sat on systems reachable in 2025. For justice agencies the exposed names of judges, lawyers and court reporters, and the probation details, are a personal-safety issue as well as a privacy one.
