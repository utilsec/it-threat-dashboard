Top Story

UTSA pulls systems offline days before classes start after IT staff spot threat activity

The University of Texas at San Antonio confirmed that its IT staff detected threat activity on university systems over the weekend of 15–16 August and responded by proactively taking a broad set of systems offline — including campus phone service — to contain it. The outage disrupted course registration and student payment processing in the final days before the fall term began, one of the worst possible moments for those particular services. UTSA’s chief technology officer said the threat was contained at the network edge and that there is no evidence at this stage that data was accessed or exfiltrated; no ransomware group or other actor has claimed the intrusion. Treat the containment findings as the university’s current assessment rather than a completed forensic conclusion — early “no evidence of exfiltration” statements are routinely revised as investigations mature. What makes this worth leading with is the decision itself: UTSA chose a deliberate, self-inflicted availability outage over the risk of letting an intruder move laterally, and it made that trade during the start-of-term window when registration, bursar and identity systems are under peak load and least tolerant of downtime. Every district and campus should know in advance who is authorized to make that call, which systems can be severed without cascading failures, and what the manual fallback looks like for registration and payments if the answer is “pull the plug.”

Critical Incident

VMware vCenter RCE added to CISA KEV as the mass-exploitation campaign keeps running

CVE-2026-59310, a directory-traversal flaw in the vCenter Server syslog service disclosed by Broadcom on 29 July in VMSA-2026-0006, was added to the CISA Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog on 18 August with a 21 August federal remediation deadline. It carries CVSS 9.8, gives unauthenticated remote code execution and has no workaround. Researchers saw the first victims connecting to attacker infrastructure on 3 August, and roughly 95% of an eventual 361 victim IPs across 47 countries had appeared by 5 August, concentrated in Germany, the United States, Turkey, Iran and France. The observed chain is path traversal to root on vCenter, then a cron job planted for reverse-SSH persistence, with ransomware derived from leaked Babuk source deployed in some cases. This is a cross-sector campaign rather than an education-specific attack, but universities and larger districts run vCenter to manage the virtualization clusters behind research computing, ERP, SIS and email — one exposed instance can expose guest VMs, datastores and often backups at once.

High Incident

District staff and dependent data turns up in a 235 GB leak from a health-plan intermediary

A 235 GB dump attributed to the Chaos extortion group and posted after a 24-hour ultimatum went unanswered contains employee and dependent personal information belonging to client organizations of provider-network firm Healthcare Highways — among them Leander Independent School District in Texas. A researcher who inspected the dump reported protected health information, prescription records and employee/dependent PII inside; Chaos’s attribution of the data to Healthcare Highways is the group’s own claim, and the leak has not been confirmed by the named organizations. The lesson for districts and campuses is structural rather than technical. Benefits administration, provider networks, third-party administrators and wellness vendors hold staff and family records that sit well outside the SIS and LMS perimeter district security teams actually monitor, and none of it is covered by the FERPA-shaped controls most education IT programs are built around. A breach at that layer produces notification duties and staff-trust damage the district did not cause and often learns about from a researcher rather than the vendor.

Medium Incident

Contract analyst at a school-facilities software vendor sentenced over a $2.5M extortion campaign

Cameron Curry, a contract data analyst engaged by Brightly Software — the Siemens-owned firm whose asset, maintenance and facilities management products are widely deployed across school districts — was convicted on six counts of extortion and sentenced to two years in prison plus supervised release. Prosecutors said he took corporate and employee data during a 2023 engagement and later sent more than 60 extortion emails demanding roughly $2.5 million, ultimately netting only $7,540. The case is a concrete precedent rather than a live threat, and that is exactly its value to education IT: it involves a trusted contractor at a vendor with deep, routine access to district facilities and operational data, not an external intruder. Districts and campuses tend to scope vendor risk reviews around the SIS and LMS while facilities, maintenance, transportation and energy-management platforms — which know building layouts, access schedules and staff assignments — are onboarded with far lighter scrutiny and often with standing contractor accounts that outlive the engagement.

High Research

DOJ indictment describes 13 years of research-network theft across 144 US universities

A superseding indictment unsealed by the Department of Justice charges 17 members of the Mabna Institute over an IRGC-linked intrusion campaign running since 2013. The charging documents describe targeting of 144 US universities alongside 42 private companies and at least five federal and state agencies, with more than 100,000 professor accounts targeted, roughly 8,000 successfully compromised, and 31 or more terabytes of research and intellectual property exfiltrated. The tradecraft described is unglamorous and durable: credential phishing aimed at faculty, then reuse of those accounts to pull material from library systems and research repositories at scale. Charges are allegations, and the conduct described is historical rather than evidence of a live campaign against any specific institution today — but it is the clearest current statement of sustained state-aligned interest in university research networks and library credential access. For campus IT the practical read is that faculty accounts are high-value targets in their own right, that federated library access is a data-egress path worth instrumenting, and that phishing-resistant MFA for research staff is not a compliance nicety.

High Research

CISA and FBI refresh the Medusa advisory: 500+ victims and exploits used before disclosure

CISA and the FBI updated joint advisory AA25-071A on 18 August. The cumulative Medusa ransomware victim count passed 500 critical-infrastructure organizations as of April 2026, up from roughly 300 in March 2025 — about 200 new victims in a year. Named target sectors include healthcare, critical manufacturing, the defense industrial base, government facilities, IT and financial services. The finding education IT should act on is about timing: the agencies report that Medusa weaponizes newly disclosed vulnerabilities within roughly 24 hours, and has been observed using exploits up to a week before public disclosure, because the group buys access and exploits rather than developing them. That breaks the informal patch calendar many districts and campuses run on, where change windows are scheduled around academic terms and a vendor advisory is treated as the starting gun. If an internet-facing system cannot be patched inside a day, the compensating controls — exposure reduction, MFA everywhere, tested offline backups and egress monitoring — are what actually carry the risk.

Active — 500+ cumulative critical-infrastructure victims; ~24h weaponization Advisory AA25-071A refresh — multiple CVEs, no single flaw
Mitigations, not a patch — see the joint advisory Action: Shorten emergency-patch SLAs for internet-facing systems to under 24h, verify offline backup restores before term peak, and enforce MFA on all remote access including contractor and vendor paths.
Suggested SLA: Emergency patch-window and backup-restore validation — 14 days Owner:

Critical Advisory

Four new CISA KEV additions with a 21 August deadline — including a macOS Screen Sharing flaw

CISA added four actively exploited vulnerabilities to the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog on 18 August, all with a 21 August federal remediation deadline: CVE-2026-33824, a double-free in the Microsoft Windows IKE service extensions (IKEEXT) leading to remote code execution; CVE-2026-59310, the VMware vCenter path traversal covered above; CVE-2026-55040, a weak-authentication security-feature bypass in Microsoft SharePoint; and CVE-2026-65400, an improper-authentication flaw in Apple macOS Screen Sharing. Federal deadlines are not binding on schools or universities, but they are a reasonable urgency signal. The macOS entry deserves particular attention in education: Mac fleets are far more common in classrooms, labs, design and media programs, and among faculty than in a typical enterprise, and Screen Sharing is frequently left enabled for remote support of exactly those machines. Confirm whether it is exposed beyond the management VLAN before assuming it is not reachable.

Critical Advisory

Ray code-injection flaw added to KEV — research-computing clusters are the exposed population

CVE-2025-62593, a code-injection flaw in the Ray distributed-computing framework carrying CVSS 9.4, was added to CISA KEV on 17 August with a 20 August federal deadline. The underlying design issue is that Ray ships with no authentication on critical endpoints, so an attack does not need network position at all: a malicious webpage or ad combined with DNS rebinding can reach a developer’s local Ray environment and execute code there. It is fixed in version 2.52.0. This one lands squarely on higher education. Ray is a staple of AI and machine-learning research computing, and academic clusters are frequently stood up by graduate students or lab staff on defaults, on networks that are deliberately open for collaboration, and without the segmentation an enterprise ML platform would get. Ray dashboards and head nodes reachable from a general campus network — or from a browser on a researcher’s laptop — should be treated as compromised-until-verified given confirmed exploitation.

It was exploited within roughly 24 hours of Rapid7 publishing a proof-of-concept on 11 August, and allows unauthenticated file disclosure and data modification. High Advisory

Windows Task Host privilege-escalation flaw now used by ransomware gangs — hunt for stragglers

CISA updated its KEV entry for CVE-2025-60710 on 18 August to note that the flaw is now being abused by ransomware operators. It is a link-following privilege-escalation weakness in the Windows Task Host that takes a low-privileged local user to SYSTEM on Windows 11 and Windows Server 2025. Microsoft patched it in November 2025 and it entered KEV in April 2026, so this is not a new patch cycle — it is a signal to go find the machines that were missed. That framing matters more in education than most sectors, because campus and district estates carry long tails of lab machines, cart-based devices, kiosks, departmental servers and faculty-managed systems that fall outside the main patch-management scope and only get touched between terms. Ransomware crews adopting an eight-month-old local escalation is precisely a bet that those stragglers exist.

Windows zero-day exploited by Lazarus Group in Operation Dream Job, added to CISA KEV

CVE-2026-68820, a use-after-free in the Windows Ancillary Function Driver for WinSock (AFD.sys), CVSS 7.0, is a local elevation-of-privilege flaw patched in the August 2026 Patch Tuesday — it is local privilege escalation, not unauthenticated remote code execution. CISA added it to KEV on 11 August with a 25 August federal deadline, so unlike most items on this page its clock is still running. Check Point ties exploitation to North Korea’s Lazarus Group in Operation Dream Job, using trojanized PDF viewers and DLL side-loading to deploy a backdoor dubbed Troy against defense and aerospace targets. Cross-sector, not education-specific, but it patches essentially every Windows endpoint on campus, and a nation-state actor chaining local escalation after a phishing foothold is directly relevant to universities with defense-research-adjacent programs.

CISA releases new K-12 cybersecurity guidance, citing 34 sector ransomware attacks

CISA published two free guides on 14 August aimed at school and district leaders — one on building a foundational cybersecurity program, the other on sustaining it — covering credential protection, device security, tested backups, incident-response planning and adoption of the NIST Cybersecurity Framework. The agency cited 34 ransomware attacks against K-12 and higher-education organizations in the first half of 2026 and pointed to March’s breach at Alamo Heights ISD in Texas, which affected 26,629 people, as an example of the stakes. The guidance arrives as the Multi-State ISAC, long a source of free threat intelligence for schools, has lost roughly 70% of its membership following federal funding cuts — part of why CISA is publishing direct, self-serve guidance rather than relying solely on that channel. Education-specific, not cross-sector.

Policy Policy

Proposed legislation would give CISA a K-12 threat exchange and a national school incident catalogue

Proposed federal legislation introduced in late July would task CISA with running a K-12-specific cyber threat information exchange and maintaining a national catalogue of school cyber incidents. It remains a bill rather than law, so nothing is required of districts today, but the design addresses a real and well-documented gap: state-level breach reporting for school districts is inconsistent in scope, threshold and timeliness, which means nobody — including districts themselves — has a reliable national picture of what is actually happening to K-12 systems. A shared catalogue would also give small districts something they cannot generate alone, namely peer-comparable incident data to justify security budget requests to boards. Worth tracking alongside the shrinking Multi-State ISAC membership noted above, since both point at the same question of who supplies threat intelligence to under-resourced districts.
