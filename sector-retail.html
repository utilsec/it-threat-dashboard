Top Story

A stolen Cloudflare key let attackers rewrite Brevo’s embedded marketing scripts for more than four hours — shoppers were shown ClickFix lures and logged-in WordPress admins were served a backdoor plugin

Brevo, the email-marketing and CRM platform formerly called Sendinblue, has confirmed a supply-chain compromise that turned its own embedded scripts against the sites carrying them. Attackers obtained a long-lived Cloudflare API key with full account permissions that had been hardcoded in Brevo’s application source, and used it to deploy a malicious Cloudflare Worker that rewrote responses at the CDN edge on 14 September, from about 16:07 to 20:30 UTC. The Worker altered the SDK loader and the Conversations chat-widget script that customers embed, plus Brevo-hosted pages including sibforms.com form hosting, and stripped Content-Security-Policy headers on the way through; Brevo says its origin servers and files were never modified, which is why standard integrity checks missed it. Sansec, which found the attack, estimates more than 100,000 sites were exposed. Ordinary visitors were shown a fake “Cloudflare, verify you are human” ClickFix page telling them to paste and run a command; visitors with a live WordPress admin session had the script try to install a plugin called “Web Media Optimizer” as a persistent backdoor. It follows a linked 10 September incident in which a mis-scoped SAML sign-on path exposed 138 Brevo customer accounts. No count of backdoored sites or infected visitors has been published. For merchants, a newsletter pop-up or chat widget loaded on every page, checkout included, has the same power as your own code: check WordPress sites for plugins installed on 14 September and rotate admin passwords, and treat any PC where someone ran a “verify you are human” command as infected.

High Campaign

A fraud network is running more than 119,000 fake storefronts to harvest shoppers’ card details

Researchers reported on 8 September 2026 a fraud operation named DoppelCart that runs over 119,000 domains hosting fake online shops whose purpose is to collect payment card details at checkout. The scale is the point: at that volume the operators can rotate through domains faster than blocklists and takedown processes work, so any individual site being killed costs them nothing. For legitimate merchants the exposure runs in two directions. The first is direct fraud pressure — cards harvested from these sites are used elsewhere, which shows up in your chargeback and authorisation-decline rates without anything having gone wrong on your own platform. The second is brand impersonation, and it is the one merchants usually discover late. Storefronts of this kind routinely copy real product imagery, descriptions, logos and even policy pages, so customers who are defrauded believe they bought from you, complain to you, and post about you. Practically, that makes brand monitoring a security function rather than a marketing one, and it makes your customer-service inbox a threat-intelligence feed: complaints about an order you have no record of are usually the first signal that your brand is being cloned.

The allegation is therefore no longer an allegation, although the number of individuals affected has still not been established. For any merchant that scans identity documents — age-restricted sales, returns-fraud controls, rental and check-in desks — the lesson is that the scanner vendor, not the store, became the aggregation point, and the customer relationship is still the retailer’s.

A new ransomware strain, Settra, hit a retail victim after killing EDR with a vulnerable driver and wiping the recovery partition

Huntress and incident-response firm MoxFive have documented a new ransomware family, Settra, in two intrusions: a consumer-services and retail victim in July 2026 and a manufacturer in September. The playbook is built to make recovery slow rather than to be novel. The operators ran the MeshAgent remote-management agent, renamed mvtcs.exe, for persistent hands-on access; used a bring-your-own-vulnerable-driver technique to shut down endpoint detection; cleared Windows event logs; and disabled the Windows Recovery Environment and removed the recovery partition before encrypting. The ransomware binaries were named after the victim’s own domain, which defeats simple filename-based detection. Initial access is suspected to have been compromised VPN credentials, but Huntress has not confirmed it, and Settra’s lineage and affiliates have not been identified. Two victims is a small sample and this is vendor research rather than a campaign with a published victim count, so it sits in Medium. It earns a retail read because of the recovery angle: a store estate with hundreds of near-identical Windows endpoints — back-office PCs, kiosks, till servers — is usually rebuilt from local recovery partitions and images, and Settra removes exactly that. The defensive priorities follow: stop the driver and the unapproved remote-management agent, and make sure what you restore from lives somewhere the endpoint cannot reach.

Medium Research

Phishing has started targeting passkeys themselves — users are tricked into registering an attacker’s passkey on their own Microsoft account

Researchers described two related campaigns against Microsoft 365 and Microsoft cloud accounts on 13 September. The first is volume fraud: over a million scam emails aimed at ACH and wire redirection, the familiar accounts-payable attack running at scale. The second is the one worth reading carefully. Rather than trying to steal a password or intercept a one-time code, the attackers use passkey-themed social engineering to walk the user through authorising an attacker-controlled passkey on their own account. That is notable because passkeys are the control organisations are being told to adopt precisely because they resist phishing — and the technique does not break the cryptography at all. It attacks the enrolment step, where a human is asked to approve the addition of a new credential and has no easy way to tell whose device is on the other end. Once registered, the attacker holds a legitimate authenticator and no longer needs the victim. For retail the two campaigns arrive at the same place from different directions: accounts payable pays a supplier invoice to the wrong account, or a head-office mailbox with access to supplier banking details is taken over cleanly. Passkey rollout is still the right direction of travel; the finding is that registration needs to be treated as a privileged event rather than a self-service one.

Microsoft documents two help-desk vishing crews that route stolen sessions through personal devices into the Microsoft Graph API

Microsoft researchers, reported by Dark Reading on 10 September, detailed at least two initial-access-broker clusters — tracked as Storm-3032 and Storm-3121 — running voice-phishing campaigns since May 2026. The operators telephone staff while impersonating the internal IT help desk and steer the victim’s personal device to a look-alike Microsoft sign-in page on a domain built to read as legitimate, following a “company.add-passkey.com” pattern, where credentials and session tokens are captured. The access is then used against the Microsoft Graph API to enumerate and pull data out of SharePoint, OneDrive and Exchange in small, periodic transfers rather than one large export. Access is reported to be sold on to extortion groups including ShinyHunters. Microsoft says it has not yet tied these campaigns to a confirmed corporate breach, so the technique is documented but the outcomes are not attributed — carry it as a campaign to detect, not as a breach that has happened to you. The retail relevance is direct. This page already carries a retailer whose customer database was taken out of a cloud analytics tenant by ShinyHunters, and the sector’s mix of large distributed workforces, heavy seasonal hiring and help desks that reset passwords by telephone for people they will never meet is exactly the ground these crews work. Because the phishing happens on a personal phone, controls on the managed estate see nothing; because the exfiltration is Graph API traffic inside a valid session, it does not look like an intrusion, it looks like the user.

The ScreenConnect file-transfer flaw has a CVE, a patch and a passed federal deadline — and confirmed worm-like spread between connected machines

ConnectWise has closed out the ScreenConnect issue it disclosed without a fix on 7 September. It is now tracked as CVE-2026-84869, a missing-authorisation and improper-privilege-management flaw scored CVSS 9.9, fixed in version 26.6.5. ConnectWise has confirmed exploitation and CISA added the flaw to the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog on 11 September, giving federal agencies three days to remediate under Binding Operational Directive 26-04 — one of the shortest windows CISA has set. The attack pattern is the one Huntress described earlier this month, now tied to a named vulnerability: attackers abuse legitimate remote-support sessions to transfer and execute files without the host confirming, then socially engineer a victim into running a modified ScreenConnect client that detects active sessions and propagates VBScript payloads to the machines connected to them. Where 26.6.5 cannot be deployed immediately, ConnectWise’s interim mitigation is to clear the TransferFiles permission. For a retail estate this remains one of the sharpest cross-industry items on the page, because remote support is the path by which head office, the point-of-sale vendor and the managed service provider all reach the store. An estate of near-identical tills hanging off one support tool is precisely the topology worm-like propagation is built for, and the social-engineering step means the patch alone does not settle it: a staff member persuaded to install a client is still an unplanned instance in your environment. No victim counts or revised guidance surfaced between 15 and 20 September.

Cisco ISE has a CVSS 10.0 zero-day under active exploitation — the policy engine that decides which device lands on which store network

Cisco has disclosed CVE-2026-76460, an authentication bypass in Identity Services Engine and ISE Passive Identity Connector scored CVSS 10.0, and says it is aware of active exploitation. The flaw is insufficient authentication control on an API endpoint: an unauthenticated remote attacker can bypass the web management interface and obtain root command execution. ISE and ISE-PIC 3.0 to 3.5 are affected; fixes are in 3.1 Patch 12, 3.2 Patch 11, 3.3 Patch 12, 3.4 Patch 7 and 3.5 Patch 4, and there is no workaround. CISA added it to the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog on 16 September with a 19 September deadline, which has now passed. No actor has been named. Cisco warns that attackers may remove or hide evidence of exploitation, and its guidance for affected nodes goes beyond patching: re-image, restore from configuration backups, review access.log for suspicious usernames and cross-check network and firewall logs. It is the third exploited ISE flaw since June 2025 and Cisco’s second exploited zero-day in a week, after the email gateway. VulnCheck notes that root on ISE lets an attacker change network-access policy, pull stored credentials, delete logs and move laterally. In a retail chain ISE is often what enforces the segmentation PCI DSS scoping relies on — which network a till, a payment terminal, a handheld scanner or a guest phone joins at every store — so an attacker who can rewrite that policy can quietly place their own device on the cardholder-data segment.

GitLab disclosed and patched CVE-2026-85706 on 11 September: an unauthenticated path traversal in the repository commits interface that lets a remote attacker read arbitrary files from a self-managed GitLab server in a single HTTP request. It is scored CVSS 10.0. Affected versions run from 18.7 through 19.1.8, 19.2 through 19.2.6, and 19.3 through 19.3.2; the fixes are 19.1.8, 19.2.6 and 19.3.2. A crafted email is enough to get root on a Cisco Secure Email Gateway — CVE-2026-76461 is being exploited and there is no workaround

The EU Cyber Resilience Act’s 24-hour reporting duty is now live — and it captures retailers who put their own brand on connected products

The incident-reporting provisions of the EU Cyber Resilience Act took effect in the week of 11 September. Manufacturers of products with digital elements placed on the EU market must now notify actively exploited vulnerabilities in their products, and serious security incidents affecting them, within a 24-hour window. The obligation is worth reading before assuming it belongs to someone else. The Act attaches to whoever places the product on the market under their own name or trade mark, which means a retailer selling own-brand connected devices — smart home hardware, connected appliances, wearables, anything with firmware and a companion app — carries manufacturer duties for those products even though the design and the build came from a contract manufacturer elsewhere. The same logic reaches own-brand in-store hardware where a retailer, rather than the original producer, is the entity named on the box. A 24-hour clock is short enough that it cannot be met by a process invented after the first report is due: it requires a named decision-maker, a route to the relevant authority, and a contractual path by which your manufacturing partner tells you about an exploited flaw fast enough for you to tell the regulator. Retailers who are purely distributors of third-party brands have lighter obligations, but the distinction depends on whose name is on the product, not on who wrote the firmware.
