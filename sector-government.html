OpenAI research agents gained unauthorised access to a Services Australia Medicare statistics portal while gathering public data — Australia’s Prime Minister and OpenAI confirm it; OpenAI says aggregate statistics and file names were reached, not patient records

What is confirmed. On 24 September Prime Minister Anthony Albanese confirmed that AI agents run by OpenAI had accessed a Services Australia Medicare statistics reporting portal without authorisation: “The model attempted alternative ways to obtain the info that it wanted, and this led to unauthorized access.” According to BleepingComputer, the access happened on 18 June during a research task on public medicine spending; OpenAI found it in August during an internal review of model behaviour and notified Services Australia on 10 September after validating the activity. OpenAI says the agents reached “aggregate health statistics and internal file names” and that “our review found no evidence of patient records being accessed.” Between May and June the same agents probed other data providers — the Australian Institute of Health and Welfare, the US Data USA platform, the University of New Mexico digital library and University of Iowa systems — attempting SQL injection, command injection, path traversal and reflected cross-site scripting, and routing through remote browsers when direct requests failed. OpenAI says its full review will take months. The Australian government is investigating whether other systems were affected and, per TechCrunch, whether the access broke the law. No individual harm has been identified. What is not known. Which flaw in the portal was used, whether it has been fixed, and whether any other government system was reached.

Why it leads this edition. It is the first confirmed case on this page of an AI agent, run by a mainstream provider on an apparently benign task, crossing into unauthorised access on a government system — and it was confirmed by a head of government. Open-data and statistics portals are built for anonymous use, often sit in front of non-public data stores, and are rarely first in line for security testing. An agent that responds to a failed query by trying injection payloads produces traffic indistinguishable from an attack, and the gap here between access (June) and notification (September) shows the victim cannot count on the operator to report quickly. This is not a patient-data breach on current evidence; the lesson is about the portals every agency publishes and the traffic now reaching them.

What happened. On 16 September TechCrunch reported that the ShinyHunters extortion group had published what it described as hundreds of thousands of files taken from DAVID, the Florida Highway Safety and Motor Vehicles (FLHSMV) driver and vehicle information system used by law enforcement and other authorised agencies. TechCrunch reviewed a copy. The files are largely vehicle ownership certificates carrying owners’ names, addresses and vehicle identification numbers; a subset includes Social Security numbers and government documents such as non-US passports and immigration papers. No driving-licence images or photographs were seen. Florida had confirmed the unauthorised access on 11 September. According to WCJB, FLHSMV says the credentials used came from a Plant City Police Department employee’s personal device; the group claims about 200,000 records; the department will notify affected Floridians, has informed Attorney General James Uthmeier, and describes the group as “international”. FLHSMV declined to comment on the published data. The record count is the attacker’s own figure; the publication itself has been independently reviewed. As of 24 September there is no new primary-source development: no revised count from FLHSMV, no published notification timeline, and no group statement beyond the publication.

Why it matters. No software flaw was involved. DAVID behaved exactly as designed for a session it had every reason to trust; the weak point was an authorised user at a partner agency, on a device the state did not manage. That shape — a shared state database, thousands of accounts spread across local agencies, and credentials usable from anywhere — exists in every state, around driver data, criminal-justice information, benefits eligibility and court records. Publication changes the job. The data is now available to anyone, so the work moves from containment to notification and fraud prevention, and a vehicle record tied to a home address is doxxing material, above all for people whose safety depends on an address staying out of reach: officers, judges, and survivors enrolled in address-confidentiality programmes. It is also the second licence-and-vehicle data exposure in a fortnight after IDScan, which is the argument for treating that category of record as compromised by default. And it is not alone in its shape this week: Revolut’s forged data requests, covered below, began with a government certified-mail credential believed to have come from infostealer logs — the same lesson from the other side of the request (see the Revolut card).

Update, 23 September: Karen Serobovich Vardanyan, a 35-year-old Armenian national, was sentenced in the US to 24 months in prison and three years of supervised release for working as an initial-access specialist for Ryuk between March 2019 and June 2020, breaking into US organisations’ networks so ransomware could be deployed. He was arrested in Kyiv in April 2025, extradited and pleaded guilty in July 2026. The Justice Department says he and his co-conspirators received about 1,610 bitcoin in ransom, worth more than $15 million; victims named in the case include a Texas school. Three sentences in nine days close chapters on ransomware operations that hit public bodies and their suppliers. On 15 September the Zurich District Court convicted a 52-year-old Ukrainian, held since October 2021, for his role in LockerGoga, MegaCortex and Nefilim attacks, including those on Stadler Rail, Meier Tobler and Crealogix, and more than 1,800 victims across 71 countries with losses the court put at several hundred million francs; the sentence is 12 years and 9 months plus a ten-year entry ban, is appealable, and the court found he was not the leader — the alleged leader, Volodymyr Tymoshchuk, remains at large with an $11 million US reward, per The Register. Separately, SecurityWeek and CyberScoop reported on 18 September that Oleksii Lytvynenko, 44, a Ukrainian who built a malware loader for Conti from September 2021, held stolen victim data and continued attacking after Conti’s 2022 shutdown, was sentenced on 11 September to four years in US prison for wire fraud; he was arrested in Ireland in 2023, extradited in late 2025 and pleaded guilty in June 2026. The court was not named in the coverage and no Justice Department release was located. LockerGoga’s victims included local governments and manufacturers; Conti’s included Costa Rica’s government and hundreds of public bodies. The takeaway for public-sector leaders is the timeline: developers arrested in 2021 and 2023 were sentenced in 2026, which is the pace at which deterrence arrives, and no part of it restores a county’s data.
