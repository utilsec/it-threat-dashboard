ShinyHunters publishes files stolen from Florida’s DAVID driver and vehicle database after the state did not pay — and the way in was one police employee’s personal device

What happened. On 16 September TechCrunch reported that the ShinyHunters extortion group had published what it described as hundreds of thousands of files taken from DAVID, the Florida Highway Safety and Motor Vehicles (FLHSMV) driver and vehicle information system used by law enforcement and other authorised agencies. TechCrunch reviewed a copy. The files are largely vehicle ownership certificates carrying owners’ names, addresses and vehicle identification numbers; a subset includes Social Security numbers and government documents such as non-US passports and immigration papers. No driving-licence images or photographs were seen. Florida had confirmed the unauthorised access on 11 September. According to WCJB, FLHSMV says the credentials used came from a Plant City Police Department employee’s personal device; the group claims about 200,000 records; the department will notify affected Floridians, has informed Attorney General James Uthmeier, and describes the group as “international”. FLHSMV declined to comment on the published data. The record count is the attacker’s own figure; the publication itself has been independently reviewed.

Why it leads this edition. No software flaw was involved. DAVID behaved exactly as designed for a session it had every reason to trust; the weak point was an authorised user at a partner agency, on a device the state did not manage. That shape — a shared state database, thousands of accounts spread across local agencies, and credentials usable from anywhere — exists in every state, around driver data, criminal-justice information, benefits eligibility and court records. Publication changes the job. The data is now available to anyone, so the work moves from containment to notification and fraud prevention, and a vehicle record tied to a home address is doxxing material, above all for people whose safety depends on an address staying out of reach: officers, judges, and survivors enrolled in address-confidentiality programmes. It is also the second licence-and-vehicle data exposure in a fortnight after IDScan, which is the argument for treating that category of record as compromised by default.

What is confirmed. IDScan.net, an identity-verification vendor whose document-scanning software sits at car-hire counters, hotel desks, retailers, financial institutions and a range of government-adjacent identity checkpoints, issued a breach notice dated 4 September and by 11 September had confirmed unauthorised access to customer data held on its cloud platform. With Florida’s DAVID data now published as well, licence data should be treated as compromised by default.

The ScreenConnect flaw now has a CVE, a patch and a three-day federal deadline — and a worm-like spread pattern that moves between connected machines

ConnectWise has patched CVE-2026-84869 (CVSS 9.9), a missing-authorisation and improper-privilege-management flaw in ScreenConnect that lets an attacker transfer and execute files across an active remote-support session without the host confirming it. CISA added the flaw to the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog on 11 September and, under Binding Operational Directive 26-04, gave federal civilian agencies three days to remediate. Exploitation has been running since around 20 August. The propagation pattern is what earns the attention: attackers abuse legitimate support sessions to move files, then use social engineering to get a victim to run a modified ScreenConnect client that detects active sessions on the machine and pushes VBScript payloads onto the systems connected to them. That is worm-like movement travelling along a trust relationship rather than a network path. For public-sector bodies the exposure usually arrives through a supplier, because ScreenConnect is heavily used by managed service providers and it is small agencies, districts, county offices and municipalities that outsource endpoint support — so the instance able to reach your machines may not be one you can patch, inventory or even see. Fixed in version 26.6.5; the interim mitigation is to disable the TransferFiles permission.

Winona County, Minnesota has confirmed that it paid $128,539 to recover from a ransomware attack in January 2026, and that a second, separate ransomware attack struck the county in April. For small counties and municipalities the transferable lesson is about the recovery period rather than the payment decision: the months after an incident are the months you are most likely to be hit again, and that is when hardening investment has to land.
