A BAE Systems cybersecurity incident halts bankruptcy-notice delivery for US federal courts nationwide — cause and data impact not disclosed

What is confirmed. The Administrative Office of the US Courts said on 15 October that the Bankruptcy Noticing Center (BNC), which BAE Systems runs for the judiciary, has been hit by a cybersecurity incident. Production and delivery of notices for the country’s bankruptcy courts has stopped, and the BNC websites and the National Creditor Registration Service are offline. A court notice dated 13 October says BAE told the Administrative Office late on 9 October. SecurityWeek reports that paper and electronic notices issued through the BNC since then have not been delivered, while courts still accept filings and open cases and electronic filing notifications still reach registered attorneys and trustees. BAE says the incident is “isolated to the BNC system” and is investigating. Not known: whether it was an attack, whether any data was taken, who is responsible and when service returns.

Why it leads this edition. It is a confirmed, nationwide disruption of a federal court function, and it came through a contractor rather than the courts’ own systems: one vendor-run service sits between every bankruptcy court and the creditors, debtors and agencies that rely on its notices for deadlines. Early court notices called it a service disruption; the judiciary’s statement now calls it a cybersecurity incident, and this page does not call it ransomware. The lesson for other agencies is the dependency map — which statutory notices, deadlines or payments rest on a single contractor platform, and what happens when it stops. Editorial assessment: agencies that appear as creditors in bankruptcy cases, such as tax and benefits offices, should assume BNC-mailed notices since 9 October may not have arrived. It replaces Denmark’s CPR register incident as the lead; that card moves to Incidents with new reported detail (covered in Incidents).

High Data Breach

The UK Legal Aid Agency breach is wider than first thought: records back to 1978, probation data and the names of judges and lawyers in Crown Court trials were taken, the Ministry of Justice says

What is confirmed. In a written statement to Parliament, published with a Legal Aid Agency update on Wednesday 14 October and reported on 15 October, Justice Minister Sarah Sackman said forensic work on the April–May 2025 intrusion found more data had been stolen than first believed. Most records relate to applications, claims or payments from January 2007 to 16 May 2025, but about 250,000 records are older, mostly from the 1990s and 2000s, the earliest verified one from 1978. Newly listed data includes probation records (names, dates of birth, nationalities and case references), financial assessments of defendants and their partners, bank and property details, some partially redacted payment-card details, and the names of judges, lawyers and court reporters in Crown Court trials. The ministry says it has no indication the data has been published or shared, and that it has strengthened authentication and access controls, improved monitoring and begun retiring or isolating higher-risk legacy systems, working with the National Crime Agency, the NCSC and the ICO. Claimed: the extortionists say they hold data on more than 2 million people; the government has given no count and has a long-standing policy of not paying.

Why it matters. The scope grew because old data was still there: records nearly five decades old sat on systems reachable in 2025. For justice agencies the exposed names of judges, lawyers and court reporters, and the probation details, are a personal-safety issue as well as a privacy one.

Confirmed — Ministry of Justice written statement (14–15 Oct 2026) widens the scope of the 2025 breach; no indication of publication; the 2M+ figure is the attackers’ claim No CVE — the intrusion method has not been published
No patch applicable — the agency reports stronger access controls and monitoring and is retiring legacy systems Action: Justice, probation and legal-aid bodies: find where case, payment and assessment records older than your retention schedule still live, including legacy and archive systems that stayed online, and delete them or take them offline. Tell judges, court staff and lawyers named in proceedings what may be exposed and how to report approaches that use it, and offer address-protection measures to staff in sensitive roles. Check that legacy case systems have the same MFA and monitoring as current ones.

High Data Breach

Arizona’s courts say hackers copied backup files after an employee clicked a phishing link — fines-and-restitution records on about 1.3 million people, foster-care reports and protective orders among them

What is confirmed. The Arizona Supreme Court announced on 25 September an intrusion that began on 24 September. Its cybersecurity FAQ says criminal hackers accessed and copied backup court files after an employee clicked a link in a phishing email, and that court IT shut the intrusion down within about two hours. The Record reported on 7 October that the copied data includes Fines/Fees and Restitution Enforcement (FARE) records on about 1.3 million people going back roughly 30 years, with names, Social Security numbers and case numbers; more than 150,000 Foster Care Review Board reports dating to 2010, covering about 8,000 children now in foster care among others; and active and inactive protective-order records. A court spokesperson said it was not ransomware and no ransom had been demanded, and no group had claimed it. The chief justice has spoken with the FBI, and FARE victims are being contacted by text and urged to freeze their credit.

Why it matters. Backups are often treated as an availability control and protected less carefully than live systems, yet here they were what the attackers took. The set mixes debt and identity records with child-welfare and protective-order files — the categories where exposure can put people at physical risk.

Confirmed — Arizona Supreme Court / AOC: backup files accessed and copied (intrusion 24 Sep 2026, announced 25 Sep); scope reported 7 Oct 2026; not ransomware, no ransom demand, no claim No CVE — an employee clicked a phishing link

Courts and child-welfare agencies: identify protective-order parties, foster families and children in any exposed set so they can be warned first, and agree with partner agencies who sends which notice. Text messages asking victims to act are themselves a phishing opportunity, so publish what a genuine notice will and will not ask for.

Denmark’s CPR register incident: reporting names the Funen IT firm Pays ApS as the company whose lookup access was abused, and a claimant says a “123456” password let them in — about 8.8 million people’s names, addresses and CPR numbers were taken, per the ministry

Update, 9–13 October: The Register, citing Politiken and TV 2, reports that the company whose access was misused is Pays ApS, an IT firm on Funen, whose managing director confirmed on 9 October that its access had been exploited; the government has still not named it. A person claiming responsibility told Politiken they got in through an account protected by the password “123456”, which they say at least three accounts, including an administrator account, shared — that is the claimant’s account, not an official finding. According to the same reporting, about 14 million automated searches ran between 10 and 20 September, the activity was spotted on 2 October after a ministry employee noticed an invoice showing unusually high use, and Datatilsynet was notified on 4 October.

What the ministry confirmed (5 October). Unauthorised parties misused a private company’s legitimate lookup access to the Central Person Register during September and extracted names, addresses and CPR numbers for about 8.8 million registered people — living, emigrated and deceased; people with name and address protection were not included. The company’s access was cut off, the police are investigating, a citizen hotline was opened and a wider review of CPR security began. No misuse of the data has been reported and no group has claimed it.

Why it matters. If the reported details hold, a national register was drained through one client’s weak shared password, and the first warning came from a bill rather than from monitoring. Any government that grants bulk lookup access to its base registers should ask how each client authenticates, whether anyone watches query volume per client, and how fast a single client can be cut off. It led the previous edition and moves here behind the US court-noticing incident.
