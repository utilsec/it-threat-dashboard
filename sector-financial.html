Top Story

Revolut’s forged government requests came from a hijacked Italian Interior Ministry certified-mail account — researchers put the exposure at about 680 high-value customers over five to six months, and a $3M ransom demand has been posted that Revolut says it never received

Revolut has confirmed that fraudsters sent it fake government data requests, that its compliance process accepted them as genuine, and that customer data was released. The disclosure was first reported on 12 September 2026 and widely covered on 14 September. Revolut’s own description is that “an unauthorized third party utilized a legitimate government agency domain email to submit fraudulent requests for information”. Updated 17–18 September: SecurityWeek, citing Hudson Rock and other investigators, reports that the domain was genuine because the account was: infostealer malware had captured the login of an employee of Italy’s Ministry of the Interior on its certified-email (PEC) system, and Hudson Rock found around 300 compromised logins for that domain in infostealer logs. The forged requests were sent to Revolut Bank UAB in Lithuania and, per the same reporting, ran for five to six months. American Banker reports the Lithuanian entity appears to have been chosen because EU law obliges it to answer European Investigation Orders, and that about 680 customers were affected — described as high-net-worth and crypto “whales” — none of them in the US. Revolut itself still says only “a limited number of customers” and has not named the agency. An actor calling itself IAmNotAVillain posted a $3M ransom demand on 16 September; Revolut says it “has not received any direct contact or demand”. Earlier reporting had cited a 10,000 BTC demand. The same actor claims 147 GB taken from an Italian law-enforcement agency, which Italian police are investigating. The 680-customer, five-month and $3M figures come from researchers and the actor, not from Revolut. What was handed over is unusually complete: names, dates of birth, occupations, addresses, email addresses and phone numbers, passport and driver’s licence copies, identity-verification selfies, statements with IBANs, and transaction and crypto history. Revolut says its systems and customer funds were not affected and that it has notified law enforcement, data-protection authorities and financial regulators. The new detail removes the comfortable reading of this incident. The request did not come from a look-alike domain that a careful analyst could have spotted; it came from a real government mailbox whose credentials were sitting in infostealer logs, so every check that validates the sender’s domain, headers or reference format would have passed. Law-enforcement request desks, subpoena and court-order intake, emergency disclosure requests and regulator information demands all end at a small team authorised to release exactly this dossier, and most verify the request rather than the requester. The control that works is an out-of-band callback to a number the institution already holds for that authority, dual authorisation before any bulk identity or transaction export, and volume alerting on the request desk itself — a single requester drawing hundreds of high-value customer files over months is a pattern somebody should have seen. It is also a reporting event: under DORA the initial notification clock for a major ICT-related incident runs in hours, and US banking institutions sit under a 36-hour supervisory notification rule, both starting from classification.

Medium Resilience

AWS says data held only in its Bahrain region cannot be recovered after drone strikes — the first permanent regional loss at a hyperscaler is a resilience test financial firms can no longer call hypothetical

Amazon Web Services has told customers that resources and data hosted exclusively in its Bahrain region, me-south-1, cannot be restored, and that one Availability Zone in its UAE region, me-central-1, is a total loss, Help Net Security reported on 17 September 2026 from AWS’s 15 September updates. The cause is physical rather than cyber: Iranian drone strikes that began in early March. AWS says two of its UAE facilities were directly struck; in Bahrain a nearby strike caused structural and power damage and fire suppression caused water damage, which AWS says exceeded what its redundancy design could withstand. A second Bahrain Availability Zone was disrupted in April, taking the region fully offline. AWS says most customers had already relocated using backups; restoration of the remaining UAE zones continues and it expects further Bahrain updates in early 2027. No specific financial institution has been reported as losing data. The lesson for regulated firms is nonetheless direct. Operational-resilience regimes such as DORA and the UK impact-tolerance rules assume a cloud region can fail; here one did, permanently, and the customers who kept their data were the ones holding copies outside it. A multi-zone design protected against a single facility failure, not against a regional event that took out more than one zone.

IDScan.net confirms the breach behind 153 million driver-licence scans — the identity-verification layer under remote account opening is the thing that was taken

IDScan.net, the Louisiana identity-verification vendor whose technology is used by banks, car-rental firms, retailers and hospitality businesses to check government IDs, has confirmed unauthorised access to its cloud platform. The intrusion was discovered around 1 September 2026 and disclosed on 4 September; BleepingComputer reported the confirmation on 10 September. Exposed material covers more than 153 million driver-licence scans, about 10 million identification cards, roughly 3 million travel documents and some 579,000 medical cards. Brian Krebs first surfaced the data on 1 September after it was advertised on a dark-web marketplace calling itself Nexus, and verified the listing by searching the database. The FBI is investigating. This is a state change from the position this page carried on 10 September, when the vendor had published nothing and the breach was unconfirmed: the occurrence is now acknowledged, though the number of individuals affected has not been stated. For banks, insurers and fintechs the exposure sits directly under the onboarding stack. Remote account opening, step-up verification and contact-centre re-identification all lean on a photographed government ID, and reporting describes multi-image captures — the output of an automated document scanner rather than a consumer phone upload. A corpus of authentic scans lets an attacker satisfy a document check without ever holding the document, which is exactly the assumption most know-your-customer flows are built on. Class actions were filed in Louisiana in early September and the Nexus site is offline, but BleepingComputer reports criminals retain access to the underlying database.

High Breach

Trezor says a former fulfilment vendor still held customer records it had certified as deleted — roughly 80,689 US customers now exposed

Hardware-wallet maker Trezor disclosed on 5 September 2026 that a breach at its former fulfilment provider ShipMonk exposed a further 67,000 US customers, taking the reported total to about 80,689 once an earlier tranche of 13,689 is included. The records cover orders placed between November 2019 and August 2021 and contain names, email addresses, phone numbers, shipping addresses and order numbers. Trezor stated plainly that throughout the relationship it had repeatedly requested and received written assurance that the data had been deleted in line with its contract and data policy — and that the data was nonetheless still present in ShipMonk’s systems. The underlying compromise is attributed by blockchain security firm Holborn to the ShinyHunters extortion group, which reportedly reached ShipMonk through CVE-2026-72898, a critical SQL injection flaw in Metabase carrying a CVSS score of 10.0. ShipMonk has not publicly acknowledged the incident. The predictable consequence arrived on 11 September, when the exposed mailing list was used to phish 347,000 Trezor customers; the company reports that roughly 2,500 recipients clicked the malicious links, with no indication so far that recovery phrases or funds were taken as a result. That is the shape of a list breach: the loss event is not the exfiltration, it is the campaign that runs against the list weeks later, against people who have every reason to believe the sender knows them. Trezor is clear that its wallets themselves are unaffected; the exposure is that a list of confirmed crypto-hardware owners with home addresses and phone numbers is an unusually well-qualified target set for phishing, vishing, impersonation and, given the addresses, physical coercion. For banks, insurers, brokers and payment firms the transferable lesson has nothing to do with crypto. This is a fourth-party retention failure: a vendor kept data past a contractual deletion date, attested in writing that it had not, and the attesting firm carried the notification duty when the attestation proved wrong. Written deletion assurances are not evidence of deletion, and DORA, the FCA regime and most US supervisory expectations put the burden on the regulated firm regardless of who actually held the file.

Seven agencies in four countries say North Korea’s fake-job-interview operation infected 30,000 devices and emptied 7,000 crypto wallets — and the developers it targets are the ones holding the keys

A joint alert published on 18 September 2026 by Japan’s National Police Agency and National Cybersecurity Office, the FBI, the US Defense Department’s Cyber Crime Center, Australia’s ACSC and Germany’s BND and BfV puts numbers on the North Korean operation tracked as WaterPlum, also known as Contagious Interview. Between December 2025 and July 2026 it infected at least 30,000 devices in more than 100 countries and drained over 7,000 cryptocurrency wallets, taking about ¥1.7 billion — roughly $10.71 million by the advisory’s own conversion. The lure is a recruitment process: operators pose as AI, crypto and NFT companies or work through recruiting and freelance platforms, and during the interview the applicant is told to download and run a project. The NPA and FBI place WaterPlum and some North Korean IT-worker operations under the 313 General Bureau of the Munitions Industry Department. For exchanges, custodians, fintechs and bank digital-asset teams this lands on two populations at once: the developers and engineers who are the audience for the fake interviews, whose machines hold signing keys, cloud credentials and wallet extensions; and the hiring pipeline itself, since the same alert addresses North Korean IT workers seeking real jobs. A developer who runs a take-home test on a work laptop has handed over whatever that laptop can reach.

Both depend on an authority claim that nobody verifies through a channel the fraudster does not control. The FBI’s categories also give fraud teams a ready typology list for the richer suspicious-activity reporting Treasury asked for earlier this month.

CrowdStrike profiles Slim Spider — a financially motivated actor that has learned how Brazilian instant payments and bank cloud actually work

CrowdStrike published research on 8 September 2026 on Slim Spider, a financially motivated adversary it has tracked against Brazilian financial institutions since at least March 2026. What distinguishes the group is domain knowledge rather than novel malware: it understands Pix instant payments, digital-asset platforms and the cloud services financial firms run underneath them, and it aims at the money rather than at resale data. In a late-March intrusion at a Brazilian bank, CrowdStrike observed custom Bash scripts querying cloud instance metadata to lift temporary cloud credentials, moving them out over socket connections, and then pursuing crypto assets and instant-payment accounts. The tradecraft is worth reading outside Brazil. Instance-metadata abuse is a generic cloud weakness — a workload that can reach the metadata endpoint can often mint short-lived credentials for whatever role it runs as, and those credentials rarely trigger the alerting that a stolen human password would. Pair that with an actor who already knows your payment initiation flow and your fraud thresholds, and the result is theft that looks like ordinary automated activity. UK, EU and US institutions running instant-payment schemes on cloud infrastructure face the same structural exposure even though this actor’s current targeting is regional.

High Threat Actor

Microsoft names two access brokers running helpdesk vishing into personal devices, then pulling SharePoint and Exchange out through the Graph API

Microsoft researchers, in reporting published by Dark Reading on 10 September 2026, detailed at least two initial-access-broker clusters — tracked as Storm-3032 and Storm-3121 — running voice-phishing campaigns since May 2026. The pattern is consistent: the caller impersonates the IT helpdesk, steers the target onto their own personal device rather than a managed one, and sends them to a lookalike Microsoft sign-in page on a plausible domain such as “company.add-passkey.com”. Credentials and session tokens are captured there. The financial-services relevance is the helpdesk itself. Bank, insurer and broker service desks handle high volumes of urgent reset requests across branches, advisers and contractors, and the routing onto a personal device is deliberate — it sidesteps device compliance, conditional access and every endpoint control the firm actually owns. Because the exfiltration runs through Graph with a legitimate token, it will not look like malware on any endpoint; it looks like a user reading their own mail.

CISA and NIST published NIST IR 8587 on 15 September 2026, guidance on protecting the identity tokens and signing keys that cloud services use to decide who a caller is. This page carries WSO2 API Manager CVE-2026-5430, where a forged token is the whole attack, and the helpdesk-vishing and passkey campaigns, where a stolen session or rogue credential outlasts a password reset. Token lifetime, audience restriction and key custody are the settings that decide how far one forged or stolen token travels.
