For operators, backup agents run with high privilege on the servers that matter most, so a vendor’s update channel is part of the attack surface (editorial assessment).

The FBI and Secret Service say the FortiBleed credential campaign against FortiGate firewalls is still active — no CVE, and targets span every US critical-infrastructure sector

In a joint advisory dated 6 October 2026 (JCSA-20261006-01), the FBI and US Secret Service warned that the FortiBleed campaign against internet-facing Fortinet FortiGate firewalls and SSL VPN gateways remains an active threat. There is no CVE: the agencies describe abuse of reused or leaked credentials and of legacy SHA-256 password storage, and researchers describe credential stuffing and spraying from leak dumps, offline cracking of extracted hashes and a Go-based sniffer that passively captures authentication traffic. The advisory cites SOCRadar’s count of more than 86,644 compromised devices in 194 countries — a figure from 19 June, not a new count — and the agencies say targets span all 16 US critical-infrastructure sectors, communications among them. Access is resold, and authorities say compromised FortiGates have served as ransomware entry points; SOCRadar says it has confirmed at least 12 ransomware deployments. Researchers describe the operator as a Russian-speaking access broker; that is their assessment, not the agencies’. For carriers and managed-security providers that run FortiGate fleets for customers, the same credential questions apply to every tenant.

High Threat Intel

Rapid7 finds BPFDoor and a new SMTP-mimicking implant, AVERAT, built to blend into telecom environments in South Korea and Taiwan — relayed through devices on a carrier’s network, not through the carrier

Rapid7 published research on 2 October 2026 on Linux implants that, in its description, blend into the software and device conventions of the telecom environments they target. In South Korea it found a new BPFDoor variant that can tunnel over HTTPS POST and a BPF-based Rekoobe variant that filters on port 25; mail-security appliances such as SpamSniper are among the device types it describes as targeted. In Taiwan it found six builds of a new modular implant it calls AVERAT, delivered by an AES-encrypted dropper. AVERAT connects out on TCP 25 and behaves like a mail client — EHLO, then STARTTLS, then its own encryption — and checks in every 600 to 699 seconds, so on the wire it looks like an appliance sending mail. The relay infrastructure is the part to read carefully. Rapid7 says the command-and-control relays were compromised customer equipment belonging to third-party victims — NAS boxes, ADSL and FTTH customer premises equipment and CCTV recorders — sitting in Chunghwa Telecom’s HiNet address space in three Taiwanese cities. That is end-user equipment on a carrier’s network repurposed as relays; Rapid7 does not report a compromise of the carrier itself. It is equally careful on attribution: it places the activity in the pattern of China-nexus covert relay (ORB) networks but found no infrastructure or indicator overlap with any named ORB network, and names no actor. Rapid7 publishes hashes for the dropper, the AVERAT builds and the Korean cluster, plus domains and IP addresses. Its detection cues travel well into any operator estate: processes whose executable has been deleted from disk, unexpected state files and scripts on appliances, and outbound SMTP from an appliance to mail-role hostnames that resolve to consumer or embedded devices.

For ISPs this is a customer-premises story twice over: the recruits are the kind of home and small-office gateways that stay in service for years, and STUN is ordinary enough on consumer lines that it rarely gets a second look at the edge.

Fortinet’s PSIRT advisory FG-IR-26-175, published 1 October 2026, covers CVE-2026-104286, a path-traversal flaw combined with improper NULL-byte handling in FortiMail that lets an unauthenticated attacker write arbitrary files to the underlying system with crafted HTTP or HTTPS requests. A third MikroTik RouterOS flaw is in KEV — CVE-2026-67279 is the other half of the exploited “MikroTrick” SSH chain, and its 28 September federal deadline has passed

CISA added CVE-2026-67279 to the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog on 25 September 2026 with a federal deadline of 28 September. It is an SSH workflow flaw in MikroTik RouterOS that, in CISA’s words, “could allow an unauthenticated client to open a session channel and send an exec request” and “can be chained to achieve unauthenticated exploitation of CVE-2026-86060”; CERT Polska describes it as the SSH connection protocol entering an authenticated state before user authentication has completed. The Hacker News gives it CVSS 6.9 without stating the CVSS version — a modest score for one half of a chain that ends in full administrative control. It joins CVE-2026-86060, an argument-injection flaw in RouterOS SSH login handling that escalates privileges when a username begins with characters the platform should reject, and CVE-2026-67277, an unauthenticated bandwidth-test (btest) connection flaw that can restart the kernel, both added to KEV on 10 September with a 13 September deadline, now passed. CERT Polska reported on 5 September that a two-flaw SSH chain it calls “MikroTrick” was being exploited in the wild, with activity from about 2 September. Its 22 September technical analysis names the chain: CVE-2026-67279 combined with CVE-2026-86060, which gives an unauthenticated attacker administrative control of an exposed router. Earlier editions of this card named CVE-2026-67276, an SSH RSA key-verification flaw, as part of the chain; no source supports that, and CVE-2026-67276 is not in KEV. The same CERT Polska advisory covers CVE-2026-67278 (malformed RSA signatures accepted, 7.x only) and CVE-2026-67281 (unauthenticated file reads through the WebFig /jsproxy path). Fixed builds are RouterOS 6.49.21, 7.23.4 and 7.24.2; CVE-2026-67278 needs 7.23.6 or 7.24.3 because the earlier fix was incomplete. Shadowserver counted more than 122,500 MikroTik devices with SSH reachable from the internet in early September — an exposure figure, not a count of unpatched or compromised routers.

The exposure is not evenly spread. RouterOS is the low-cost routing, PPPoE-termination and wireless-backhaul platform that a large share of ISPs, WISPs and small carriers build access networks on, and those boxes routinely sit at the customer edge with management reachable because that is how they are provisioned — so administrative control means routing changes, tunnel creation, DNS redirection and traffic interception, and historically compromised MikroTik fleets are monetised as proxy and botnet infrastructure rather than quietly cleaned up. Two qualifications belong with the confirmation: CERT Polska notes that devices left on RouterOS default firewall rules are largely protected, and no count of compromised devices, victim or actor has been published, so this is established as active, not as widespread. The useful part is the indicators CERT Polska published: log lines reading login failure for user -2 from <ip> via ssh, entries of the form user <name> added by ssh:-2@<ip>, and the presence of a privileged account named ops. Any of the three is a compromise, not a curiosity — and patching a router that already carries an attacker-created admin account does not remove the attacker. A separate pre-authentication flaw in RouterOS web management, CVE-2026-84411, is on the previous card; it is not exploited or KEV-listed, but on CISA’s version range the 6.49.21 and 7.23.4 builds that fix this chain are still exposed to it.

Connecticut settles with Frontier for $625,000 over its 2024 breach and orders a security programme with MFA, segmentation and a third-party assessment

Connecticut Attorney General William Tong announced a $625,000 settlement with Frontier Communications on 20 October 2026 over the April 2024 breach in which attackers used compromised credentials of Frontier employees and vendors to reach internal IT systems and took unencrypted personal data, including Social Security numbers, that was later offered for sale. The breach affected 58,841 Connecticut residents. The AG’s office cited weaknesses in identity and access management, network segmentation, logging, encryption, incident response, training and data-loss prevention. Beyond the payment, Frontier must run a comprehensive security programme reporting to its CEO that includes multi-factor authentication, encryption, logging, segmentation, penetration testing, endpoint detection and response and incident response, and must commission a third-party assessment within a year and share it with the AG. A separate $5.64 million federal class settlement in Texas covers about 750,000 people nationwide. Verizon acquired Frontier in January 2026, and both companies cooperated. DataBreaches.net attributes the 2024 attack to RansomHub. For carriers, the order reads as a list of what a state regulator now expects after a credential-led breach.

UK ICO reprimands altnet Ogi and Welsh public body Data Cymru after ransomware got in through a 12-year-old “Test 2” account with the password “Password1”

The UK Information Commissioner’s Office published reprimands on 20 October 2026 for the Local Government Data Unit – Wales (Data Cymru) and its IT provider Ogi Networks, over a ransomware attack on 1 November 2024. The attackers entered Data Cymru’s network through a “Test 2” account that Ogi had set up more than 12 years earlier and protected only with the password “Password1”. They took 42 GB from a domain controller, a file server and an SQL server, then encrypted 182.7 GB. Personal data of about 3,300 people was affected, most of them children, from two projects supporting children and families in Wales. The ICO found that Ogi did not enforce password strength, audit the accounts it created or review local administrator rights, and that Data Cymru had no compliant sub-processor contract and no routine account reviews. No fine was issued, and the ransomware group is not named. The Register describes Ogi as an alternative broadband network operator that also supplied Data Cymru’s servers, connectivity and support. That is why it belongs here: a network operator that doubles as a managed IT provider answers for the accounts it leaves behind in customers’ systems.

A bipartisan Senate bill would have NTIA develop voluntary telecom cybersecurity practices in response to Salt Typhoon — introduced, not law

Senators Mark Warner (D-Virginia) and Ted Cruz (R-Texas) introduced the Telecommunications Cybersecurity and Resilience Act on 24 September 2026, presented as a response to the Salt Typhoon intrusions into US telecommunications networks. As described by CyberScoop and The Record, the bill would create a telecom cybersecurity working group within the National Telecommunications and Information Administration to develop voluntary, industry-wide best practices for identifying, responding to and mitigating cybersecurity incidents within 18 months of passage. According to Senator Warner’s office and Industrial Cyber, the FCC, CISA, NIST, NSA and the Office of the National Cyber Director would sit on the group, with threat information from ODNI, its practices would align with the NIST Cybersecurity Framework and Risk Management Framework, and the bill would not give the FCC new authority to mandate them. It would also set up an optional third-party certification process for companies that adopt them, and the practices would be reviewed every two years or after a major incident. The sponsors frame it as cooperation rather than regulation: Warner said that if telecommunications companies adopt cybersecurity best practices “our networks can be more resilient”, and Cruz described the approach as developing voluntary practices “rather than adopting rigid federal mandates”. The bill has only been introduced; it changes no operator’s obligations today, and its path through Congress is unknown. For operators the practical interest is in what the working group would be asked to produce, since voluntary sector practices tend to become the reference point that customers, insurers and auditors ask about, whatever their legal status.

India orders operators to show callers’ registered names on 4G and newer networks from 1 November — and not to keep the name data after the call

India’s Department of Telecommunications issued a circular dated 13 October 2026 requiring telecom service providers to display a caller’s registered name with the number on incoming calls from 1 November 2026, The Hacker News reported on 15 October. The rule, Calling Name Presentation (CNAP), covers mainly wireless networks on 4G and newer technologies; 2G, 3G and fixed-line rollout will be examined separately. A name saved in the recipient’s contacts takes precedence; otherwise the originating operator’s subscriber database supplies the name for domestic calls, and calls from abroad are labelled as international. Originating operators must maintain the name-to-number database that receiving operators query, must offer calling line identification restriction so users can withhold their identity, and must not retain the calling name after the call ends. Business and bulk connections may present a preferred name only if it is a registered trademark or GST-registered trade name. The stated aim is to help people identify unknown callers and to curb spam and fraud. For operators the security work is in the new inter-operator lookup: a database every network queries on every call is both a privacy asset and an availability dependency, and the circular asks for a secure-by-design approach.

Four more US states sue TP-Link over its router-security claims, citing end-of-life models and flaws in ISP-supplied devices — TP-Link says the suits rest on false premises

Florida, Iowa, Montana and Nebraska filed consumer-protection lawsuits against TP-Link Systems on 6 October 2026, The Hacker News reported on 9 October. The complaints allege that the company overstated the security of its routers and its separation from China. They cite end-of-life models such as the Archer AX21 and flaws CVE-2025-30237 through CVE-2025-30241 in Aginet devices supplied through internet service providers, which have no known exploitation and were not in CISA’s KEV catalog as of 8 October. The complaints do not allege a backdoor or Chinese access to customer data. TP-Link denies the claims and says the suits are built on false premises. These are allegations in litigation, not findings. Their relevance to operators is the device fleet rather than the outcome of the cases: ISPs that ship or manage TP-Link or Aginet CPE should know which models and firmware they have in the field and which have reached end of life, because that is what customers will ask about.

CISA’s final CIRCIA incident-reporting rule is at White House review — 72-hour incident and 24-hour ransom-payment reporting, but no start date yet

The White House Office of Information and Regulatory Affairs logged receipt of CISA’s final rule under the Cyber Incident Reporting for Critical Infrastructure Act on 1 October 2026, according to its regulatory-review record. The rule text is not public; the statutory deadline of October 2025 and CISA’s later targets of May and September 2026 have passed, and HIPAA Journal reports that publication is expected before the end of the year. The statute sets 72-hour reporting for covered cyber incidents and 24 hours for ransom payments, but obligations start only on dates the final rule sets, and the rule will also define which entities are covered. Review can still change the text. In a GAO roundtable report released on 28 September, industry participants singled out the forthcoming rule as overlapping with other federal reporting regimes. The act spans all 16 critical-infrastructure sectors, communications among them; which telecom entities are covered will depend on the final rule’s definitions, and until it is published nothing is required.
