A cybersecurity incident at contractor BAE Systems has halted notices from every US bankruptcy court since 9 October

The Administrative Office of the US Courts said on 15 October that a cybersecurity incident at BAE Systems, which runs the Bankruptcy Noticing Center for all federal bankruptcy courts, has stopped the production and delivery of bankruptcy notices and taken the BNC’s websites offline, including the National Creditor Registration Service. BAE first told the judiciary late on 9 October and says the incident is isolated to the BNC system. Courts warn that paper and electronic notices issued through the BNC since 9 October have not been delivered; filings are still accepted and electronic filing notices still reach registered attorneys and trustees. Neither BAE nor the judiciary has said what kind of incident it was, whether data was taken or when service will return. For bankruptcy and creditor practices, assume BNC-served notices since 9 October did not arrive and follow each court’s posted guidance (editorial assessment).

Arizona’s courts confirm hackers copied backup files after one phishing click — fines and restitution records on about 1.3 million people, foster-care reports and protective orders

Arizona’s court system announced a cyberattack on 25 September, and its updated cybersecurity FAQ now says federal and state investigators have confirmed that the intruders accessed and copied backup court files. The attack began on 24 September after a court employee clicked a link in a phishing email, and ran until court IT shut the affected system down. The copied files include records from the statewide Fines/Fees and Restitution Enforcement (FARE) programme on roughly 1.3 million people going back about 30 years — names, Social Security numbers and case numbers — more than 150,000 Foster Care Review Board reports dating to 2010, and active and inactive protective-order records. The court says the file format may make the data hard to read, that the incident was not ransomware and that no ransom had been demanded as of early October; no group has claimed it. The FBI is involved. FARE victims are being told by text message to put holds on their credit, and notices have gone to the Department of Child Safety, judges, Foster Care Review Board members and the attorneys who represent parents. For practitioners the exposure sits at client level: criminal-defence, family and dependency lawyers have clients and opposing parties in these records, and protective-order data is the kind that can put a person at physical risk if it surfaces (editorial assessment). It is also the plainest version of the most common entry route — one click, by one employee, on a system that held decades of backups.

The UK Legal Aid Agency’s breach is bigger than first reported — records back to 1978, plus the names of judges and of defence and prosecution lawyers in Crown Court trials

The Legal Aid Agency, part of the Ministry of Justice, published an updated assessment on 14 October of the cyberattack it disclosed in May 2025, and Justice Minister Sarah Sackman told Parliament in a written statement that forensic work had found more data was taken than first believed. Most of the stolen records relate to legal aid applications, claims or payments made between January 2007 and 16 May 2025, or to cases live in that period, but about 250,000 records are older, the earliest verified one from 1978. Newly identified categories include probation records (names, dates of birth, nationality and case references), financial assessments of defendants and their partners, bank account and property details, some partially redacted card details, details of opposing parties in some civil cases, and the names of judges, defence and prosecution lawyers and court reporters in Crown Court trials. The minister says there is no indication the data has been published or shared. The attackers claimed to hold data on more than two million people and demanded payment; that figure is theirs, not the ministry’s, and the government’s policy is not to pay. The ministry says it has tightened authentication and access controls, improved monitoring and begun retiring or isolating higher-risk legacy systems, working with the National Crime Agency, the NCSC and the ICO. For legal aid practices the update changes two things: their own lawyers’ names are now in the confirmed data, and the affected client population reaches back to files closed decades ago.

Greenberg Traurig’s breach is now in federal court — two proposed class actions in Manhattan, one reported to be on behalf of clients, after Silent Ransom Group leaked the firm’s files

Court dockets show two proposed class actions against Greenberg Traurig in the US District Court for the Southern District of New York: Addison v. Greenberg Traurig, filed on 9 September, and Hancock v. Greenberg Traurig, filed on 20 September. MLex reported on 21 September that a proposed class of the firm’s clients had sued over that weekend. According to MLex, the clients allege the firm failed to protect their data before a preventable breach and waited about two weeks to tell them, and they seek injunctive relief. These are allegations in complaints this page has not reviewed, and the firm has not answered them in court. The breach itself is confirmed. The firm filed a draft notice with the California Attorney General, dated 9 September, confirming that Social Security numbers and other personal information were involved, and told Vermont’s Attorney General that 10 residents there are affected. It says its systems were not compromised and that an unauthorised actor reached a limited number of documents. According to DataBreaches.net, Silent Ransom Group got in through a corporate iPad from 13 August, the firm detected the intrusion on 26 August, and the group published the data on 2 September after the firm let negotiation deadlines pass. DataBreaches.net counts roughly 126,000 people with Social Security numbers in the leaked files, mostly from spreadsheets of class-action member data, and later reported that Oregon’s breach list shows 150,000 affected; neither figure is official. The exposed data includes names, Social Security numbers, dates of birth and contact details, and some files hold medical records, tax documents and passport copies. The group, also tracked as Luna Moth, Chatty Spider and UNC3753, has targeted law firms since 2023. FBI (May 2026) and Mandiant (June 2026) reporting describes callers posing as IT support who talk staff into installing remote-management tools, data taken out with WinSCP or Rclone, and operators sometimes turning up in person claiming they need to image a device; often there is no encryption stage, because the leverage is the client material. No newer law-firm breach was verified for 6 to 16 October. The litigation is the new lesson. A client class action turns a firm’s own incident into a claim by the people it acts for, and the alleged delay in telling clients is part of the claim, so the notification timeline a firm keeps during an incident can become evidence.

The Ministry of Justice apologises after court and probation staff looked at Southport victims’ files without authorisation — an insider-access breach inside the courts, now with the ICO

The UK Ministry of Justice said on 16 September that staff in HM Courts and Tribunals Service and HM Prison and Probation Service had accessed records relating to victims and survivors of the July 2024 Southport attack without authorisation. For a limited number of individuals the material included sensitive personal data the department assessed as “likely to pose a high risk to their rights and freedoms”; the MoJ says it has found no evidence the information was shared outside the organisations. The Information Commissioner’s Office has been notified, both agencies are investigating, and the Prime Minister has asked the Lord Chancellor to oversee the investigation. The department has not said how many staff were involved. In its own words: “We are appalled that this happened and recognise the distress it will have caused victims, survivors, and their families.” It follows earlier reports of similar unauthorised access to the same victims’ records at NHS and emergency-service bodies. This is not an attack: people with legitimate accounts opened files they had no business reason to open, on a case with intense public interest, and every access looked like a permitted user doing their job. For firms the read-across is direct. Matter files for a high-profile client or a case in the news attract curious staff, a document management system that gives every fee-earner read access to every matter cannot tell curiosity from need, and ethical screens are built for conflicts, not curiosity.

High Breach

Forged legal requests sent from a genuine ministry mailbox pried passport and financial data out of Revolut for months — the sender check passed because the account was real

Revolut disclosed on 14 September that “an unauthorized third party utilized a legitimate government agency domain email to submit fraudulent requests for information”. Reporting on 17–18 September filled in how. According to SecurityWeek, citing infostealer researchers Hudson Rock, an infostealer compromised the email account of an employee of Italy’s Ministry of the Interior on its certified-mail (PEC) domain; Hudson Rock found around 300 compromised logins for that domain in infostealer logs. The account was used to send fraudulent government data requests to Revolut Bank UAB in Lithuania — which American Banker says was apparently chosen because EU law obliges it to answer European Investigation Orders — over roughly five to six months. About 680 high-net-worth customers are reported affected, none of them in the US, and the data included passport details, contact details and financial information. Those figures come from researchers and reporting, not from Revolut, which still says only “a limited number of customers” and has notified regulators and law enforcement. An actor calling itself “IAmNotAVillain” posted a $3 million ransom demand on 16 September; Revolut says it has received no direct contact or demand. The actor also claims 147 GB from an Italian law-enforcement agency, which Italian police are investigating. Update 20 September: American Banker’s updated report carries no new statement from Revolut or any regulator; it adds analysis that US banks lack standardised controls for verifying legal data requests and that the compliance teams answering them often sit outside security. Update 21 September: Infosecurity, citing Malwarebytes, reports a wave of SMS phishing against Revolut customers that began around 14 September, within days of the bank’s first acknowledgement. The pages ask for camera access and mimic Revolut’s video identity check before requesting the password; several hundred accounts are believed affected, with high-net-worth cryptocurrency holders singled out after blockchain analysis. Malwarebytes links the targeting to data from the same breach — that is the researcher’s assessment, not a Revolut statement. This belongs on a legal page because production orders, subpoenas and law-enforcement data requests pass through firms constantly — received for clients, advised on and answered. The lesson is that sender authentication proves only which mailbox sent a message, not that the person behind it had authority, and the pressure to comply quickly is exactly what the attacker relies on.

A hijacked Brevo CDN key turned embedded sign-up forms and chat widgets into a malware channel for more than four hours — check the firm website if it uses them

Email-marketing platform Brevo (formerly Sendinblue) has confirmed a two-stage compromise first reported by e-commerce security firm Sansec on 16 September. Attackers obtained a long-lived Cloudflare API key with full account permissions that had been hardcoded in Brevo’s application source code, and used it to deploy a Cloudflare Worker that rewrote content at the CDN edge on 14 September, from about 16:07 to 20:30 UTC. Affected assets included brevo.com, sendinblue.com, the sibforms.com form host, the login and account portals, and the forms, Conversations chat widget and SDK loader scripts that customers embed on their own websites. The Worker also stripped Content-Security-Policy headers. Brevo says that because it acted at the edge, origin servers and files were unmodified and standard integrity checks did not detect the change. Ordinary visitors to affected sites were shown a fake “Cloudflare, verify you are human” page telling them to paste and run a command — the ClickFix technique. Visitors with a live WordPress administrator session had the script try to install a malicious plugin called “Web Media Optimizer” as a persistent backdoor. Sansec estimates more than 100,000 sites were exposed. Earlier, on 10 September, attackers had abused an improperly scoped SAML single sign-on path to reach 138 Brevo customer accounts, exporting contacts from 43 and sending phishing from six. No law firm has been named. The exposure for a firm is its own website: if a marketing page, newsletter or client-alert sign-up embeds Brevo forms, chat or tracking scripts, it served this code to visitors in the window, and anyone logged in to its WordPress admin at the time may have left behind a backdoored site. Scripts that marketing added are easy to miss on an IT asset register.

Canva confirms enterprise customer data was reached through a small feedback vendor’s connection to its Salesforce — the SaaS-to-SaaS link most firms have never inventoried

Canva has confirmed that “the unauthorised access to Canny allowed access to limited enterprise customer information through its connection to our Salesforce account, including business contact details and contract information.” Canny is a product-feedback tool. DataBreaches.net reports that data was exported from 26 to 28 August, that the attack took place on 28 August, and that Canny notified Canva on 29 August. The story became public on 23 September. An actor calling itself “The Seven Deadly Sins” claims far more: over 2 million CRM records and 200 million data-warehouse rows from Canva, 3.8 TB across several victims, and access to more than 300 Jira instances and 50 or more Salesforce and other instances at other companies. Canva has not confirmed any of those claims, did not answer DataBreaches.net’s questions, and the story has one outlet so far. Only Canva’s statement is confirmed. No law firm is involved. It is here because the pattern fits how firms are wired. The firm’s CRM, whether InterAction, Salesforce or Dynamics, holds client and prospect contacts, matter relationships and fee terms. It is usually connected by OAuth to marketing, event, survey, e-signature and intake tools that nobody reviews after onboarding. Each connection is a standing credential, held by a supplier the firm has never assessed, into the system that maps who the firm acts for. This is the same shape as the vendor-mediated access at Brevo on the card above, one layer further back.

High Breach

An intruder spent four months inside C-Track, the court case-management platform Thomson Reuters supplies to thirteen judicial jurisdictions — Wyoming’s courts now say case data from 2015 to 2025 was exposed

Thomson Reuters disclosed on 3 September that an unauthorised party obtained files from C-Track, the court case-management platform run by its West Publishing subsidiary. Access ran from March 2026 until discovery on 30 June. Affected courts include the appellate courts of eleven US states — Alabama, Kentucky, Montana, Nevada, New Hampshire, North Dakota, Ohio, Pennsylvania, South Carolina, Tennessee and Wyoming — plus the US Virgin Islands and Ontario, Canada. The exposed material may include names, Social Security numbers, driver’s licence numbers, dates of birth, and medical and health-insurance information, alongside confidential, redacted or sealed court information — the detail that separates this from an ordinary vendor breach, because sealed records exist precisely because a court ordered them withheld. Thomson Reuters says C-Track itself was not disrupted and that there is no evidence to date of fraud or misuse, and twelve months of credit monitoring is on offer. A criminal investigation is under way in at least North Dakota. For firms litigating in the named jurisdictions the exposure is matter-level rather than firm-level, and the scoping work sits with individual courts. Downstream, the notification work sits with the courts. The North Dakota Supreme Court acknowledged on 3 September that data associated with the court was affected and that a criminal investigation is running; on 4 September the Kentucky Administrative Office of the Courts confirmed exposure covering its Supreme Court and Court of Appeals, said no individual count was yet established, and said it is coordinating with other affected states through the National Center for State Courts while Thomson Reuters reviews systems court by court. New on 24 September: the Wyoming Judicial Branch has given the first court-level scoping. Its communications director, Jacob Just, told the Wyoming Tribune Eagle that an internal preliminary review, done after the court was notified of “the incident involving West Publishing”, showed data was exposed for cases between 2015 and 2025, and that exposed personal information was “likely limited to names, dates of birth, and addresses”. The full analysis is still pending. West Publishing previously provided case-management software to both the Wyoming Supreme Court and the state’s district courts; the court says current judicial-branch networks and systems were not affected, and West Publishing is offering twelve months of credit monitoring through CTrackNotification.com. Two points for firms. A ten-year window means closed matters are in scope, not just live dockets. And Wyoming’s early read of the data types is narrower than Thomson Reuters’ own list, which included sealed and redacted material; each court is doing its own review, so wait for that court’s own answer before telling a client which applies. Other affected states have not yet published comparable scoping.

LinkedIn takes a consent judgment against ProAPIs and Netswift over mass scraping through millions of fake accounts — delete everything, stop selling it, no more bogus profiles

A federal court in California entered a consent judgment on 18 September against ProAPIs, Netswift and their chief executive, requiring them to stop scraping LinkedIn, stop selling or transferring the data already scraped, stop creating and using fake accounts, and delete everything collected. LinkedIn’s complaint, as reported by The Record, described a network of bogus accounts “numbering in the millions”, with hundreds or thousands created every day to pull profiles, company and school pages, posts and comments before LinkedIn’s blocks caught up. The company’s litigation lead, Sarah Wight, confirmed the outcome. It is a consent judgment, so there is no reasoned opinion and no new precedent, but the shape of the relief is the useful part: an injunction against the scraping, the downstream sale and the fake-account infrastructure, plus a deletion obligation — the template a platform client will want and the exposure a data-broker or AI-training client needs to hear about. Two further angles. Firms are on the receiving end of scraping: partner and associate profiles are the raw material for the fake IT-support calls described elsewhere on this page. And a firm buying tools that ingest public profile data for business development or conflicts should ask where the data came from, because a deletion order upstream can reach a dataset the firm has already paid for.
