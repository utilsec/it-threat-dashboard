Top Story

Suisun City, California shuts down its entire IT network and declares a state of emergency after malware disrupts 911 dispatch

Suisun City, a small Northern California municipality of roughly 30,000 residents, detected malware on its network and shut its entire municipal IT system down on 9 August 2026 as a containment measure. The City Council declared a local state of emergency to free up emergency resources and expedite the response, working alongside outside incident-response support. With the core network offline, 911 dispatch calls had to be rerouted through the Solano County dispatch centre so emergency response was not interrupted, while the city’s own police and fire records systems went offline. City Hall’s online services and several administrative functions were also disrupted while staff worked to rebuild systems. As of this writing the city has not said whether the malware was ransomware, whether any data was exfiltrated, or which actor, if any, is responsible; the investigation remains open. Suisun City is the sharpest of several small local-government incidents confirmed in the same window — Coweta, Oklahoma; Mitchell, South Dakota; and Coryell County, Texas each disclosed separate incidents — and none has been publicly linked to a common actor or campaign. The pattern is the operationally useful takeaway for public-sector IT leaders: small municipalities routinely run 911 dispatch, police and fire records on the same general-purpose network as email and permitting, with a fraction of the IT staff of a mid-sized enterprise, so a single opportunistic intrusion can force a choice between shutting everything down — including emergency-services connectivity — or leaving a live compromise running on systems dispatchers depend on.

Medium Incident

Ransomware hits Coweta, Oklahoma while Mitchell, South Dakota and Coryell County, Texas confirm separate municipal incidents

Coweta, Oklahoma (population roughly 12,000) confirmed a ransomware attack on 5 August 2026 that encrypted city computers and files; officials said the city’s website, its third-party billing portal and 911 emergency services continued operating normally, and that no payment-card data was exposed. Mitchell, South Dakota disclosed a separate network intrusion in the same window and has since reported its systems fully restored. Coryell County, Texas confirmed a technology disruption affecting county operations without releasing further detail on scope, cause or recovery timeline. None of the three has been publicly attributed to a specific ransomware group, and none has been formally linked to the malware that forced Suisun City, California to shut its network down the same week. Taken together, the cluster is a reminder that small municipalities and counties are a soft, repeatable target: thin IT staffing, aging infrastructure and a small pool of shared technology and billing vendors mean opportunistic ransomware crews can produce several unrelated-looking outages in a short window without needing a coordinated campaign.

High Incident

French tax authority DGFiP confirms a breach reaching 678,000 individuals and businesses via stolen VPN access

France’s DGFiP tax authority confirmed an intrusion, detected and stopped in late June 2026, in which an attacker used stolen VPN credentials together with an internal search tool to reach tax, cadastral and business-registration records. Reporting puts the total affected at 678,000 individuals and businesses. DGFiP cut off access to the sensitive systems involved and notified France’s national cybersecurity agency ANSSI and its data-protection authority. The agency has not said how the VPN access was obtained or whether it involved a compromised credential, a third party, or a separate intrusion. For public-sector IT teams, the case is a reminder that VPN access plus a legitimate internal tool — not a novel exploit — was enough to reach hundreds of thousands of citizen and business records at a national tax authority, and that detection depended on catching anomalous use of that internal tool rather than a perimeter alert.

Confirmed — intrusion detected and stopped in late June 2026 No CVE identified — stolen VPN access plus an internal tool
No single patch — credential & access-control review Action: Review VPN access logging and anomaly detection for high-privilege internal tools, not just the perimeter; confirm least-privilege access to internal search/lookup tools that can return bulk citizen records; rotate credentials tied to any account with broad VPN access.

High Incident

Attackers breach Liechtenstein’s foundations and trusts registry, accessing beneficial-owner data on ~31,000 entities

Attackers penetrated Liechtenstein’s confidential register of foundations and trusts in late July 2026, gaining access to records on roughly 31,000 registered entities. Exposed data reportedly includes beneficial owners’ names, dates of birth, nationality and place of residence; the government says financial data, addresses and phone numbers were not accessed. Officials said the country will not pay a ransom if one is demanded. The register was established in 2021 to meet international financial-transparency and anti-money-laundering standards, so it is precisely the kind of government-run system whose compromise has knock-on consequences for AML and due-diligence processes well beyond Liechtenstein’s own borders. Officials said they plan to rebuild the registry, judging continued access for international financial authorities more important than the risk of a repeat incident.

Confirmed — registry data accessed and exfiltrated No CVE identified

High Incident

Ransomware disrupts Colombia’s Ministry of Justice five days before the presidential transition

A ransomware attack struck Colombia’s Ministry of Justice on 2 August 2026, encrypting files and disrupting systems used for drug-monitoring and legal-case processing. The timing put the incident five days ahead of the country’s presidential transition, which added operational pressure to the response; that timing is noted here only as operational context. Acting officials said some files were encrypted but that they had verified no data was captured or exfiltrated, an assessment that has not been independently confirmed. The incident follows a string of intrusions against Colombian government and critical-infrastructure targets earlier in the year, including a breach at the national tax authority DIAN and a compromise affecting more than a dozen subsidiaries of the state oil company; researchers point to government and state-linked organisations expanding cloud infrastructure faster than their security capabilities as a contributing factor. For government IT teams, the case underscores that ministry-level case-management and monitoring platforms sit squarely inside ransomware crews’ target set, with downtime landing directly on active legal and regulatory processes rather than administrative overhead.

Confirmed ransomware — no data exfiltration confirmed No CVE identified

Medium Intel

Joint federal advisory profiles Gunra ransomware, naming government services among its targets

CISA and the FBI published a joint #StopRansomware advisory, AA26-222A, on 10 August 2026 profiling Gunra, a double-extortion ransomware-as-a-service operation built on leaked Conti source code. The advisory says Gunra affiliates gain initial access through internet-facing VPN gateways and firewalls, citing known Fortinet flaws CVE-2024-55591 and CVE-2025-24472 among the entry vectors, and lists government services and facilities among the sectors the group has targeted, alongside healthcare, financial services and critical infrastructure. Both cited Fortinet CVEs already have vendor patches and are established CISA KEV entries. The advisory functions as a reminder that unpatched, internet-exposed network-edge devices remain a common door for ransomware crews long after fixes exist, rather than a report of a new vulnerability.

Medium Intel

Mustang Panda adds a signed kernel-mode rootkit to its backdoor in espionage against government targets abroad

Chinese state-linked group Mustang Panda has added a signed Windows kernel-mode rootkit to its CoolClient backdoor, using an expired 2013-era code-signing certificate to hide malicious processes and network activity from security tooling. Researchers observed the updated toolset used in espionage operations against government targets in Myanmar, Mongolia, Pakistan and Russia. No US or allied government agency has been named as a target in this reporting. The technique — abusing an old but technically valid signing certificate to load a kernel driver — is notable because it can defeat detection approaches that trust signed drivers by default, a pattern government IT and security teams anywhere should account for in driver-signing and allow-list policies, rather than assuming this actor’s targeting stays confined to the countries named so far.

Active — espionage tooling observed in use against named government targets No CVE identified — signed rootkit driver, not a software vulnerability
No patch applicable — detection & driver-signing policy issue Action: Review driver-signing allow-list and block-list policies to ensure expired-but-technically-valid certificates cannot load kernel drivers; hunt for unexplained kernel-mode drivers and unexpected outbound connections on internet-facing government systems.
Suggested SLA: Driver-signing policy review — 30 days Owner:

High Intel

Windows zero-day tied to North Korea’s Lazarus Group in espionage against defence-sector targets

August’s Patch Tuesday fixed 398 CVEs, including one actively exploited zero-day: CVE-2026-68820, a use-after-free flaw in the Windows Ancillary Function Driver for WinSock (AFD.sys), rated CVSS 7.0. It is a local elevation-of-privilege issue, not a remotely exploitable flaw on its own — an attacker needs existing code execution on the target first. Check Point ties exploitation to North Korea’s Lazarus Group as part of Operation Dream Job, using trojanised PDF viewers and DLL side-loading to deploy a new backdoor called Troy against defence and aerospace targets in France, Germany, Brazil and India. CISA added the flaw to the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog on 11 August. For government and defence-adjacent IT teams, the notable part is the delivery chain — a fake job-application or recruiting lure carrying a trojanised PDF viewer — rather than the elevation-of-privilege flaw in isolation, since the flaw only becomes dangerous after that initial foothold is established.

Critical Advisory

Metabase zero-day (CVSS 10.0) exploited for unauthenticated admin takeover — federal deadline 14 Aug

CVE-2026-72898 is an unauthenticated SQL injection in Metabase’s /reset_password endpoint that grants full admin access, rated CVSS 10.0 under both v3.1 and v4.0. It affects open-source Metabase 0.58.0 through 0.63.x before 0.63.5 and Enterprise 1.58.0 through 1.63.x before 1.63.5. CISA added it to the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog on 11 August 2026 with a federal remediation deadline of 14 August, and it was exploited as a zero-day before a patch was available. Confirmed victims include laptop maker Framework, whose customer names, emails, addresses, phone numbers and billing information were exposed, and ShipMonk, Trezor’s fulfilment partner, where roughly 13,689 customer records were affected without any seed phrases or keys. Government agencies and contractors that run self-hosted Metabase for internal analytics or dashboards should treat any internet-reachable instance as compromised until proven otherwise, not merely unpatched.

Critical Advisory

Progress Kemp LoadMaster command-injection flaw draws hundreds of exploitation attempts within days of KEV listing

CVE-2026-8037 is an unauthenticated command-injection vulnerability in Progress Kemp LoadMaster, rated CVSS 9.6. CISA added it to the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog on 7 August 2026, triggering a Binding Operational Directive 26-04 remediation deadline of 10 August for federal civilian agencies. Researchers logged 792 exploitation attempts from 65 distinct IP addresses across 18 countries. LoadMaster appliances are frequently deployed as load balancers in front of internal and public-facing government applications, so a compromise can sit directly in the path of production traffic. Progress has published patches; no workaround is documented for agencies that cannot patch immediately.

GeoServer patches an unauthenticated SQL injection affecting PostGIS-backed layers used across public-sector geospatial systems

GeoServer released 3.0.1, 2.28.5 and 2.27.6 on 14 August 2026, fixing an unauthenticated SQL injection in the jsonArrayContains filter against PostGIS-backed layers. The project’s advisory, GHSA-mqjf-5f49-2fjh, rates the issue High; no CVE number has been assigned and the project has not published a CVSS score — a CVSS 9.8 figure circulating in some press coverage does not come from the project and should not be cited. Exploitation attempts began within hours of disclosure on 12 August, two days before the fix shipped. GeoServer is widely used across federal, state and local government for geospatial data sharing, mapping portals and GIS infrastructure, which makes this an unusually broad-reach advisory for the sector even without a CVE or CVSS number attached.

Medium Policy

UK regulator reprimands criminal-records office after intrusions went undetected for roughly two years

The UK Information Commissioner’s Office issued a reprimand, without a fine, against ACRO Criminal Records Office after three intrusions between 2021 and 2023 went undetected for roughly two years. Investigators found the intrusions exploited an unpatched Kentico content-management system, and that antivirus alerts which would have flagged the activity went unread. Data on nearly 11,000 people, including domestic-violence survivors, was staged for exfiltration. The ICO’s findings point to two ordinary but consequential failures rather than a sophisticated attack: a CMS patch that was not applied, and security alerts that existed but were not acted on. For any government or public-sector body running a public-facing CMS, the case is a reminder that alerting infrastructure only provides protection if someone is watching it, and that reprimands without fines still carry reputational and oversight consequences for the agencies involved.

Medium Incident

Contractor breach exposes ~300 Scottish prosecution-service employee records via a mandatory data-maturity assessment

Scotland’s Crown Office and Procurator Fiscal Service disclosed on 13 August 2026 that a third-party contractor running a mandatory Data Maturity Assessment for Scottish public bodies suffered a data breach, after suspicious activity was detected on 5 August. Roughly 300 COPFS employees had names, job titles and work email addresses exposed; the agency says case, victim and witness data was not affected. Because multiple Scottish government agencies use the same contractor for the same assessment programme, the exposure may extend to other agencies that submitted data through it; the full scope had not been confirmed as of the report. The incident is a familiar shape for public-sector supply-chain risk: a vendor engaged for a periodic compliance assessment holds data on an ongoing basis, with no continuous monitoring between assessment cycles.

Confirmed — contractor-side compromise; case/victim data reportedly unaffected No CVE identified
No single patch — vendor risk management Action: Inventory every contractor holding government employee or case data on an ongoing basis, not just at onboarding or assessment time; confirm which other public bodies share the same vendor and notify them; require contractors to disclose breach detection and monitoring capability as a condition of engagement.
