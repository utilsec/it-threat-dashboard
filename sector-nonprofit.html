Top Story

An AWS key left in public build files exposed supporter data for 1,500+ UK charities in 87 minutes

An Amazon Web Services access key leaked in Beacon CRM’s public JavaScript build artifacts let an attacker download the platform’s entire dataset during an 87-minute window on 27 July. Supporter names, email addresses, phone numbers and donation records belonging to more than 1,500 UK charities were exposed; named victims include The Survivor’s Trust, Shrewsbury and Telford Hospital Charity, and Victim Support. The UK Information Commissioner’s Office found that the charities themselves bore no responsibility for the failure. One technical detail deserves to be pulled out and remembered, because it undoes an assumption a great many nonprofit vendor questionnaires rest on: the data was encrypted at rest, and it made no difference whatsoever. The credential was valid, so the platform decrypted the data on the way out, exactly as it would have for a legitimate request. “Encrypted at rest” on a supplier’s security page tells you nothing about whether a stolen key can walk out with the plaintext. The useful questions for a CRM, fundraising or case-management provider are narrower and much harder to answer with marketing copy: does it scan its own build output for secrets before publishing, how quickly does it rotate cloud credentials, and would it notice a single client pulling the entire database inside two hours? This is the honest shape of nonprofit risk this cycle — charities are exposed overwhelmingly through the shared platforms they buy, not through anyone targeting them directly.

Breach at UK research contractor Data Orchard raises supplier questions for its nonprofit clients

Data Orchard, a UK research and data-analysis organisation, has been identified as the source of the breach disclosed by the Crown Office and Procurator Fiscal Service, Scotland’s prosecution service. That link is confirmed. What is not confirmed is any impact on the contractor’s other clients, which reporting says include the World Wildlife Fund and the Welsh government — no exposure of their data has been established, and it should not be assumed. The practical point for this sector is procedural rather than alarming. Charities routinely hand beneficiary records, impact-measurement data and benchmarking submissions to small specialist research firms that sit well outside whatever passes for a vendor-review process, and the same firm often serves dozens of organisations in the same field. If you have shared datasets with an evaluation, assessment or benchmarking partner, this is a reasonable week to ask that partner directly whether it was affected, what data of yours it still holds, and when it intends to delete it.

High Incident

Boston Healthcare for the Homeless Program confirms a breach affecting more than 185,000 people

Boston Healthcare for the Homeless Program, a nonprofit safety-net provider, has confirmed a data breach affecting over 185,000 Massachusetts residents; it is one of several nonprofit and community health organisations disclosing incidents in the same period. The uncomfortable arithmetic facing mission-driven service providers is on full display here. Organisations supporting people experiencing homelessness, domestic abuse, addiction or immigration difficulty hold some of the most sensitive records anyone holds — where a person sleeps, what they are being treated for, who they are hiding from — on security budgets far thinner than a comparable commercial operator would carry, because every pound spent on controls is a pound not spent on the mission. Where a breach at a bank costs money, a breach here can cost a client their safety. That is worth stating plainly to a board that is weighing security spend against programme spend.

Medium Incident

Attackers breach Liechtenstein’s foundations and trusts registry, downloading beneficial-owner data

Attackers gained access to Liechtenstein’s public register of foundations and trusts and downloaded beneficial-owner names and other personal details covering roughly 31,000 entities. The government confirmed the breach and said it will not pay a ransom. The register underpins due-diligence and anti-money-laundering checks used across the philanthropic and trust-administration world, including by foundations that fund or operate as nonprofits. A leak of beneficial-ownership data is primarily a privacy exposure for the individuals named — often donors, founders or trustees who structured their giving through a foundation — rather than a threat to any single charity’s day-to-day operations, but it is a reminder that the legal structures nonprofits rely on for governance also concentrate sensitive personal data in centralized registries outside any charity’s own control.

High Incident

Akira affiliate disables EDR by forcing Windows into Safe Mode after breaching a SonicWall VPN without MFA

Huntress documented an Akira ransomware affiliate breaching a network through a SonicWall VPN account that had no multi-factor authentication enabled, then forcing the endpoint into Safe Mode with Networking — a Windows startup mode most EDR and antivirus agents do not load into. With defenses disabled for roughly ten minutes, the actor stole data, though in this documented case it failed to encrypt it. The technique needs nothing more exotic than a stolen or guessed VPN password on an account without MFA, a configuration that is common at nonprofits running remote-access VPNs on a limited IT budget. Because it abuses a startup mode rather than a software flaw, there is no patch for it; the defense is entirely about VPN account hygiene and monitoring for unexpected reboots into Safe Mode.

High Intel

CISA and the FBI refresh the Medusa advisory: 500+ victims and new flaws weaponised in about a day

CISA and the FBI updated their #StopRansomware advisory on Medusa (AA25-071A) on 18 August. The cumulative victim tally passed 500 critical-infrastructure organisations as of April 2026, up from roughly 300 a year earlier — about 200 new victims in twelve months. The detail that matters most for thinly-staffed IT teams is the tempo: the group buys exploits rather than developing them, has been observed weaponising newly disclosed vulnerabilities within roughly 24 hours, and in some cases has used exploits before public disclosure. No nonprofit is named, and the listed targets are healthcare, critical manufacturing, the defence industrial base, government, IT and financial services. But the 24-hour figure is the part to internalise, because a monthly patch cycle — which is what a charity with one IT generalist or a part-time managed-service provider can realistically sustain — is not a defence against that speed. The compensating controls carry the load instead: MFA everywhere, offline backups that someone has actually restored from, and a short list of internet-facing assets that a named person watches.

Joint advisory details Gunra ransomware, which reaches victims through exposed VPN and firewall gear

CISA, the FBI and international partners published a joint advisory (AA26-222A, 10 Aug) on Gunra, a Conti-derived ransomware-as-a-service operation running double-extortion campaigns against healthcare, government and critical-infrastructure victims. Initial access typically comes through internet-facing VPN gateways and firewalls; the advisory cites Fortinet flaws CVE-2024-55591 and CVE-2025-24472 among observed entry points. No nonprofit victim is named, but the access pattern is exactly the one that matters most for this sector: an unpatched or unmanaged remote-access appliance sitting on the internet, often the only edge device a small organisation has and rarely the one that gets timely attention.

Medium Research

A self-propagating worm in the npm package “keyv” poisoned hundreds of others in about 30 minutes

Researchers documented a worm that used stolen publishing credentials to compromise the widely-used npm package keyv, then automatically poisoned hundreds of further packages within roughly half an hour, harvesting developer credentials, CI/CD tokens and cloud keys along the way; in some cases it planted editor or task hooks that could trigger the payload later. Most nonprofits do not maintain large codebases, but many run a donation page, event site or custom tool built by a volunteer developer or contractor pulling in open-source JavaScript packages — the same kind of dependency chain, and the same kind of build-time credential exposure, that put Beacon CRM’s AWS key into a public build artifact in the first place.

Critical Advisory

CISA adds four actively-exploited flaws to KEV with a 21 August federal deadline

CISA added four vulnerabilities to the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog on 18 August, giving federal agencies until 21 August to remediate: CVE-2026-33824, a double free in the Microsoft Windows IKE service extensions (IKEEXT) leading to remote code execution; CVE-2026-55040, a weak-authentication flaw in Microsoft SharePoint allowing a security-feature bypass; CVE-2026-65400, improper authentication in Apple macOS Screen Sharing; and CVE-2026-59310 in VMware vCenter, covered separately below. The federal deadline does not bind charities, and nobody should pretend otherwise — but it is the clearest free signal available about which flaws attackers are using right now, and it is a defensible basis for telling a board why a patch cannot wait for the next quarterly window. Windows and SharePoint are close to universal across the sector, and macOS Screen Sharing matters for the many small nonprofits running on Macs with remote support enabled.

Metabase flaw exploited as a zero-day before a patch existed, now in CISA KEV at CVSS 10

CVE-2026-72898, an unauthenticated SQL-injection flaw in analytics tool Metabase reachable via its /reset_password endpoint, grants full admin access and carries the maximum CVSS 10.0 score. It was exploited as a zero-day before a fix existed and was added to CISA’s KEV catalog on 11 Aug, with a federal remediation deadline of 14 Aug. Confirmed victims include laptop maker Framework and Trezor’s fulfilment partner ShipMonk. Metabase and similar self-hosted BI dashboards are exactly the kind of tool some charities and foundations use to build donor and grant-reporting analytics on top of their CRM data — if yours is one of them, this is the week to check the version.

Critical Advisory

VMware vCenter RCE flaw enters CISA KEV after mass exploitation across 47 countries

CVE-2026-59310 (CVSS 9.8), a directory-traversal flaw in vCenter Server’s syslog service giving unauthenticated remote code execution, was added to CISA’s KEV catalog on 18 August with a 21 August federal deadline. Broadcom patched it on 29 July (VMSA-2026-0006) and published no workaround. First victims were seen contacting attacker infrastructure on 3 August, and roughly 95% of an eventual 361 victim IPs across 47 countries had appeared by 5 August. The observed chain runs from path traversal to root on vCenter, then a cron job for reverse-SSH persistence, and in some cases ransomware built from leaked Babuk source. vCenter owns the virtualisation layer — guest VMs, datastores and usually the backups — so the exposure for nonprofits is mostly indirect but real: if a managed-service provider or a donated hosting arrangement runs your servers, this is the question to put to them in writing this week.

National League of Cities and CyberAlliance offer free security help to under-resourced local governments

The National League of Cities, a membership association representing US municipalities, is partnering with the nonprofit CyberAlliance to provide free cybersecurity assistance to under-resourced local governments. The programme leans on an AI assistant, Sally, to translate technical assessment findings into plain recommendations that city managers and elected officials can actually act on. It follows the Iranian-linked compromises of water utilities in several states, which exposed how little in-house security capacity many small public bodies have. The model is worth watching from the association-and-membership side of this sector: a membership body commissioning assessment centrally, once, for organisations that could never each fund it, and then doing the translation work so a non-technical board can approve a budget line. Sector associations, umbrella bodies and funders weighing something similar for their own members now have a live example to point at rather than a hypothetical.

UK data-protection regulator reprimands a public body for a two-year-undetected breach — a preview of charity-sector scrutiny

The UK Information Commissioner’s Office reprimanded ACRO Criminal Records Office — without a fine — after finding three intrusions between 2021 and 2023 exploited an unpatched Kentico CMS and went undetected for roughly two years despite unread antivirus alerts; data on nearly 11,000 people, including domestic-violence survivors, was staged for exfiltration. ACRO is a government body, not a charity, but the ICO regulates UK charities’ data protection to the same standard, and the failures it cited — an unpatched public-facing CMS and alerts nobody was watching — are exactly the gaps a resource-constrained nonprofit is most likely to have too. It is a useful preview of what a regulator expects to see documented if a charity’s own donor or beneficiary data is ever exposed.
