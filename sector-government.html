Top Story

CISA and FBI refresh the Medusa advisory: 500+ critical-infrastructure victims, and exploits weaponised inside 24 hours

CISA and the FBI republished their #StopRansomware advisory on Medusa, AA25-071A, on 18 August 2026. The cumulative victim tally passed 500 critical-infrastructure organisations as of April 2026, up from roughly 300 in March 2025 — about 200 new victims in a year. Government facilities sit among the group’s core target sectors alongside healthcare, critical manufacturing, the defence industrial base, IT and financial services. The operationally load-bearing finding in the refresh is about speed, not volume: the agencies report that Medusa weaponises newly disclosed vulnerabilities within roughly 24 hours, and has been observed using exploits up to a week before public disclosure — consistent with an operation that buys working exploits rather than developing them. That timeline is the part public-sector IT leaders should act on. A monthly maintenance window, a 30-day change-advisory cycle or a quarterly patch campaign is not a defence against an adversary that is exploiting on the day of disclosure and occasionally before it. The practical response is to split the estate: internet-facing and remote-access assets move to an emergency track measured in hours to days, driven by CISA KEV and vendor PSIRT feeds, while everything else stays on the routine cycle. Where a patch cannot land inside that window, compensating controls — removing the service from the internet, restricting management interfaces to trusted networks, adding authentication in front of it — are the fallback, not a deferral request. The advisory also carries current tradecraft, indicators and detection guidance worth folding into hunt queries rather than filing unread.

High Incident

French tax authority DGFiP confirms a breach affecting about 680,000 people — discovered only when the attacker boasted on a forum

France’s Direction générale des Finances publiques has confirmed a data breach affecting roughly 680,000 individuals and businesses. According to reporting published on 17 August 2026, the attackers combined a compromised employee account with a compromised third-party account and used that access to exfiltrate records between June and July 2026. The data taken includes reference tax income figures, withholding tax rates, company identifiers and cadastral property data; passwords are not believed to have been taken. The detail public-sector security teams should sit with is how the intrusion came to light: it surfaced when the actor bragged about it on a hacking forum, not through internal monitoring. Two working accounts — one staff, one partner — produced a two-month bulk extraction of citizen and business tax data without tripping an alert, which makes this a detection-engineering problem rather than a patching one. The exposed fields are also a ready-made fraud kit: income figures plus property records plus company identifiers give a caller enough verifiable detail to pass as the tax authority in a follow-on scam, so the downstream phishing wave is a predictable second stage.

No patch applicable — identity, access and detection controls Action: Alert on volume, not just on login — set thresholds for bulk record retrieval through internal lookup and reporting tools, and review whether any single account can pull citizen records at scale unchallenged. Apply the same monitoring to third-party and partner accounts, which are routinely exempted from staff-account controls. Enforce phishing-resistant MFA on staff and contractor access, and prepare public warning messaging about follow-on tax-themed fraud using the exposed fields.

Medium Incident

Assessment contractor breach exposes around 300 Scottish prosecution-service staff records, with the blast radius still open

Scotland’s Crown Office and Procurator Fiscal Service disclosed that a third-party contractor running its Data Maturity Assessment suffered a breach, after suspicious activity was detected around 5 August 2026. Names, job roles and work email addresses for roughly 300 COPFS staff were exposed. The service says case files and victim and witness data were not affected. The reason this is worth more than a 300-record footnote is the programme behind it: the same data maturity assessment covers multiple Scottish public bodies, so the exposure may extend to other agencies that submitted data through the same contractor, and the full scope was not confirmed at the time of reporting. Reporting identifies the contractor as Data Orchard, a research organisation whose other clients are said to include the World Wildlife Fund and the Welsh government — that attribution comes from press reporting rather than an official statement. The shape of the incident is a familiar public-sector one: a vendor engaged for a periodic compliance exercise retains agency data continuously, with no monitoring obligation between assessment cycles, and staff directory data of exactly this kind is the raw material for targeted phishing and help-desk impersonation against a prosecution service.

Require contractors to state their detection and breach-notification capability as a contract condition, and set a retention and deletion clause for assessment data. Brief affected staff that their names, roles and work addresses are now in circulation and to expect targeted phishing.

Attackers breach Liechtenstein’s foundations and trusts registry, accessing beneficial-owner data on ~31,000 entities

Attackers penetrated Liechtenstein’s confidential register of foundations and trusts in late July 2026, gaining access to records on roughly 31,000 registered entities. Exposed data reportedly includes beneficial owners’ names, dates of birth, nationality and place of residence; the government says financial data, addresses and phone numbers were not accessed. Officials said they plan to rebuild the registry, judging continued access for international financial authorities more important than the risk of a repeat incident.

Medium Incident

Still recovering: four small US jurisdictions hit in the same window, one of them rerouting 911 dispatch to the county

Suisun City, California — a Northern California municipality of roughly 30,000 residents — detected malware and shut its entire municipal IT network down on 9 August 2026 as a containment measure, with the City Council declaring a local state of emergency to speed up the response. With the network offline, 911 dispatch had to be rerouted through the Solano County dispatch centre, and the city’s own police and fire records systems went offline alongside City Hall online services. Three other jurisdictions confirmed separate incidents in the same window: Coweta, Oklahoma disclosed a ransomware attack on 5 August that encrypted city computers while its website, third-party billing portal and 911 service kept running; Mitchell, South Dakota reported an intrusion and has since restored its systems; and Coryell County, Texas confirmed a technology disruption without releasing scope. None has been attributed to a named actor, and none has been publicly linked to the others — which is the point. Four unrelated opportunistic intrusions in one week is what a soft target class looks like, not a campaign. Small municipalities routinely run dispatch, police and fire records on the same general-purpose network as email and permitting, with a fraction of the IT staffing of a mid-sized enterprise, so a single intrusion forces a choice between pulling the plug on emergency-services connectivity or leaving a live compromise running underneath it.

Confirmed — malware and ransomware incidents at four separate jurisdictions, 5–9 Aug 2026 No actor attributed; no common campaign established
No single CVE — incident response and recovery Action: Segment public-safety systems — dispatch, police and fire records — from general municipal IT so one compromise cannot take both down, and rehearse the manual fallback before an incident forces it. Confirm mutual-aid dispatch arrangements with the county are current and tested. Verify that offline, immutable backups for financial, billing and records systems actually restore on a tested schedule, enforce MFA on all remote and administrative access, and review the security posture of shared billing and IT vendors that serve several small jurisdictions at once.

High Intel

DOJ charges 17 Mabna Institute members over a 13-year campaign against universities, companies and federal and state agencies

The Department of Justice announced a superseding indictment on 18 August 2026 charging 17 people connected to Iran’s Mabna Institute, described as linked to the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps, over a credential-theft campaign running since 2013. The charging documents describe targeting of 144 US universities, 42 private companies and at least five federal and state agencies, with more than 100,000 professor accounts targeted, roughly 8,000 of them compromised, and in excess of 31 terabytes of research data and intellectual property exfiltrated. As with any indictment, the allegations are unproven in court. The security lesson is in how ordinary the tradecraft was: sustained, patient credential phishing against academic and agency accounts at scale, not exotic exploitation. Thirteen years of access to research and agency systems was bought with password reuse and users typing credentials into convincing lookalike pages. That makes the countermeasure equally ordinary and equally unglamorous — phishing-resistant MFA on every account that can reach research data, agency records or a document repository, and alerting on bulk download activity from library, repository and file-share systems that normally see one document at a time.

Active since 2013 per the indictment — credential phishing at scale; charges are allegations No CVE identified — credential theft, not exploitation of a software flaw
No patch applicable — identity and data-egress controls Action: Move every account with access to research data, grant records or agency document repositories onto phishing-resistant MFA rather than one-time codes. Alert on bulk retrieval from library, repository and file-share systems, and on logins from residential proxy or unusual geographies for research accounts. Review whether federated academic and agency identity providers apply the same conditional-access policy as corporate accounts, and check retrospectively for long-lived sessions and legacy authentication protocols still permitted.

High Intel

Windows zero-day tied to North Korea’s Lazarus Group in espionage against defence-sector targets

August’s Patch Tuesday fixed 398 CVEs, including one actively exploited zero-day: CVE-2026-68820, a use-after-free flaw in the Windows Ancillary Function Driver for WinSock (AFD.sys), rated CVSS 7.0. It is a local elevation-of-privilege issue, not a remotely exploitable flaw on its own — an attacker needs existing code execution on the target first. Check Point ties exploitation to North Korea’s Lazarus Group as part of Operation Dream Job, using trojanised PDF viewers and DLL side-loading to deploy a new backdoor called Troy against defence and aerospace targets in France, Germany, Brazil and India. CISA added the flaw to the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog on 11 August. For government and defence-adjacent IT teams, the notable part is the delivery chain — a fake job-application or recruiting lure carrying a trojanised PDF viewer — rather than the elevation-of-privilege flaw in isolation, since the flaw only becomes dangerous after that initial foothold is established.

Critical Advisory

CISA adds four actively exploited flaws to KEV with a 21 August federal deadline — Windows IKEEXT, vCenter, SharePoint and macOS

On 18 August 2026 CISA added four vulnerabilities to the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog, each carrying a federal civilian remediation deadline of 21 August. They are CVE-2026-33824, a double free in the Microsoft Windows IKE extensions service (IKEEXT) leading to remote code execution; CVE-2026-59310, a path traversal in Broadcom VMware vCenter allowing code execution; CVE-2026-55040, a weak-authentication flaw in Microsoft SharePoint enabling a security-feature bypass; and CVE-2026-65400, improper authentication in Apple macOS Screen Sharing. The vCenter entry deserves separate attention: Broadcom published VMSA-2026-0006 on 29 July for a directory-traversal flaw in the vCenter syslog service with no workaround, and researchers subsequently tracked roughly 361 victim IP addresses across 47 countries, with the attacker gaining root on vCenter, planting a cron job for reverse-SSH persistence and, in some cases, deploying ransomware derived from leaked Babuk source. For agencies that is a hypervisor-management compromise, not an application one. Every product in this set is standard government estate — Windows servers running IKE for IPsec, vCenter under the virtualisation platform, SharePoint holding internal records, and managed Macs — so this is a genuinely broad remediation cycle rather than four niche items.

Critical Advisory

Ray code-injection flaw added to KEV with a 20 August deadline — no authentication on critical endpoints

CISA added CVE-2025-62593, a code-injection vulnerability in the Ray distributed-computing framework rated CVSS 9.4, to the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog on 17 August 2026 with a federal remediation deadline of 20 August. The underlying condition is a design decision rather than a coding slip: Ray ships with no authentication on critical endpoints. That matters more than it first appears, because it means an internet-exposed cluster is not required for exploitation. A malicious webpage or advertisement combined with DNS rebinding is enough to reach a developer’s local Ray environment from the browser and execute code on it. Agencies, national labs and federal contractors running AI and machine-learning workloads should assume Ray is present on data-science workstations and in analytics environments even where it was never formally procured, since it is commonly pulled in as a dependency of ML tooling. The fix is available in Ray 2.52.0.

High Advisory

Windows Task Host privilege-escalation flaw now abused by ransomware gangs — a hunt-for-stragglers signal, not a new patch cycle

CISA updated its Known Exploited Vulnerabilities entry for CVE-2025-60710 on 18 August 2026 to record that ransomware operators are now abusing it. The flaw is a link-following weakness in the Windows Task Host that lets a low-privileged local user escalate to SYSTEM on Windows 11 and Windows Server 2025. Nothing about the fix has changed: Microsoft patched it in November 2025 and CISA added it to KEV on 13 April 2026. What changed is the threat context, and that is exactly why it is worth a look rather than a shrug. A local privilege-escalation flaw is only interesting to an intruder who already has a foothold, which is precisely the position a ransomware affiliate is in after a phishing or valid-account intrusion — it converts one compromised standard user into domain-relevant SYSTEM access. For government IT the task is not a new patch cycle but a straggler hunt: the machines that missed the November 2025 cumulative update are, almost by definition, the unmanaged, re-imaged, long-lived or seasonally used devices that will also be missing everything since.

Medium Advisory

Researchers disclose a NASA ground-control software weakness allowing commands to be issued without authentication

Researchers disclosed on 18 August 2026 a weakness in NASA ground control software that allows commands to be issued without authentication. No in-the-wild exploitation has been reported. The value of the disclosure for the wider government and defence audience is what it says about a whole class of systems rather than this one product. Mission, aerospace and ground-segment software is often decades old in lineage and was designed on the assumption that the command path is physically isolated and that anyone who can reach it is already authorised. When that software is later deployed on routable networks, connected to contractor sites or reachable through a shared management VLAN, the missing authentication check that was once a non-issue becomes the whole attack. Agencies and defence contractors running specialised telemetry, command-and-control or ground-segment platforms should assume the same inherited assumption is present in their own estate until they have checked, because these systems typically sit outside the scope of the vulnerability-management programme that covers ordinary enterprise IT.

The National Security Science and Technology Strategy was released on 19 August 2026. Structured around four pillars, it names cybersecurity, the resilience of operational technology and industrial control systems, and technology supply chains as explicit national priorities. It is a strategy document rather than a directive: it carries no binding remediation requirement of its own and does not change any existing deadline. Its practical effect is downstream. The audience that should read it now is therefore not only federal agency security teams but the contractors, grantees, national laboratories and university research programmes that will be asked to demonstrate alignment with it in future awards. Reading it early is cheaper than retrofitting to it after it lands in a contract clause.
