Forged legal requests sent from a genuine ministry mailbox pried passport and financial data out of Revolut for months — the sender check passed because the account was real

Revolut disclosed on 14 September that “an unauthorized third party utilized a legitimate government agency domain email to submit fraudulent requests for information”. Reporting on 17–18 September filled in how. According to SecurityWeek, citing infostealer researchers Hudson Rock, an infostealer compromised the email account of an employee of Italy’s Ministry of the Interior on its certified-mail (PEC) domain; Hudson Rock found around 300 compromised logins for that domain in infostealer logs. The account was used to send fraudulent government data requests to Revolut Bank UAB in Lithuania — which American Banker says was apparently chosen because EU law obliges it to answer European Investigation Orders — over roughly five to six months. About 680 high-net-worth customers are reported affected, none of them in the US, and the data included passport details, contact details and financial information. Those figures come from researchers and reporting, not from Revolut, which still says only “a limited number of customers” and has notified regulators and law enforcement. An actor calling itself “IAmNotAVillain” posted a $3 million ransom demand on 16 September; Revolut says it has received no direct contact or demand. The actor also claims 147 GB from an Italian law-enforcement agency, which Italian police are investigating. This belongs on a legal page because production orders, subpoenas and law-enforcement data requests pass through firms constantly — received for clients, advised on and answered. The lesson is that sender authentication proves only which mailbox sent a message, not that the person behind it had authority, and the pressure to comply quickly is exactly what the attacker relies on.

A hijacked Brevo CDN key turned embedded sign-up forms and chat widgets into a malware channel for more than four hours — check the firm website if it uses them

Email-marketing platform Brevo (formerly Sendinblue) has confirmed a two-stage compromise first reported by e-commerce security firm Sansec on 16 September. Attackers obtained a long-lived Cloudflare API key with full account permissions that had been hardcoded in Brevo’s application source code, and used it to deploy a Cloudflare Worker that rewrote content at the CDN edge on 14 September, from about 16:07 to 20:30 UTC. Affected assets included brevo.com, sendinblue.com, the sibforms.com form host, the login and account portals, and the forms, Conversations chat widget and SDK loader scripts that customers embed on their own websites. The Worker also stripped Content-Security-Policy headers. Brevo says that because it acted at the edge, origin servers and files were unmodified and standard integrity checks did not detect the change. Ordinary visitors to affected sites were shown a fake “Cloudflare, verify you are human” page telling them to paste and run a command — the ClickFix technique. Visitors with a live WordPress administrator session had the script try to install a malicious plugin called “Web Media Optimizer” as a persistent backdoor. Sansec estimates more than 100,000 sites were exposed. Earlier, on 10 September, attackers had abused an improperly scoped SAML single sign-on path to reach 138 Brevo customer accounts, exporting contacts from 43 and sending phishing from six. No law firm has been named. The exposure for a firm is its own website: if a marketing page, newsletter or client-alert sign-up embeds Brevo forms, chat or tracking scripts, it served this code to visitors in the window, and anyone logged in to its WordPress admin at the time may have left behind a backdoored site. Scripts that marketing added are easy to miss on an IT asset register.

Still the sector’s defining campaign: Silent Ransom Group works law firms by phone, and sometimes by walking through the front door

Carried forward, and now with a named victim: on 14 September DataBreaches.net reported that this group breached Greenberg Traurig and leaked the data after talks failed (see the Top Story). The firm has filed notices with state regulators. This is still the most likely way a firm actually gets hit. The group tracked as Silent Ransom Group, Luna Moth, Chatty Spider and UNC3753 has been active since 2022 and targeting law firms since spring 2023. An FBI FLASH alert issued in May 2026 and Google Mandiant’s June 2026 reporting on a targeted campaign against US law firms both describe the operation as still running; no update to either has appeared since. The tradecraft is almost entirely human. Operators call or email posing as IT support, talk a partner, paralegal or billing clerk into a remote-support session, and install a legitimate remote monitoring and management tool. Exfiltration runs over WinSCP or a hidden or renamed copy of Rclone, with data pushed to Google Drive, Microsoft OneDrive, external hard drives or USB storage. There is frequently no encryption stage at all — the leverage is the client material itself, which in a firm means privileged files that cannot be restored away. The outlier detail, and the one that breaks most legal threat models, is that operators have turned up in person, claiming they need to image a device or take a backup to deal with the effects of a phishing email. Halcyon’s research centre ranked law firms the fourth most-targeted industry in early 2026, attributing it to the sensitivity of client data, regulatory pressure to resolve incidents quickly and a perceived willingness to pay. Read this alongside the ScreenConnect card further down the page: the remote-support tooling this campaign relies on being opened for it is the same class of tool now being exploited in worm-like attacks, patched and KEV-listed. The FBI’s own recommendations are unglamorous and correct: verify visitor identity against ID, deploy phishing-resistant multi-factor authentication, restrict remote access and external-drive permissions on sensitive systems, and train staff to recognise the pretext.

That is a useful reference point for privacy practices when clients ask whether an AI-driven intrusion changes their analysis.

Microsoft documents help-desk vishing that enrols the caller’s own phone, then quietly drains SharePoint and Exchange through the Graph API

Microsoft researchers detailed on 10 September 2026 at least two initial-access-broker clusters — tracked as Storm-3032 and Storm-3121 — running voice-phishing campaigns since May 2026. The operators call staff while impersonating the IT help desk, steer the victim’s personal device to a convincing fake Microsoft sign-in page on a lookalike domain of the “company.add-passkey.com” pattern, and capture credentials and session tokens. With a token in hand they abuse the Microsoft Graph API to enumerate and exfiltrate data from SharePoint, OneDrive and Exchange in small, periodic transfers rather than one conspicuous bulk pull. Access is reportedly sold on to extortion groups including ShinyHunters. Microsoft says it has not yet tied these campaigns to a confirmed corporate breach, and no law firm has been named as a victim — this is a documented technique, not an incident report. It is carried here because the technique maps almost exactly onto how firms are built. A firm’s privileged material does not sit in a bespoke application an attacker has to learn; it sits in SharePoint sites and mailboxes that Graph is designed to read. Three details deserve a firm’s attention specifically. First, the bring-your-own-device angle defeats endpoint controls entirely — the fee-earner’s personal phone is not carrying your EDR agent, so the credential theft happens somewhere you have no telemetry. Second, Graph exfiltration is API traffic to a Microsoft endpoint the tenant legitimately talks to all day; without mailbox and SharePoint audit logging switched on and actually retained, there is no record to reconstruct afterwards. That is not only a detection problem but an evidentiary one: a firm that cannot show which matters were reached cannot scope client notification, cannot advise on privilege waiver, and cannot answer an e-discovery custody question about its own custody. Third, the slow, periodic transfer pattern is chosen precisely to stay under volume-based alerting. Note this is a separate cluster set from the PREY-0058 activity carried elsewhere on this page; the recurrence of the help desk as the entry point across unrelated crews is itself the finding.

Attackers have started phishing the passkey itself — talking Microsoft 365 users into registering the attacker’s key on their own account

Researchers reported on 13 September 2026 two related campaigns against Microsoft 365 and connected cloud accounts. The first is volume: more than a million scam emails aimed at ACH and wire fraud. The second is the one that changes something. Rather than trying to intercept or relay an authentication, the attacker uses passkey-themed social engineering — a prompt to “set up” or “re-register” a passkey — to walk the user through authorising an attacker-controlled key on their own account. Nothing about the cryptography fails. Passkeys remain phishing-resistant at the point of sign-in; what is being attacked is the enrolment workflow, which is a human process like any other and in most tenants is guarded far more loosely than the sign-in it protects. The consequence is worse than a stolen password, because the attacker ends up holding a legitimate, durable credential that satisfies multi-factor authentication and survives a password reset — the standard containment step does nothing. CISA and NIST set out how cloud identity tokens should be protected — one-hour lifetimes and 90-day key rotation give firms concrete questions for every SaaS supplier

CISA and NIST have published NIST IR 8587, joint guidance on protecting the identity tokens and signing keys that cloud services use to authenticate users and applications. The recommendations are specific: access and identity tokens valid for no more than an hour, with expired tokens rejected outright; signing keys rotated every 90 days for high-impact systems and at least annually for others; keys held in hardware-backed or isolated storage, with signing done in isolated environments for high-impact systems; a mandatory audience claim, so a token issued for one service cannot be replayed against another; and tokens never written to logs. The report cites two precedents — the 2020 SAML-forgery supply-chain attack and the incident in which forged tokens exposed more than 60,000 government emails. It is voluntary and aimed at federal agencies, cloud providers and the organisations that buy cloud services. Firms are in the last group, and this page shows why it matters. Nearly every campaign on it this month — the BigBear phishing service, the PREY-0058 and Storm-3032 help-desk crews, the passkey-enrolment abuse — ends with the attacker holding a stolen session token rather than a password. How long that token stays valid decides whether a phish becomes weeks of quiet access to a matter mailbox. Token lifetime is partly the firm’s own configuration and partly the supplier’s design, so the guidance is useful on both sides of the contract.

The EU Cyber Resilience Act’s 24-hour reporting duty is now live — for most firms the obligation is a client’s, and the first call will arrive well inside the clock

The incident-reporting provisions of the EU Cyber Resilience Act took effect on 11–12 September 2026. Manufacturers of products with digital elements placed on the EU market must now notify actively exploited vulnerabilities and serious security incidents within a 24-hour window. For the typical law firm the direct obligation is nil — a firm is not a manufacturer of products with digital elements — and the temptation is to file this as a client matter and move on. Two things argue against that. The first is the nature of a 24-hour clock: it is a fact-finding deadline as much as a filing deadline, and it runs from awareness, not from the completion of forensics. A client that discovers an exploited vulnerability in a connected product on a Friday evening will call counsel inside the first few hours, needing to know what triggers notification, what an initial notification has to contain when almost nothing is yet known, and how what is said at hour four constrains what can be said at week four. That is a view a firm wants settled before the call rather than during it. The second is that parts of this sector are in scope directly: consultancies with product arms, and the legal-technology businesses that firms own, incubate or hold stakes in, place software on the EU market in their own right. Third-order, and quietly useful: products the firm buys will begin carrying CRA obligations, which gives procurement a concrete question to ask suppliers about disclosure timelines instead of a general one about security posture. ENISA has confirmed the CRA Single Reporting Platform went live on 11 September, routing reports automatically to national CSIRTs; obligations for open-source software stewards follow from 11 December 2027.
