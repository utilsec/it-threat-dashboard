Cisco ISE zero-day CVE-2026-76460 — a CVSS 10.0 unauthenticated root compromise of the system that decides which devices get onto the clinical network, exploited and KEV-listed

Cisco has confirmed active exploitation of CVE-2026-76460, a CVSS 10.0 flaw in Identity Services Engine and ISE Passive Identity Connector that Cisco describes as insufficient authentication control on an API endpoint. An unauthenticated remote attacker can bypass the web management interface and execute commands as root. Affected releases run from 3.0 through 3.5; fixes are in 3.1 Patch 12, 3.2 Patch 11, 3.3 Patch 12, 3.4 Patch 7 and 3.5 Patch 4, and there is no workaround. CISA added the flaw to the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog on 16 September 2026 with a three-day federal deadline of 19 September, which has now passed. Cisco has not named an actor or given a count of affected customers, but it warns that attackers may remove or hide evidence of exploitation, and its remediation guidance goes beyond patching: re-image affected nodes, restore from configuration backups, review access logs for suspicious usernames and cross-check network and firewall logs. This is the third actively exploited ISE flaw since June 2025, and CyberScoop notes it is Cisco’s second exploited zero-day in days, after the Secure Email Gateway flaw carried below. It leads this page because of what ISE does in a hospital. It is the network access control layer that authenticates clinicians on wired and wireless networks, profiles connected medical devices such as infusion pumps, patient monitors and imaging modalities, and assigns them to the segments that keep them apart from general IT. VulnCheck’s assessment is that root on ISE lets an attacker change network access policy, pull stored credentials, delete logs and move laterally — so the risk is not only a compromised appliance but a quietly rewritten segmentation model and a harvest of the credentials it holds.

Critical Breach

McKesson breach: about 6.4 million unique email addresses independently verified by Have I Been Pwned — the attacker’s 284 million figure counts rows, not people

McKesson, one of the largest pharmaceutical and medical-surgical distributors in the United States, has confirmed unauthorised access tied to third-party applications supporting its Oncology & Multispecialty and Medical-Surgical business units, with initial disclosure at the end of August 2026 and scoping continuing through September. Two numbers are in circulation and they are not equivalent. What is confirmed is that the extortion group ShinyHunters obtained data, that McKesson did not pay the more than $55 million demanded by its 1 September deadline, that the data was subsequently published, and that Have I Been Pwned’s independent analysis of the leaked dataset verified approximately 6.4 million unique email addresses, the closest independently verified proxy for the number of people affected — names, addresses, dates of birth, phone numbers, employer details and health information. What is claimed and unverified is ShinyHunters’ assertion of 284 million records — HIPAA Journal describes these as rows rather than unique patients; McKesson has not confirmed it, HIBP could not substantiate it from the published data, and HIBP likewise could not substantiate the group’s claim that Social Security numbers were included. This page reports 6.4 million as the confirmed figure and treats 284 million as an extortion claim, because that is what the evidence supports. HIPAA Journal’s consolidated timeline of 18 September puts detection on 25 August, exfiltration of roughly 1 TB between 21 and 25 August, the 8-K on 28 August and McKesson’s confirmation of exfiltration on 8 September; McKesson has still not given an individual count. Initial access is reported to have come through social engineering of employees against third-party applications rather than an exploited product flaw, and no CVE has been named. McKesson says there is no ongoing unauthorised activity on its network, that distribution centres and core services remain operational, and that it has engaged an external incident-response firm and is offering credit monitoring; affected-party notifications are still being scoped. For provider organisations the operational read is twofold. Supply is not the exposure here — distribution has continued — but the data is, and much of it belongs to people who never had a direct relationship with McKesson: patients, clinicians and practice staff whose details sat in a distributor’s commercial and specialty-pharmacy systems. Oncology is the sensitive part, because a record indicating that someone is an oncology patient is disclosing a diagnosis, which makes this raw material for highly credible targeted fraud against exactly the patients least able to absorb it.

LHC Group reports a voice-phishing breach that reached patient files on a third-party referral and care-coordination platform — 28,000+ confirmed so far, with the total expected to rise

LHC Group, the Lafayette, Louisiana-based provider of home health, hospice and home and community-based services in 28 states and Washington, DC, has begun notifying patients after an employee was voice-phished and the stolen credentials were used to reach files on a third-party vendor platform used for referral management and care coordination. The vendor has not been named. The company dates the unauthorised access to 7–15 April 2026 and says it began confirming identities on 9 July. State attorney-general filings account for more than 28,000 people so far; HIPAA Journal, which reported the breach on 15 September, expects the eventual total to be considerably higher, and no figure has yet appeared on the HHS OCR breach portal. The exposed data is clinical rather than just demographic: names, addresses, dates of birth, clinical summaries, treatment plans, diagnosis codes, dates of service, Medicare and Medicaid numbers and insurance information, with Social Security numbers and financial information in a limited number of cases. LHC is offering two years of credit monitoring. It is the company’s second vendor-related breach of 2026, after an earlier incident at Doctor Alliance. No group has claimed the attack and LHC has not attributed it; the voice-phishing entry resembles the help-desk pattern Health-ISAC warned about earlier this month, but that is a resemblance, not an attribution. The lesson for home health and post-acute providers is that referral and care-coordination portals hold some of the richest clinical detail an organisation shares, sit on a vendor’s infrastructure, and are often reached with a single staff credential.

Cedar County Memorial Hospital in El Dorado Springs, Missouri, disclosed a cyberattack on 23 August 2026, took systems offline, and says it returned to routine operations on 28 August. Critical Breach

AdaptHealth confirms 4.1 million people exposed after a contractor’s cloud account was talked open

AdaptHealth, one of the largest home medical equipment and supplies providers in the United States, has confirmed through SEC and HHS filings that 4,115,802 individuals had data exposed in a breach its filings date to 5 June 2026. The route in was not a vulnerability. According to the company, attackers used social engineering to compromise a third-party contractor’s privileged account in a cloud application, and from that single trusted account reached names, contact details, demographic information, health insurance information and health information. AdaptHealth attributes the intrusion to ShinyHunters, which notified the company directly; the company’s name was later removed from the group’s extortion site. AdaptHealth says it has no confirmed reports of identity theft or fraud arising from the incident to date and is offering credit monitoring to those notified. Read this next to the Health-ISAC alert further down the page and the pattern is the same one the sector keeps losing to: no exploit, no malware, a convincing phone call to somebody with elevated access in a SaaS or cloud console, and then bulk export of patient data from a system that was working exactly as designed. The contractor detail is the part most health systems under-model — the privileged account did not belong to AdaptHealth staff, and vendor and contractor identities frequently sit outside the conditional-access, session-length and privileged-access rules an organisation applies to its own workforce.
