Top Story

Heights Finance confirms breach of at least 1.2 million people via a third-party cloud platform

Consumer lender Heights Finance has confirmed a data breach affecting at least 1.2 million individuals after intruders reached a third-party cloud platform holding customer records in early May 2026. The exposed material is the full identity-theft set: names, Social Security numbers, driver’s licence and other government identification numbers, bank account details and dates of birth. The company reports that its loan management systems themselves were not touched — the loss sits with the outsourced platform, not the lending applications — which is precisely the pattern financial-services security teams keep meeting: the records leave through a vendor environment that sits outside the perimeter the institution instruments most heavily. Texas (734,828) and South Carolina (486,463) dominate the state-level counts, and no extortion crew has claimed the incident, so there is currently no leak-site listing to gauge what was taken or whether it will be published. For lenders and their downstream partners the practical work is twofold: confirm which third-party platforms hold copies of loan-origination and servicing records, and make sure contractual notification triggers and logging on those platforms are strong enough that an intrusion in early May would not take until August to surface publicly. Borrowers whose SSNs and bank account numbers are in scope face durable account-takeover and synthetic-identity risk that credit monitoring alone does not close.

BlackFile extortion crews compromise 24+ organisations by phone, with financial firms squarely in scope

Mandiant has published on a financially motivated cluster it tracks as UNC6671, operating publicly under four affiliate brands — Redact, Pink, Helix and Falcon — and collectively branded BlackFile. The group has compromised more than 24 organisations since January at a pace of roughly 1.5 new victims a day, and its initial access is almost entirely voice phishing: calls to staff and service desks that talk a target into approving access or resetting credentials, with no exploit required. Extortion demands open near $3 million. Flashpoint separately observed attacker infrastructure staged against Blackstone, Bain Capital, Moody’s, CME and Apollo — it is not confirmed that any of those firms were breached; staged infrastructure indicates preparation or targeting interest, not intrusion, and none of the named firms has disclosed an incident. The practical read for banks, insurers and asset managers is that the sector’s most active adversary right now walks in through the help desk, so identity-proofing procedure and call-handling discipline matter more this week than any single patch.

High Incident

Arrests across Europe and Brazil over a €30 million bank fraud that abused a service-provider weakness

European and Brazilian authorities have arrested and charged suspects over a scheme that diverted roughly €30 million from bank customer accounts by abusing a weakness introduced at a third-party banking service provider rather than by stealing customer credentials. The takedown spanned Germany and Brazil and involved coordinated law-enforcement action across both jurisdictions. The security lesson is uncomfortable and durable: the softest entry point into a bank is frequently its outsourced payment and transaction plumbing, where a defect in a provider’s change or update can open a fraud window that the bank’s own perimeter controls will never see. Detection in cases like this depends on transaction-anomaly monitoring that flags unusual direct-debit and transfer patterns quickly, not on network defences. The case also illustrates how long the tail is — the underlying fraud predates the arrests by a considerable margin, and cross-border evidence-gathering and asset seizure take years even when the facts are well documented.

High Incident

A run on hardware-wallet vendors: SafePal customer data now advertised for sale, days after Trezor

SafePal has disclosed a breach affecting 39,798 customers, and the stolen information is now being advertised for sale — a material escalation over an exposure that stays private. It lands three days after Trezor disclosed a breach touching roughly 14,000 customers. Neither vendor reports that seed phrases, private keys or customer funds were affected; what left in both cases is customer and order data. The pattern is the point: two hardware-wallet vendors compromised inside a week through the ordinary support, CRM and fulfilment tooling that surrounds the product rather than through the wallets or any blockchain infrastructure, which suggests a shared weakness across the vendor ecosystem serving this market. The consequence for named holders is unusually concrete. A list that pairs a real name and home address with proof of hardware-wallet ownership is direct input for both targeted phishing and physical coercion, and it does not expire the way a password does.

CLAIMED: 2 million-plus TaxAct records taken, with about 450,000 already published

An actor claims to have acquired more than 2 million user records from tax-filing service TaxAct and says roughly 450,000 of them have already been published. This is a claim, not a confirmed breach — the company has not verified the assertion and the total remains unvalidated, though the portion said to be already leaked is the part most easily checked by researchers. Financial-crime teams should not wait for confirmation to act on the downstream risk. Tax-filing data is among the highest-value inputs to fraud against banks: it links verified names, Social Security numbers, income figures, employer details and often the bank account and routing numbers used for refund deposit. That combination directly enables refund fraud, but more importantly for institutions it supplies the raw material for synthetic-identity creation and for passing knowledge-based identity checks at account opening. Expect any large tax-data exposure to show up weeks later as a rise in fraudulent applications rather than as an immediate incident.

Medium Incident

CLAIMED: ~200,000 customer records from Israel’s largest regulated crypto broker

An actor claims to hold roughly 200,000 customer records taken from Bits of Gold, Israel’s largest regulated cryptocurrency broker. The claim is unverified and has not been independently confirmed, so both the count and the contents should be treated as an assertion until the broker or a regulator says otherwise. If accurate, the sensitivity sits above the usual customer-list breach because a regulated broker’s records are KYC-heavy by design: they tie verified government identity documents to specific customers and, by implication, to wallet holdings. That pairing is reusable — identity documents submitted once can be replayed against onboarding at other exchanges and financial institutions — and it exposes named individuals whose asset holdings can be inferred, the same targeting risk the hardware-wallet breaches raise. Institutions relying on document-image verification alone at onboarding should assume leaked KYC packets circulate and pair document checks with liveness or issuer-side validation.

CISA/FBI refresh the Medusa advisory: 500+ critical-infrastructure victims, financial services among them

CISA and the FBI have updated their #StopRansomware advisory on Medusa (AA25-071A). The cumulative victim tally passed 500 critical-infrastructure organisations as of April 2026, up from roughly 300 in March 2025 — about 200 new victims in a year — with financial services named alongside healthcare, critical manufacturing, the defence industrial base, government facilities and IT. The finding that should change how banks schedule work is the tempo: the agencies assess that Medusa weaponises newly disclosed vulnerabilities within roughly 24 hours, and has been observed using exploits up to a week before public disclosure, because the operation buys exploits rather than developing them. That collapses the comfortable gap between a vendor advisory and real-world exploitation, and it means an internet-facing system left unpatched over a weekend is exposed for the whole window. For financial institutions running formal change control, the practical answer is a pre-approved emergency path for internet-facing systems that does not wait for the next change board.

High Intel

SpyNote + WindRelay turns bank customers into live NFC card relays during a fake support call

Group-IB research describes a fraud chain aimed at bank customers in Czechia, Slovakia and Slovenia that starts with a vishing call from someone posing as bank support staff, talks the victim into installing the SpyNote RAT via abused Android Accessibility Services, then silently side-loads a companion tool called WindRelay. When the victim taps their contactless card against their own phone, WindRelay streams the live chip exchange to an attacker-controlled payment terminal, letting the fraudster complete a transaction as the card while the victim still physically holds it, often paired with fraudulent digital loan origination for a larger cash-out. Group-IB identified 23 samples on VirusTotal active between November 2025 and July 2026 and more than 70 command-and-control servers. Because the scheme never uses screen-sharing, it evades the RAT detections banks’ fraud and mobile-security teams typically rely on, making it a live, evolving threat to contactless payment security rather than a one-off scam.

Active — live fraud campaign, Central/Eastern Europe Mobile banking fraud — no CVE
No software patch — behavioural/detection control Action: Brief fraud-operations and customer-support teams on the WindRelay pattern, review mobile-banking app tamper and accessibility-service detections, and warn customers that legitimate bank staff never ask them to install remote-support software or tap their card to their phone.

CLAIMED Entra directory theft is BEC and help-desk-impersonation fuel for the finance sector

An actor calling itself ‘TheHatman’ claims to have pulled employee directory data from Microsoft Entra tenants at a string of large enterprises using leaked credentials — roughly 3.6 million records in total, with McDonald’s, TCS, Vodafone, HCL, IHG, Kyndryl, Gap, Hexaware and Wyndham named. These are claims, and Hudson Rock links most of the named victims to prior infostealer infections rather than to any vulnerability in the platform itself, so this reads as credential reuse against tenants rather than a break in Entra. Financial institutions are not on the named list, but the material matters to them anyway. Directory extracts are org charts: names, titles, managers, email formats and internal reporting lines. That is precisely the input a caller needs to impersonate a specific employee to a service desk, or a specific executive to a payments team — the same tradecraft the BlackFile crews are running against the sector right now. Treat any confirmed directory extract as a social-engineering enablement event, not merely a privacy incident.

Four new CISA KEV entries with a 21 August deadline — two of them near-universal in bank estates

CISA added four actively exploited flaws to the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog on 18 August 2026, setting a 21 August remediation deadline for federal agencies and, in practice, a sensible target for regulated financial institutions too. They are: CVE-2026-33824, a double free in the Microsoft Windows IKE service extensions (IKEEXT) leading to remote code execution; CVE-2026-59310, the Broadcom VMware vCenter path traversal already being exploited at scale; CVE-2026-55040, a weak-authentication security-feature bypass in Microsoft SharePoint; and CVE-2026-65400, improper authentication in Apple macOS Screen Sharing. For a typical bank or insurer, the first two are the priority: IKEEXT is present across the Windows fleet and sits on the IPsec path used for site-to-site connectivity, and vCenter manages the virtualisation platform underneath most of the data centre. SharePoint carries the sector’s document and policy estate, which makes an authentication bypass there a confidentiality problem rather than an availability one.

Critical Advisory

VMware vCenter RCE under active exploitation — 361+ victim IPs, directly relevant to bank data centres

A critical directory-traversal flaw in the vCenter Server syslog service (CVE-2026-59310, CVSS 9.8) gives unauthenticated remote code execution against the platform that manages virtual machines, datastores and, in most environments, backups. Broadcom patched it 29 July 2026; a DFIR firm recorded first attacker contact just five days later and had counted 361 distinct victim IPs across 47 countries by 7 August, with Germany, the US, Turkey, Iran and France most affected. The observed chain uses the path traversal to plant a malicious cron job for persistence, then the open-source reverse_ssh tool for command-and-control; a suspected China-nexus operator is deploying a Babuk-derived ransomware variant that researchers believe may be a smokescreen for deeper, longer-term intrusion. Banks and insurers running vSphere-based private clouds or on-premises data centres — which is most of the sector — are directly in scope: compromise of vCenter can cascade into every guest VM it manages, making this one of the highest-impact enterprise flaws of the window for financial-services infrastructure teams. CISA added the flaw to its Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog on 18 August 2026 with a 21 August federal remediation deadline.

High Advisory

Windows Task Host privilege-escalation flaw now being used by ransomware crews

CISA has updated its KEV entry for CVE-2025-60710, a link-following privilege-escalation flaw in the Windows Task Host, to record that ransomware groups are now abusing it. The bug takes a low-privileged local user to SYSTEM on Windows 11 and Windows Server 2025 — the step that turns a single phished workstation or a compromised service account into domain-wide reach, which is exactly the pivot that separates a contained incident from an encrypted estate. Microsoft patched it in November 2025 and it entered KEV on 13 April 2026, so for most institutions this is not a new patch cycle but a hunt for stragglers: the machines that missed the November rollup, the servers held back by a change freeze, and the build images that were never refreshed. Given the vishing-led intrusions currently aimed at the sector, a reliable local-to-SYSTEM primitive on freshly built Windows Server 2025 hosts deserves a specific check rather than an assumption that the patch baseline is complete.

High Advisory

Joint CISA/FBI advisory names financial services among Gunra ransomware's targets

A joint CISA/FBI advisory (AA26-222A), published 10 August, details the Conti-derived Gunra ransomware-as-a-service operation and its double-extortion model, naming financial services alongside healthcare, government and other critical-infrastructure sectors among its targets. The group’s documented initial-access route is internet-facing VPN gateways and firewalls, including known Fortinet flaws CVE-2024-55591 and CVE-2025-24472 — both older vulnerabilities that remain exploitable wherever they are left unpatched. Investigators also recorded MFA bypass during those intrusions, tens of terabytes exfiltrated through OneDrive and SharePoint in at least one case, and activity concentrated roughly between 22:00 and 06:00 victim local time — outside the hours most bank security operations are fully staffed. For financial-services security teams the advisory is a direct prompt to check exposed VPN and firewall infrastructure against these two CVEs specifically, since Gunra’s targeting explicitly includes the sector rather than being a generic ransomware listing.

Medium Policy

Liechtenstein foundations and trusts registry breach exposes data central to bank AML due diligence

Attackers accessed Liechtenstein’s register of foundations and trusts and downloaded beneficial-owner names and personal details covering roughly 31,000 entities. The government says it will not pay a ransom. Liechtenstein foundations and trusts are commonly used structures in private banking and wealth-management relationships, and registries of this kind are exactly the records financial institutions rely on to satisfy anti-money-laundering and beneficial-ownership due-diligence obligations. A breach of the underlying registry data raises two distinct risks for banks: the exposed beneficial-ownership details could be used to build more convincing impersonation or business-email-compromise attacks against wealth-management clients, and institutions that rely on this registry as a source of truth for KYC/AML checks should treat its data as potentially less trustworthy until the government confirms the scope and integrity of what was accessed.

Medium Policy

French tax authority breach hits ~680,000 — feeding the identity-fraud pipeline banks’ AML programmes must catch

France’s DGFiP tax authority has confirmed a breach affecting roughly 680,000 people. Attackers used a compromised employee account together with a third-party account to exfiltrate reference tax income figures, withholding rates, company identifiers and cadastral property data between June and July 2026. Passwords are not believed to have been taken. Notably, the intrusion surfaced only when the actor boasted about it on a hacking forum — a reminder that dwell time in a document-rich government system can outlast the detection controls guarding it. DGFiP is not a financial institution, but this is exactly the material that helps an attacker pass a bank’s identity-verification and KYC checks: verified income, property holdings and business identifiers that back-stop a synthetic identity or make an account-takeover pretext convincing. Financial-crime and fraud teams should treat large government tax-data breaches as a leading indicator of subsequent account-opening and identity-fraud attempts rather than as a government IT story to note and move past.
