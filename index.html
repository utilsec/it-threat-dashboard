High Incident — Edge device compromise

Attackers are planting a Linux rootkit on F5 BIG-IP APM appliances that hides a web shell in memory and leaves nothing on disk

Reporting on 8 September describes intrusions in which F5 BIG-IP Access Policy Manager devices were compromised and fitted with a Linux rootkit that hooks the loading of PHP files and injects a fileless web shell straight into memory. Because no malicious file is written to the appliance’s filesystem, the usual checks — comparing binaries, scanning for dropped scripts, reviewing modified timestamps — come back clean while the attacker retains interactive access. The placement is what makes this expensive. BIG-IP APM is the authentication and remote-access front door for a large share of enterprise and government networks, so a device in this state sits between your users and everything they reach, with visibility of the sessions and credentials passing through it. Treat an affected appliance as a credential-exposure event rather than a malware cleanup, and assume anything that authenticated through it during the compromise window needs rotating.

High Incident — Credential theft campaign

A phishing-as-a-service platform called BigBear 2.0 bypassed MFA at 258 organisations and collected more than 5,000 Microsoft 365 logins

Researchers detailed a phishing-as-a-service operation, BigBear 2.0, that defeated multi-factor authentication at 258 organisations and harvested over 5,000 Microsoft 365 credentials. The technique is adversary-in-the-middle: the victim reaches a proxy that relays the real Microsoft sign-in page, so the login looks correct, the MFA prompt is genuine and the user approves it — and the operator captures the resulting session token rather than the password. A stolen token is a working session, which is why resetting a password after this kind of compromise often changes nothing. Sold as a service, the platform puts this capability in reach of operators with no development ability of their own, which is how the victim count reaches the hundreds. The defensive answer is not more user training but authentication that cannot be relayed and session controls that notice when a token turns up somewhere implausible.

Where that is not yet possible, tighten conditional access: require compliant or hybrid-joined devices for token issue, shorten session lifetimes for high-value roles, and alert on sign-ins from residential proxy ranges or on token use from a location the account has never used. MikroTik routers are being taken over through exposed SSH — CERT Polska names the chain MikroTrick and gives you a one-line indicator to check for

CERT Polska warned that MikroTik RouterOS vulnerabilities are being actively exploited against devices with SSH reachable from the internet, with attacks occurring since at least 2 September 2026. Six issues were disclosed; the chain being used, which the CERT calls MikroTrick, combines CVE-2026-67276, an SSH authentication bypass rated CVSS 9.2, with CVE-2026-86060, an SSH privilege escalation through a crafted username also rated 9.2, to give an unauthenticated attacker full control of the device. High Incident — Identity federation

Around 5,000 Dropbox accounts were taken over without a password — the flaw was in Lenovo ID, the identity provider on the other side of the federation

Dropbox said roughly 5,000 accounts were compromised through a flaw in Lenovo ID, the single sign-on identity provider used to access them. Reporting describes an authentication weakness that allowed account access without the password, over a window of about seventeen days, with accounts that had no multi-factor authentication enabled worst affected. Neither the storage platform nor the customer did anything wrong at the point of failure — the trust decision was delegated to an external identity provider, and that provider’s login flow was broken. This is the shape of federated-identity risk that rarely appears on a risk register: your access control is only as strong as the weakest identity provider you have agreed to trust, your logs show a successful federated login rather than an attack, and your ability to detect it depends on session and device anomalies rather than failed passwords. MFA on the relying party is what limited the damage here, which is the practical takeaway.

High Incident — Mass web compromise

More than 5,400 hacked websites are serving ClickFix lures whose payloads live in blockchain smart contracts — there is no takedown target

Netskope reported a campaign using more than 5,400 compromised websites, mostly WordPress and PrestaShop installations, to deliver ClickFix payloads. What makes it durable is where the next stage is kept: the injected scripts fetch their payload from smart contracts on the BNB Smart Chain testnet, a technique known as EtherHiding. Because the code sits on a blockchain, there is no hosting provider to notify and no domain to sinkhole, and the operators can change the payload at any time without touching the compromised sites. Netskope observed a peak of 536 newly infected sites in a single day in August 2026, with around 300 infected sites contacting its endpoints daily. The exposure runs both ways: your own marketing sites may be among the compromised, and your users are the ones being asked to run the command.

Medium Research — Phishing technique

Microsoft documented a phishing operation hiding invisible Unicode characters inside words to slip past keyword filters — peaking at 2.37 million messages a day

Microsoft threat researchers described a large-scale phishing campaign using ASCII smuggling: invisible characters from the Unicode Tags block, U+E0000 to U+E007F, inserted inside finance-related keywords so that a word such as “funding” is split by a character the recipient never sees but a keyword-matching filter does. The campaign peaked at about 2.37 million messages a day in late February 2026 and stayed active through mid-May, with the technique itself observed from 9 February to mid-May. The forward-looking part of Microsoft’s guidance is the more interesting one — the same normalisation should be applied before email content is fed to AI assistants, because invisible characters that fool a keyword list can equally carry instructions a model will read and a human reviewer will not.

A continuing resolution passed by the House and previously approved by the Senate extends the Cybersecurity Information Sharing Act of 2015 through the stopgap funding period into early December 2026. France’s CNIL fines a private hospital €500,000 — and half the penalty is about who was never told, not how the attacker got in

On 3 September 2026 the CNIL fined Hôpital Privé de la Loire €500,000 following a breach in which an attacker accessed the hospital’s computerised patient record system in summer 2025, exposing data on 524,867 patients and 202,246 designated trusted third parties. The regulator found violations of GDPR Article 32 on security and Article 34 on communicating a breach to data subjects. The security failings named are notable for how ordinary they are: authentication without VPN or multi-factor protection, access controls that let one user’s credentials reach every patient record, no real-time monitoring for suspicious activity, and consequently no detection of an extraction that ran over several days. The Article 34 finding is the one worth carrying into your own planning — the hospital notified affected patients but not the 202,246 trusted third parties whose data was also involved, which the CNIL held deprived those individuals of information they needed. Breach notification is routinely scoped to the obvious population, and secondary populations such as emergency contacts, guarantors, referees and beneficiaries are exactly the ones a hurried exercise leaves out.

Regulatory enforcement following a 2025 intrusion; no current exploitation activity Not applicable
Compliance and control-design matter, not a patchable issue Action: Two concrete tests. First, on access control: pick your most sensitive record system and check whether one ordinary user account can retrieve the entire population, and whether anything alerts when a single account reads records at bulk volume — that combination is what turned one compromised credential into half a million records here. Second, on notification: list every category of person whose data sits in that system, not just the primary data subjects, and confirm your incident-response plan can identify and contact the secondary categories. Identity-verification vendor IDScan is being sued over an alleged breach said to involve 153 million driver’s licence records

A lawsuit has been filed against identity-verification provider IDScan over an alleged data breach reported to affect around 153 million drivers, following dark-web advertising of a large collection of driver’s licence scans. Treat the figure and the breach itself as alleged: this is litigation reporting, the claims have not been tested, and the company’s position is not established in the reporting available at the time of writing. What makes it worth carrying regardless is the category. Age and identity verification is being pushed into more and more consumer and workplace flows by regulation and by platform policy, and each new requirement creates another vendor holding scanned government identity documents on behalf of businesses that never wanted to hold them directly. That data is the most durable a person has — a licence number and a scanned document cannot be rotated after a breach the way a password can — and the liability tends to follow the business that chose the vendor, not only the vendor.
