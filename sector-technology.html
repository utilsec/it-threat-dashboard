CISA added CVE-2026-102764 to its Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog on 16 October with a 19 October federal deadline, which has now passed. Remote-support appliances hold privileged reach into customers’ and employees’ machines, so MSPs and help desks that run one should treat this as an incident review, not only a patch.

Deleting the run or the branch does not undo the exposure.

CISA released “CISA 2030: Defend, Harden, Prevail” on 26 October. Acting Director Nick Andersen describes a move from passive defence to an anticipatory stance, and the plan says CISA will deprioritise threat-agnostic, all-hazards work in favour of risk-informed, consequence-based analysis. It names operational-technology security, third-party and supply-chain guidance, and support for a national risk register among its priorities. CISA will begin a review of its core services within 60 days, publish an implementation plan with metrics within 180 days and report progress after a year; the plan creates no new legal authorities. Analysts quoted by GovInfoSecurity question how much CISA can deliver after losing about a third of its staff. For technology vendors, the supply-chain guidance and the services review are the parts to watch, since the review could change which CISA services suppliers and their customers rely on (editorial assessment).

The UK will look at using the Cyber Security and Resilience Bill to cover AI-related risk to essential services, and extends frontier-AI pilots that found more than 1,000 potential government vulnerabilities

The UK government said on 12 October that it will extend pilots that used frontier AI to find and fix more than 1,000 potential vulnerabilities in government systems, work with the NCSC and the AI Security Institute on how to respond to major incidents involving autonomous AI agents, and examine how serious AI incidents and near misses can be identified and reported. It will also explore strengthening the Cyber Security and Resilience Bill, and the wider regime around it, so that essential services such as hospitals and utilities are better protected from AI-related risks, including loss of control. Most of this is exploratory and no specific amendments have been proposed; the bill’s Lords Report stage was scheduled for 26 October, and this page has not confirmed its outcome. For technology suppliers to UK essential services, the bill is the vehicle to watch for any future AI-incident reporting duty.

CISA’s final CIRCIA incident-reporting rule reaches White House review — a year after the statutory deadline, with the text still not public

The Office of Information and Regulatory Affairs logged receipt on 1 October of CISA’s final rule under the Cyber Incident Reporting for Critical Infrastructure Act (RIN 1670-AA04). The statute required the rule by October 2025, and CISA’s later targets of May and September 2026 slipped; the text has not been published and can still change in review, and HIPAA Journal reports that publication is expected before the end of the year. The Act sets a 72-hour deadline for reporting covered cyber incidents and 24 hours for ransom payments, but those duties start only on dates the final rule sets, and who is covered depends on its definitions. In a GAO report published on 28 September, industry participants singled out the coming CIRCIA rule and the SEC’s disclosure requirement as overlapping obligations. Information technology is one of the 16 critical-infrastructure sectors, so cloud, software and managed-service companies should expect to check whether they are in scope once the text is out.
