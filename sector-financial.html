Top Story

A second critical NetScaler flaw is now under attack — CVE-2026-19490 lets an unauthenticated attacker walk past gateway login entirely

Citrix disclosed CVE-2026-19490 on 20 August 2026 in bulletin CTX696939: an authentication bypass carrying a CVSS score of 9.3 that affects NetScaler ADC and NetScaler Gateway appliances configured as a Gateway — SSL VPN, ICA Proxy, CVPN or RDP Proxy — or as an AAA virtual server, with some builds requiring a SAML action to be configured and earlier builds affected regardless. On 3 September the threat-intelligence firm Previdian reported that its NetScaler sensors had recorded requests matching a publicly circulating proof-of-concept, initially from three distinct source addresses geolocated to Australia, the United States and Germany, growing to roughly ten attempts from six unique addresses. Belgium’s national cybersecurity centre issued a warning the following day. Fixed builds are 14.1-73.32 and later, 13.1-63.21 and later, 14.1-73.32 FIPS and later, and 13.1-37.277 and later for the FIPS and NDcPP lines; the same bulletin also covered CVE-2026-19489, a CVSS 8.8 memory overflow reachable through the SIP ALG on LSN groups. What makes this the item of the window for financial services is not the single bug but the stacking on one box. This is the second critical NetScaler defect under confirmed exploitation inside three weeks, after the CVE-2026-8452 memory-overflow flaw that CISA added to the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog on 26 August and that attackers are using to plant web shells. NetScaler Gateway is the remote-access and application-delivery front door across a very large share of bank, insurer, broker and asset-manager estates: it terminates staff VPN sessions, publishes internal applications to contractors and branches, and by design faces the internet. An authentication bypass there does not need a phishing lure, a stolen password or a second factor — it produces a session on the appliance that fronts the identity infrastructure. Note also what an auth bypass implies for incident response: unlike an exploit that leaves a dropped file, a bypassed login can look like a legitimate session in appliance logs, so patching alone does not tell you whether someone came through before you did. Treat any internet-facing NetScaler on an unfixed build as requiring both a patch and an assumption-of-access review.

High Breach

Trezor says a shipping vendor still held customer records it had been assured were deleted — 80,689 US customers now exposed

Hardware-wallet maker Trezor disclosed on 4 September 2026 that a breach at its former fulfilment provider ShipMonk exposed a further 67,000 US customers, taking the confirmed total to 80,689 once an earlier tranche of 13,689 is included. The exposed records cover orders placed between November 2019 and August 2021 and contain names, email addresses, phone numbers, shipping addresses and order numbers; some older entries hold only a name, city and email. Trezor stated that its own retention policy deletes or anonymises purchase data after ninety days, and that it had repeatedly requested and received written assurance from ShipMonk that the data had been deleted in line with its contract. The underlying compromise is attributed to the ShinyHunters extortion group, which reportedly reached ShipMonk’s systems through CVE-2026-72898, a critical SQL injection flaw in Metabase carrying a CVSS score of 10.0. ShipMonk has not publicly acknowledged the incident. Trezor is clear that its hardware wallets are not compromised; the risk is that a list of confirmed crypto-hardware owners with home addresses and phone numbers is an unusually well-qualified target set for phishing, vishing, impersonation and, given the addresses, physical coercion. For banks, insurers, exchanges and payment firms the transferable lesson has nothing to do with crypto. This is a fourth-party retention failure: a vendor kept data past a contractual deletion date, attested in writing that it had not, and the attesting firm carried the notification duty when the attestation turned out to be wrong. Written deletion assurances are not evidence of deletion, and DORA, the FCA regime and most US supervisory expectations put the burden on the regulated firm regardless of who actually held the file.

High Breach

The 153-million driver-licence listing has moved from allegation to litigation — IDScan is notifying business customers and class actions are filed

A dark-web marketplace calling itself Nexus appeared on 31 August 2026 offering searchable digital scans of more than 153 million US and Canadian driver licences, alongside over 10 million identification cards, more than 3 million travel documents and roughly 579,000 medical cards. Records were not single photographs: each licence came with several image files — front, back and infrared or ultraviolet captures — plus a capture timestamp, which is the output of an automated document-verification scanner rather than a consumer upload. Brian Krebs traced the likely origin to IDScan.net, a Louisiana identity-verification firm, by correlating timestamps in the records with the dates those individuals had their IDs scanned at businesses using IDScan technology. The story has moved on since. Around 1 September IDScan began notifying its business customers, and by 4 September multiple US law firms had filed or announced class actions in Louisiana alleging the company failed to protect data supplied by its clients, with the car-rental firm Hertz named among them. The FBI’s New Orleans field office has an open investigation, independently confirmed by Reuters, and the Nexus site went offline shortly after publication — though nothing suggests the data itself was recovered. IDScan.net has still not published a statement confirming a breach and it remains unclear how many individuals are affected. For banks, insurers and fintechs the exposure sits under the onboarding stack. Remote account opening, step-up verification and contact-centre re-identification lean on a photographed government ID, and the infrared and ultraviolet captures are exactly the security-feature images used to prove a document is genuine. A corpus of authentic multi-spectrum scans lets an attacker satisfy a document check without ever holding the document.

Live Stripe merchant secret keys are circulating in bulk — enough to list customers, create charges and redirect refunds

A dataset published on a criminal forum on 18 August 2026 by an actor using the handle ‘Satanic’ contained live-mode Stripe secret keys for hundreds of merchant accounts together with roughly 33–35 GB of customer and payment data: names, email addresses, phone numbers, postal addresses, partial card details such as brand, expiry and last four digits, IP addresses, transaction identifiers, invoices and active discount codes. Reporting puts the leak at 1,033 sk_live_ keys across about 669 merchant folders and 688,363 customer records. Separately, researchers documented more than 50,000 exposed Stripe keys sitting in public GitHub repositories, unmasked GitHub Actions build logs and misconfigured web servers, and demonstrated that a single exposed key could be turned into a customer list, an attacker-created payment link and a live test charge within 17 hours. Stripe itself was not breached — every key belongs to a merchant that leaked its own credential. What makes this a financial-sector item rather than a merchant one is what a live secret key permits: enumerate customers and stored payment methods, create charges, issue refunds to an attacker-controlled destination, rewrite webhook endpoints so the merchant never sees the events, and in some configurations reach connected accounts through Stripe Connect. Acquirers, payment facilitators, banking-as-a-service providers and any institution with merchants or embedded-payments partners inherit the chargeback exposure and the fraud pattern, not the merchant alone.

High Intel

BREEZE COMET goes after bank payment infrastructure directly — and pushes hundreds of fraudulent transfers within 48 hours of getting in

The Google Threat Intelligence Group and Mandiant published a detailed profile of BREEZE COMET (previously UNC5669, also tracked as Plump Spider), a financially motivated crew active since 2024 that has moved from opportunistic retail-banking fraud to direct intrusion into core financial plumbing. The targets are Brazilian banks, payment processors, fintechs and banking-software providers, and the objective is explicit: reach the National Financial System Network, obtain the mutual-TLS credentials used for Pix, STR and Boleto transfers, learn how the victim’s own transfer approval and anti-fraud checks work, then move money. Initial access is voice phishing that impersonates IT support over phone and WhatsApp, followed by installation of legitimate remote-management software such as AnyDesk; the group has also exploited vulnerable JBoss application servers, staged payloads on compromised Brazilian municipal domains, attempted to recruit insiders, and physically connected rogue devices to retail networks. Once inside it runs a purpose-built toolkit — REALBREEZE for LDAP brute-forcing, COBALTSPIN for reverse SOCKS5 tunnelling across segmented financial networks, LIGHTPAINT for SoftEther VPN persistence, MILDFROST for DNS-tunnelled backdoor access, KICKPLATE masquerading as Windows Update Health Tools, and BOATBEAM posing as an IIS HTTPS listener on 443. It greps CI/CD systems and source repositories for hard-coded payment credentials using terms such as boleto, cnab and remessa alongside instant-payment webhook patterns. Within 24 to 48 hours of establishing access the operators launch two waves of hundreds of fraudulent transactions, then clear event logs and delete their working directories. Google assesses the group is using large language models to generate reconnaissance scripts and automate deployment, and has observed infrastructure aimed at Nigeria, Paraguay, Ghana and Venezuela — so the model is being exported. For institutions outside Latin America the transferable point is target selection: the crew is not after customer credentials, it is after operator consoles, mTLS certificates and segmented payment networks behind the retail banking layer, and it reaches them through a help desk and an installer rather than an unpatched perimeter box.

High Phishing

A phishing kit aimed at banks and brokerages absorbed a Google and FBI takedown and produced 700 fresh pages

Group-IB published research on 3 September 2026 into the Outsider Phishing Kit, a phishing-as-a-service operation attributed to a threat actor tracked as ChenLun and sold through Telegram. Between December 2025 and May 2026 the researchers identified more than 100,000 phishing pages tied to the kit across over 10,000 unique domains, spanning at least 54 countries. The templates — 267 of them — impersonate financial services and brokerage firms alongside telecommunications providers, postal services, government agencies and toll systems, and the kit ships with adversary-in-the-middle capability, meaning it relays the victim’s session to the real site in order to capture the authenticated session rather than merely harvesting a password. The operation had a real economy behind it: over 5,000 Telegram subscribers and more than 230 identified buyers. It was disrupted — Google filed a civil suit on 12 June 2026 and the FBI ran Operation Ghost Hook from 13 June with Google and Lumen’s Black Lotus Labs, seizing core administrative servers, a Shopify storefront, thousands of domains and roughly $100,000 from payment wallets. The finding worth acting on is what happened next: affiliates generated more than 700 new phishing pages after the disruption, and activity continued into at least July 2026. For financial-services defenders there are two takeaways. First, brand-impersonation takedowns buy time rather than closure, so brand-protection metrics should track re-emergence rate, not domains seized. Second, an adversary-in-the-middle kit sold to 230 buyers means session-stealing phishing against your customers and staff is now commodity-priced; SMS and app-push one-time codes do not stop it, and the practical counter is phishing-resistant authentication bound to the origin plus token-binding and session-anomaly detection on the relying-party side.

Medium Intel

Over 5,400 hacked websites now serve ClickFix lures whose payload lives in a blockchain smart contract

Netskope reported on 5 September 2026 that it has identified more than 5,400 compromised websites feeding a long-running ClickFix campaign, with over 300 infected sites active on any given day. The distinguishing feature is where the malicious code is kept. Rather than hosting the payload on a server that can be seized or blocked, the injected scripts pull it from smart contracts on the BNB Smart Chain testnet — the technique known as EtherHiding — which lets the operators rewrite the payload at will and gives defenders no domain or IP to sinkhole. Netskope recorded nearly 400 sites contacting BSC endpoints daily through August, peaking at 536. The compromised sites are predominantly small-business properties on WordPress and PrestaShop, and the initial compromise method is still unknown. Victims are shown a fake CAPTCHA that instructs them to paste a command into the Windows Run dialog; a newer variant then establishes a WebRTC data channel for command and control, hand-writing its own answer rather than performing a genuine handshake, so the covert channel produces few of the network indicators a proxy would normally catch. The financial-services relevance is on the endpoint rather than the perimeter. ClickFix succeeds because a person voluntarily runs the attacker’s command, which means it lands on managed laptops in the branch, the contact centre and the back office regardless of how well the gateway is patched, and web-category blocking does not help when the delivery vehicle is a legitimate small-business site an employee has reason to visit. Blockchain-hosted payloads also blunt the two controls most firms lean on here — domain reputation and payload hash blocking.

Critical Advisory

An unpatched Magento and Adobe Commerce zero-day is being used to backdoor live stores — and there is no fix yet

Sansec published research on 5 September 2026 on a zero-day it has named StyleSmuggler: an unauthenticated remote code execution flaw in Magento Open Source and Adobe Commerce that is already being exploited in the wild to install backdoors on live stores.
