Brevo’s own CDN edge served ClickFix lures and a WordPress backdoor to sites embedding its widgets — and the key that made it possible was hardcoded in source code

Brevo, the email-marketing and customer-messaging SaaS formerly known as Sendinblue, has published a post-mortem of a two-stage compromise. On 10 September attackers abused an improperly scoped SAML single sign-on path — creating their own account, enabling SSO and inviting legitimate users into an identity-provider configuration they controlled — to reach 138 customer accounts; contacts were exported from 43 and six were used to send phishing. Brevo closed that entry point within two hours and signed every user out. The second stage was worse. On 14 September, between about 16:07 and 20:30 UTC, the attackers used a long-lived Cloudflare API key with full account permissions, hardcoded in Brevo’s application source code, to deploy a Cloudflare Worker that rewrote responses at the CDN edge for brevo.com, sendinblue.com, sibforms.com, the login and account portals, and the forms, Conversations chat widget and SDK loader scripts that customers embed on their own sites. The Worker also stripped Content-Security-Policy headers. Ordinary visitors were shown a fake “Cloudflare, verify you are human” ClickFix page; visitors holding a live WordPress administrator session had the script try to install a malicious plugin called “Web Media Optimizer” as a persistent backdoor. Sansec, which first reported the attack on 16 September, estimates more than 100,000 websites were exposed. Brevo says the key was first misused in late August, that it found no malicious injection into customer-facing pages before 14 September, and that its origin servers were never modified — which is exactly why integrity checks did not fire. It leads this edition because it is the supply-chain pattern every SaaS company both consumes and operates: a third-party script on your pages is your code at the moment it runs, and one over-privileged, long-lived infrastructure credential in a repository was enough to turn a vendor’s own delivery network against its customers’ visitors.

Helpfeel, the Japanese company that operates the Gyazo screenshot and image-sharing service, disclosed on 16 September that an attacker exploited a vulnerability in Gyazo’s image upload server on 11 September and was detected and removed on 12 September. Stolen session IDs and third-party integration tokens are live credentials, not hashes waiting to be cracked.

CrowdSec’s private repositories leaked four months after a departing developer’s machine caught Shai-Hulud from the TanStack npm compromise

CrowdSec disclosed on 16 September that an archive of more than 130 of its private repositories — console and SaaS code, data-science models, its “Consensus Algorithm” and deployment scripts — had been posted on an online forum, together with 83 user email addresses, about 0.05 per cent of its roughly 150,000 users, and the names and emails of 51 potential investors from 2020. The root cause runs back to 11 May 2026, when the TeamPCP compromise of the TanStack npm packages (CVE-2026-45321; 84 malicious versions across 42 packages) shipped the “Shai-Hulud” credential-harvesting malware. A departing CrowdSec developer’s machine was infected, exposing a GitHub OAuth token, and on 22 May about 170 private repositories were downloaded in a nine-minute window. Access was removed on 25 May; an attempted misuse of an exposed AWS SNS credential followed on 17 August. CrowdSec says no client data was exposed, no infrastructure or databases were compromised and no production code was altered; credentials were rotated on 16 and 17 September and GitHub helped revoke the token. The lesson for any software company is the timeline: a package-registry compromise in May became a public source-code leak in September, and the token that bridged them belonged to someone who was on the way out. Offboarding that closes the corporate account but not the OAuth grants and personal access tokens a developer created leaves exactly this gap.

A browser extension with about 31,000 installs quietly forwarded OAuth tokens to a Russian proxy — the enterprise exposure is not Twitch, it is the extension model

Researchers reported on 14 September that an extension distributed across browser stores as “Twitch Enhanced Viewer” and “JeetBot”, with roughly 30,000 to 31,000 installs, harvested users’ session and OAuth tokens and exfiltrated them to proxy infrastructure belonging to a Russian commercial bot service. The direct victim population is consumer, and no enterprise breach has been tied to it. It earns a place here because of the mechanism rather than the brand: a browser extension runs inside the authenticated session of every site the user visits, and an extension installed on a corporate or bring-your-own device can read or replay tokens for the SaaS applications the same browser is signed into. Token theft is the quiet form of account takeover, because a stolen session cookie or OAuth token presents as a legitimate, already-authenticated user and does not trigger a second-factor prompt. Most organisations run no extension policy at all, which means the control surface for this class of risk is currently the individual employee’s judgement in a store listing.

The ScreenConnect flaw now has a CVE, a patch and a three-day federal deadline — and the exploitation pattern is worm-like, spreading from support sessions into the estates behind them

What this page carried last week as an unpatched ConnectWise disclosure has resolved into CVE-2026-84869, a CVSS 9.9 missing-authorization and improper-privilege-management flaw in ScreenConnect. ConnectWise has confirmed exploitation, which researchers trace back to 20 August, and CISA added the CVE to the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog on 11 September under Binding Operational Directive 26-04, giving federal civilian agencies three days to remediate. The mechanics are what earn the “worm-like” description: attackers abuse legitimate remote-support sessions to transfer and execute files without host confirmation, then use social engineering to get victims to run modified ScreenConnect clients that detect other active sessions and push VBScript payloads to the machines connected to them. Version 26.6.5 contains the fix; disabling the TransferFiles permission is the interim mitigation. For a technology or managed-service organisation the exposure runs in both directions, and that has not changed: an affected instance reaches into customer environments that never installed the product, and if you are the customer, your provider’s patch timeline is your patch timeline.
