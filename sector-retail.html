Top Story

CISA adds Adobe Commerce and Magento flaw CVE-2026-71362 to KEV — an unauthenticated attacker can switch a shopper’s session into another customer’s account; fixed in August, federal deadline 27 September

CISA added CVE-2026-71362 to its Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog on 24 September with a federal remediation deadline of 27 September. The flaw is an incorrect-authorisation bug (CWE-863) in Adobe Commerce and Magento Open Source, rated CVSS 9.1 by Adobe and exploitable without authentication. Adobe fixed it on 11 August in bulletin APSB26-92 and describes the impact as privilege escalation; Sansec, which tracks Magento attacks, calls it an unauthenticated customer account takeover. As reported by The Hacker News, an attacker can switch a customer session to another customer’s account and read that customer’s data. Affected releases are Adobe Commerce 2.4.4 to 2.4.9 (July 2026 and earlier), Adobe Commerce B2B 1.3.3 to 1.5.3 and Magento Open Source 2.4.6 to 2.4.9; the August security releases fix it. On exploitation, Sansec says it detected and blocked attempts in August, and Previdian reported honeypot hits from an Australian IP address on 10 September. Adobe’s bulletin said at release that it knew of no exploits. No count of compromised stores or affected shoppers has been published. This is a separate flaw from StyleSmuggler (CVE-2026-75650), the template-injection bug exploited from 4 September and still carried in Critical Advisories below, so a store patched for StyleSmuggler may still be exposed here. It leads this edition because it is agency-confirmed exploitation in one of the most widely deployed retail commerce platforms. For a merchant the harm is account takeover without a password: saved addresses, order history, stored contact details and, where enabled, store credit, loyalty balances or B2B purchasing rights. A store two releases behind, a staging copy or an agency-hosted microsite is enough to be in scope. Confirm that every Adobe Commerce and Magento instance is on the August release or later, then review customer-account activity since early August for sessions that jumped between accounts, and for email, address or password changes nobody asked for.

Critical Campaign

Gambit’s AI-agent skimming campaign: 27 organisations breached, skimmers confirmed on 19 of their sites and more than 600,000 card records taken from two of them — still one vendor’s figures, with no victim named

Gambit, an AI-security company, has published an investigation, reported by BleepingComputer on 23 September and The Register on 25 September, into a financially motivated operator who used three open-source AI-agent frameworks to find, break into and skim e-commerce and booking sites. According to Gambit, Strix handled vulnerability scanning, Cairn autonomous exploitation and Hermes orchestration, with the human operator giving the agents short goal instructions; the reporting describes the attacker’s own use of the frameworks and does not describe any compromise of the model provider. The Register’s account, citing Gambit’s threat-intelligence director Eyal Sela, puts 27 companies breached “to varying degrees”, skimmers ordered against all 27 and confirmed present on 19 of their websites, and more than 600,000 card records “from just two victim companies”. Separately, more than 100 other infected sites have been identified, for at least 119 in all. The operator’s spend was roughly $12,000–18,000. Gambit also says the agents ran deletion or cleanup routines that could disrupt a victim’s operations, “and this has indeed happened in some of the breaches”, which turns a skimming incident into a data-loss one. Victims are described only by type: a Fortune 500 hospitality company, a major US airline, a private US industrial-supplies distributor and a US online fashion retailer. How the sites were first breached is not stated and no CVE is named. It led the 24 September edition and moves down because the figures still come from a single vendor, with no company, regulator or card brand confirming any of them, while the new lead is agency-confirmed exploitation. What stays true either way is the economics: at about $25 a scan, small and mid-sized storefronts that were never worth a human operator’s time are now in range.

BigCommerce’s Ribon app compromise: the stolen key was used from 13 to 17 September and Master of Malt says customer records were pulled “page by page” until it was revoked — no card data reported

BigCommerce, the hosted e-commerce platform, has told merchants that attackers obtained an access key belonging to Ribon and Ribon 1.5, third-party shopping-experience apps from its marketplace owned by Be A Part Of, a Fastr company, and used it to inject malicious scripts into “a small number of merchant storefronts”. SecurityWeek, reporting on 22 September, sets out the timeline: the credentials were misused from 13 to 17 September, revoked on 17 September, and merchants were notified on 18 September. The data taken was names, email addresses, phone numbers and addresses; BigCommerce says passwords and payment-card data are held separately, and no card data has been reported taken. BigCommerce says its own platform was not breached. The UK spirits retailer Master of Malt, the first store to confirm it was affected, says that once the attackers had the Ribon key “they used it to access data held inside BigCommerce”, downloading customer records one page at a time until the key was disabled, a day after the developer detected the misuse. The app was installed on hundreds of storefronts, so BigCommerce’s “small number” is the figure to watch, and no count has been published. Neither Be A Part Of nor Fastr had commented at publication. In its notice to customers, reported by The Register on 23 September, Master of Malt’s founder Justin Petszaft wrote that “the attackers had access to your name, email address, phone number, and address” and asked customers to “be extra vigilant against potential phone calls, spam and phishing attacks”; the retailer says BigCommerce’s security team uninstalled the Ribon app the day the attack was discovered, that there is no ongoing compromise, and that payment data and passwords are held in a separate system that was not affected. No customer count was given, and no other merchant has been named. For shoppers the risk is delivery and refund scams that use a real name, address and recent order, and they will contact the retailer, not the app developer.

A stolen Cloudflare key let attackers rewrite Brevo’s embedded marketing scripts for more than four hours — shoppers were shown ClickFix lures and logged-in WordPress admins were served a backdoor plugin

Brevo, the email-marketing and CRM platform formerly called Sendinblue, has confirmed a supply-chain compromise that turned its own embedded scripts against the sites carrying them. Attackers obtained a long-lived Cloudflare API key with full account permissions that had been hardcoded in Brevo’s application source, and used it to deploy a malicious Cloudflare Worker that rewrote responses at the CDN edge on 14 September, from about 16:07 to 20:30 UTC. The Worker altered the SDK loader and the Conversations chat-widget script that customers embed, plus Brevo-hosted pages including sibforms.com form hosting, and stripped Content-Security-Policy headers on the way through; Brevo says its origin servers and files were never modified, which is why standard integrity checks missed it. Sansec, which found the attack, estimates more than 100,000 sites were exposed. Ordinary visitors were shown a fake “Cloudflare, verify you are human” ClickFix page telling them to paste and run a command; visitors with a live WordPress admin session had the script try to install a plugin called “Web Media Optimizer” as a persistent backdoor. It follows a linked 10 September incident in which a mis-scoped SAML sign-on path exposed 138 Brevo customer accounts. It moved down from the lead on 22 September because nothing had changed: Sansec’s write-up was last revised on 17 September, no count of backdoored sites or infected visitors has been published, and no regulator action or further Brevo statement surfaced between 18 and 22 September. For merchants, a newsletter pop-up or chat widget loaded on every page, checkout included, has the same power as your own code: check WordPress sites for plugins installed on 14 September and rotate admin passwords, and treat any PC where someone ran a “verify you are human” command as infected.

Admin Menu Editor Pro’s vendor counts about 230 customers — at least 1,500 sites — hit by its backdoored update and up to 300 more possibly affected; a clean 2.37 shipped on 19 September

The developer of Admin Menu Editor Pro, a premium WordPress plugin, has published a fuller account of the 14 September supply-chain attack in which its own update channel shipped a backdoor. Versions 2.35 and 2.36 released that day were malicious, 2.36 especially after about 19:00 UTC; the earliest sign of compromise was around 19:40 UTC on 13 September, and 2.34 is clean. The vendor now counts about 230 customers affected in the initial compromise and about 300 more potentially affected by download timing. Those are customer licences rather than sites; the separate figure of at least 1,500 compromised sites, reported by BleepingComputer, is attributed to the developer’s own update-server log analysis, and the two are compatible because customers run multiple sites. The attacker may have had root-level access to the distribution server, with a kernel privilege-escalation flaw suspected; adminmenueditor.com has been taken offline and is being rebuilt on new infrastructure (it still showed a temporarily-offline notice on 22 September), and a clean 2.37, built from 2.34, was released on 19 September through a temporary licence-key download. For merchants the lesson is the delivery path: automatic updates were the infection route, and a hidden administrator on a storefront can alter checkout pages and read order data.

IDScan.net confirms the breach behind 153 million driver’s licence scans sold on a dark-web market — the ID-check terminals on retail, hotel and rental counters — and Canada’s privacy commissioner has now opened an investigation

On 1 September Brian Krebs reported that an identity-theft service called Nexus, advertised on the Russian-language crime forum Exploit, was offering more than 153 million driver’s licence images covering the United States and Canada, plus over 10 million identification cards, roughly 3 million travel documents and about 579,000 medical cards. Investigators traced the source to IDScan.net, a Louisiana identity-verification vendor that says it handles more than 21 million verifications a month across upwards of 20,000 locations and counts Target, Hertz, FedEx and Caesars Entertainment among its customers. The provenance evidence is what makes this a retail story rather than a generic data-broker leak: Krebs found his own and a family member’s licences with timestamps matching Hertz rental transactions, and researcher Zach Edwards found his after presenting ID at a Las Vegas cannabis dispensary and at his hotel. The operators claimed to have been pulling fresh records continuously for more than a year, and roughly 400,000 new licences appeared within a day of the listing going live. The FBI’s New Orleans office opened an inquiry; Nexus went offline within hours of publication, though buyers who already pulled the data still hold it. SecurityWeek carried the account on 3 September and lawsuits followed within days. Update, 10 September: IDScan.net has now confirmed unauthorised access to its cloud platform, discovered around 1 September and disclosed on 4 September. BleepingComputer reports the exposed material as more than 153 million driver’s licence scans plus roughly 10 million identification cards, 3 million travel documents and about 579,000 medical cards, and the FBI is investigating. The allegation is therefore no longer an allegation, although the number of individuals affected has still not been established. Update, 22 September: the Privacy Commissioner of Canada, Philippe Dufresne, has opened an investigation into IDScan’s security practices and into whether its notifications to affected people were adequate under PIPEDA, Canada’s federal private-sector privacy law. The number of Canadians affected has not been disclosed, and IDScan did not respond to The Record’s request for comment. For any merchant that scans identity documents — age-restricted sales, returns-fraud controls, rental and check-in desks — the lesson is that the scanner vendor, not the store, became the aggregation point, and the customer relationship is still the retailer’s.

For retailers the lesson is the account type rather than the tool: shared mailboxes, store and warehouse kiosks, reporting and integration accounts are often exempted from MFA, never rotated and never reviewed, and a spray only needs one of them.

Microsoft disrupts EvilTokens, a device-code phishing service that compromised more than 12,000 Microsoft accounts at over 10,000 organisations — wholesale is among the sectors named, and two arrests followed in the UK

Microsoft’s Digital Crimes Unit, working with Health-ISAC, law enforcement and SpyCloud, took down the infrastructure of EvilTokens, a phishing-as-a-service platform Microsoft tracks as run by Storm-2992, on 22 September. The service abused the OAuth device-code sign-in flow: the victim is persuaded to enter a code on a genuine Microsoft page, and the attacker’s session receives tokens even where multi-factor authentication is enforced. Microsoft puts the toll at more than 12,000 Microsoft accounts at more than 10,000 organisations in 79 countries; SpyCloud separately counts 8,708 accounts across 6,585 domains. It had been sold for about $500 a month since February and used AI features to write lures and triage stolen mailboxes. Sectors named include wholesale, construction, financial services, real estate, higher education and healthcare. The Metropolitan Police arrested two men, aged 32 and 38, who have been released on bail. A takedown removes one service, not the technique, and copycat services are already appearing in the reporting. For retailers the exposure is the buying, finance and store-operations mailboxes that talk to suppliers every day: a device-code prompt looks like a normal Microsoft sign-in, and a captured mailbox is a ready-made platform for invoice and payment-redirection fraud.

Fake “LastPass Authenticator” GitHub pages push a Microsoft-signed kernel driver that kills 145 security products, then the Rapuncel infostealer

LastPass’s threat-intelligence team and Delphos Labs published on 17 September an analysis of a campaign that uses search-engine-ranked fraudulent GitHub organisations and GitHub Pages sites — one named LastPass-Authenticator, with at least 40 software brands impersonated in total — to redirect visitors to attacker servers that serve padded ZIP installers of 127 to 148 MB. The installer loads a kernel driver, Alinubx.sys, which is a renamed copy of a driver from a Chinese disk-encryption product, signed through the Windows Hardware Compatibility Publisher chain in March 2023 and presented as an NVIDIA file-system filter driver under the service name NvFsFilter. It carries a list of 145 antivirus and EDR process names and terminates them from kernel mode; the Rapuncel stealer then collects passwords from more than 20 browsers, cryptocurrency wallets, Discord, Steam and Telegram sessions, Windows Credential Manager and any file whose name suggests a password, seed phrase or recovery code. LastPass says the signature had not been revoked and the driver was not on Microsoft’s vulnerable-driver blocklist at the time of writing; BleepingComputer and The Hacker News carried the findings on 19 and 21 September. No victim count has been published. The retail relevance is the head-office user who searches for an authenticator or a utility and installs the first result. A stealer that first disables the endpoint agent and then takes saved credentials for the merchandising portal, the payment-provider console and the advertising platform is initial access to the systems on the rest of this page, and because the driver is validly signed, allow-listing by signature does not stop it.

The EU Cyber Resilience Act’s 24-hour reporting duty is now live — and it captures retailers who put their own brand on connected products

The incident-reporting provisions of the EU Cyber Resilience Act took effect in the week of 11 September. Manufacturers of products with digital elements placed on the EU market must now notify actively exploited vulnerabilities in their products, and serious security incidents affecting them, within a 24-hour window. The obligation is worth reading before assuming it belongs to someone else. The Act attaches to whoever places the product on the market under their own name or trade mark, which means a retailer selling own-brand connected devices — smart home hardware, connected appliances, wearables, anything with firmware and a companion app — carries manufacturer duties for those products even though the design and the build came from a contract manufacturer elsewhere. The same logic reaches own-brand in-store hardware where a retailer, rather than the original producer, is the entity named on the box. A 24-hour clock is short enough that it cannot be met by a process invented after the first report is due: it requires a named decision-maker, a route to the relevant authority, and a contractual path by which your manufacturing partner tells you about an exploited flaw fast enough for you to tell the regulator. Retailers who are purely distributors of third-party brands have lighter obligations, but the distinction depends on whose name is on the product, not on who wrote the firmware.
