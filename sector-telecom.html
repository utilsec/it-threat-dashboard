Arista confirms attackers are exploiting a CVSS 10.0 flaw in on-premises VeloCloud Orchestrator — the SD-WAN control plane that operators and managed-service providers run for their business customers

Arista published Security Advisory 0183 on 22 September 2026 for CVE-2026-93952, an improper-input-validation flaw (CWE-20) in on-premises VeloCloud Orchestrator (VCO), rated CVSS 10.0 under v3.1 and 9.5 under v4.0. Arista says the issue “may allow a remote attacker to access privileged internal functionality and impact the VCO host”, that successful exploitation may compromise the confidentiality, integrity and availability of the orchestrator and the data it manages, and that it “is known to be actively exploited”. CISA added it to the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog the same day, alongside the F5 and two Check Point flaws covered below, with a federal deadline of 25 September. The exposure has preconditions, and they matter: VCO is exposed only where certificate-based authentication from VeloCloud Edges to the orchestrator is configured, and the attacker needs the public portion of an Edge authentication certificate and network access to the VCO web interface — but no VCO tenant or operator credentials. Affected on-premises releases are 5.2.3.15 and earlier, 6.1.3.7 and earlier, 6.4.2.7 and earlier, and 7.0.0.2 and earlier. Arista lists fixes for only two trains so far, 5.2.3.16 and later and 6.4.2.8 and later, so operators on 6.1.x and 7.0.x currently have mitigations rather than a patch. Hosted and Dedicated VCO instances were also affected, and Arista says they have already been patched. No actor, victim or exposure count has been published. The telecom weight is in who runs this. VeloCloud is an SD-WAN platform that service providers sell and operate as a managed service, and an operator-run on-premises orchestrator is a multi-tenant control plane: it holds the configuration for every customer Edge beneath it and pushes changes to branch sites across all of them. A compromise there is a problem for the operator and for every tenant it manages, and the tenant usually has no way to inspect the box. Arista’s indicators are specific enough to hunt on now: the files /usr/local/sbin/.vcnode.js, /usr/local/sbin/vc-sysmond (md5 dc78e206eaeadec59fc5801fe4556bd0) and /etc/systemd/system/vc-sysmon.service; an x-vc-opt HTTP header in nginx logs; and connections from 142.93.149[.]77 and 104.248.126[.]159. Upgrade where a fix exists. Everywhere, restrict the VCO web interface to trusted administrative networks, block unnecessary outbound ports from the VCO host and watch its egress, check for the backdoor daemon and web shells, and review administrator activity and Edge configuration pushes for changes nobody made. Because those files are a persistence mechanism, patching alone does not close an intrusion: if any indicator is present, treat the orchestrator as compromised and plan how you will tell the tenants it manages.

Telus tells customers that attackers were inside subscriber accounts for sixteen months — and the way in was credentials, not a vulnerability

Telus customer notifications, reported by SecurityWeek on 14 September 2026, say that compromised credentials were used to access consumer accounts between February 2025 and June 2026, and that the data obtained was then used to lure customers into switching providers and to make unauthorised service changes. The exposed fields named in the notices are names, account numbers, phone numbers, billing and email addresses, partial card numbers, and subscription and payment history. Two things Telus has not said matter as much as what it has: the number of affected customers is not disclosed, and neither is the origin of the credentials — whether they came from an internal breach or were harvested elsewhere and replayed. No software flaw has been named, and none is implied. What is confirmed is the shape of the intrusion and its duration. Sixteen months of account access that fed switching lures and unauthorised service changes is a detection failure on the customer-facing estate rather than on the network, and that estate is the one part of a carrier that is deliberately exposed to the whole internet and defended mostly by a password. The operational point for other operators is that the self-service portal is the control plane for the phone number itself: an attacker holding a subscriber session can attempt a port, change the contact address a confirmation is sent to, and alter a plan, which is the same sequence that precedes SIM-swap and account-takeover fraud downstream. Telus has forced credential resets on affected accounts, increased account monitoring, notified Vancouver Police and is offering identity-theft protection. The controls that generalise are credential-stuffing defences on the login path, mandatory multi-factor authentication on self-service rather than optional, a port-out lock or PIN that is not derivable from the account record, and alerting on the sequence of contact-detail change followed by a port or plan change — because it is the sequence, not any single request, that is the signal.

Altice Portugal’s MEO told the Lusa news agency on 15 September 2026 that it had been hit by “a mass attack that caused congestion and degradation of the international network”. The operational lesson sits on the transit side. The arrangements that decide how quickly an operator recovers from this kind of event are the ones agreed with transit providers in advance.

Researchers say an intruder got into Thai broadband provider 3BB through an old FortiGate flaw, then ran MeshCentral as a hidden backdoor aimed at subscriber credentials

Hunt.io researchers, in analysis reported by The Hacker News on 14 September and SecurityWeek on 15 September 2026, say an attacker’s exposed file directory documents a compromise of 3BB, a Thai broadband provider. According to that analysis, initial access came through FortiGate SSL-VPN flaw CVE-2024-21762 — an old, KEV-listed bug — with exploits for older F5 BIG-IP flaws and Linux privilege-escalation bugs also in the toolkit. The intruder reached root, configured MeshCentral — a legitimate open-source remote-management platform — as a covert backdoor under a device group named “TH-3BB”, and targeted RADIUS subscriber credential databases; a cleanup script left the MeshCentral agent in place, and the activity was discovered around 3 June 2026. Treat this as researcher-reported: 3BB has not commented, no regulator statement has been reported, and subscriber impact has not been quantified. Two lessons hold regardless. The way in was an unpatched edge VPN appliance carrying a long-known, KEV-listed flaw. And the persistence needed no exploit at all: the tool is the implant. That second detail is worth carrying into your own environment, because a remote-management server is exactly the kind of asset that survives a hunt. It is signed, it is expected to talk to large numbers of hosts on odd ports, its agent runs as a service with administrative rights, and an unauthorised instance looks identical in process and network telemetry to an authorised one. Carrier and ISP estates are unusually exposed to this pattern because they already run several overlapping remote-management and provisioning systems — field-technician tooling, CPE and ACS management, NOC jump infrastructure, managed-service platforms for business customers — often inherited through acquisitions and rarely inventoried end to end. The control is an authoritative list of which remote-management platforms are sanctioned, which servers are authorised to run them, and which accounts are permitted to enrol agents, followed by a sweep for instances that appear on none of those lists.

CVE-2026-19490 is a critical authentication bypass in Citrix NetScaler ADC and Gateway, disclosed in a Citrix advisory on 19 August 2026. Attackers are planting a Linux rootkit on F5 BIG-IP APM appliances that leaves no malicious file on disk

Reporting on 8 September 2026 describes intrusions in which attackers deploy a Linux rootkit on F5 BIG-IP Access Policy Manager devices. The rootkit hooks the loading of PHP files and injects a web shell directly into memory, so nothing malicious is written to the filesystem — a file-integrity check of the appliance comes back clean while the attacker retains interactive access. BIG-IP APM sits at the remote-access edge of a great many enterprise, carrier and government networks, terminating VPN and application-access sessions and holding the authentication decisions for the users behind them. That position is the whole point of the target selection: an implant there sees credentials and session traffic in the clear, and it survives the checks most teams would run. For carriers and service providers there is a second dimension. Appliances of this class are frequently operated on behalf of enterprise customers, so a compromise is a multi-tenant problem rather than a single-device one, and the tenant is unlikely to be able to inspect the box themselves. The detection approach has to shift accordingly: on-box antivirus and file hashing will not find a fileless implant, so look outward at what the appliance does — unexpected outbound connections, administrative sessions from unfamiliar sources, and changes in process behaviour — and treat vendor guidance on integrity verification as the authoritative check. A separate, newly exploited APM flaw, CVE-2026-94127, is covered under Critical Advisories; no public reporting links it to this campaign.

Red Heron scanned 1,386 self-hosted Gitea servers and got into 11 organisations — telecom among the sectors named

Acronis’s Threat Research Unit disclosed on 13 September 2026, with wider coverage on 14 September, that a group tracked as Red Heron, assessed as China-linked at moderate confidence, automated a public proof-of-concept for CVE-2026-60004 — a critical remote code execution flaw in the self-hosted Gitea code platform, disclosed in July 2026 and fixed in Gitea 1.27.1 — into a Python framework that registers accounts, exploits unpatched servers, steals repositories and cleans up after itself. The framework was pointed at 1,386 Gitea instances and produced confirmed compromises at 11 organisations in five countries — Canada, Argentina, Taiwan, the United States and Sri Lanka, per Acronis’s primary report — spanning defence, elections, energy, aerospace, government and telecommunications. The telecom relevance is not that carriers run Gitea more than anyone else; it is that a self-hosted code platform inside an operator holds network automation, device configuration templates and the credentials that drive them, and it is the kind of internal service that gets stood up by an engineering team and then falls outside the patch programme. This is exploitation of unpatched instances of a two-month-old flaw, not a zero-day, which makes it a question about your asset inventory rather than about your patch speed. On 21 September GreyNoise reported that the actor behind the Zyxel GS1900 switch campaign above also exploits CVE-2026-60004 and shares command-and-control infrastructure with Red Heron; GreyNoise presents that as an inferred overlap, and this page does not treat the two as confirmed to be the same group. Confirm every Gitea instance you own is on a fixed release, then audit for accounts you did not create and repository clones you did not authorise — patching does not evict an attacker who already holds a valid account.

High Threat Intel

Microsoft documents two initial-access crews phoning helpdesks, enrolling personal devices, then draining Microsoft 365 through the Graph API

Microsoft researchers have detailed at least two initial-access-broker clusters, tracked as Storm-3032 and Storm-3121, running voice-phishing campaigns since May 2026. The pattern reported on 10 September 2026 is consistent across both: a caller impersonates the IT helpdesk, walks the target to a convincing Microsoft sign-in page on a domain built to look procedural — the example given is company.add-passkey.com — and captures credentials and session tokens from the victim’s own personal device rather than a managed endpoint. From there the operators abuse the Microsoft Graph API to enumerate and pull data out of SharePoint, OneDrive and Exchange in small, periodic transfers sized to look like ordinary synchronisation rather than exfiltration. Access is reported to be sold on to extortion groups including ShinyHunters. Microsoft says it has not yet tied the campaigns to a confirmed corporate breach, and no victim organisation has been named.

The reason this belongs on a telecom page is structural rather than sectoral. Carriers, ISPs and cable operators run some of the largest helpdesk and retail-channel workforces in any industry, with high turnover, shift coverage, outsourced tiers and a culture of resetting things quickly for colleagues and customers alike — which is exactly the ground a helpdesk-impersonation call is designed to work. The BYOD step is what defeats the usual controls: nothing touches a managed device, so endpoint detection never sees it, and the subsequent activity is a legitimately-issued token calling a legitimate API. The detections that matter are identity-side, not endpoint-side: new device registrations and passkey or MFA-method additions that follow a helpdesk contact, sign-ins from unmanaged devices, consent grants and unusual Graph API read volume against SharePoint and Exchange, and any authentication against look-alike sign-in domains. The corresponding process control is verification of the caller on the helpdesk side, not just verification of the user.

Cisco confirms attackers are exploiting a CVSS 10.0 zero-day in Identity Services Engine — root on the system that decides who and what gets onto the network

CVE-2026-76460 is an authentication bypass in Cisco Identity Services Engine and ISE Passive Identity Connector, rated CVSS 10.0. Cisco describes insufficient authentication control on an API endpoint that lets an unauthenticated remote attacker bypass the web management interface and execute commands as root. CISA added the flaw to the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog on 16 September 2026 with a three-day federal deadline of 19 September, which has now passed. ISE and ISE-PIC 3.0 through 3.5 are affected; the fixes are 3.1 Patch 12, 3.2 Patch 11, 3.3 Patch 12, 3.4 Patch 7 and 3.5 Patch 4, and there is no workaround. It is the third actively exploited ISE flaw since June 2025, and Cisco’s second exploited zero-day in a matter of days after the Secure Email Gateway bug covered below. No new development was found between 22 and 24 September: the Cisco advisory was last seen at revision 1.0 of 16 September, the exploitation statement is unchanged, and no exposure count or attribution has been published. What did arrive the same day, and is easy to miss behind the zero-day, is Cisco’s September hardening batch — 77 further CVEs by The Hacker News’s count, 41 of them in ISE and 28 in Secure Firewall, none reported exploited; the ISE hardening advisory notes that one flaw “belonging to this vulnerability class” is known exploited, and that is CVE-2026-76460 itself, not a new one. VulnCheck’s assessment is that root on ISE lets an attacker change network-access policy, pull stored credentials, delete logs and move laterally; the authorisation layer becomes the attacker’s. No telecom victim has been named. Because Cisco itself warns of evidence tampering, a patch is not closure. Cisco’s remediation guidance is to re-image affected nodes and restore from configuration backups, review access.log for suspicious usernames and cross-check network and firewall logs held somewhere ISE cannot write to. The advisory’s specific check is show logging application ise-kong/access.log | include dummyuser from the admin CLI, and the apigateway access log inside a support bundle; the only mitigation short of the patch is an infrastructure ACL on management traffic. Then rotate what ISE holds — RADIUS and TACACS+ shared secrets, directory join and bind accounts, certificates and administrator credentials — and compare authorisation policy against a known-good export.

A crafted email is enough to get root on Cisco Secure Email Gateway, and Cisco confirms the flaw is already being exploited

CVE-2026-76461 is an unauthenticated remote code execution flaw in Cisco Secure Email Gateway and Secure Email and Web Manager, rated CVSS 9.8, and Cisco has confirmed exploitation in the wild. Treat an unpatched appliance that has been internet-reachable during the exploitation window as a compromise investigation, not a patch task: apply the AsyncOS fix, then rotate the appliance’s credentials, LDAP or Active Directory bind accounts and API keys, and review mail-flow policies, message-filter rules and administrator accounts for changes you did not make.
