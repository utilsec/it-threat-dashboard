Top Story

Bitget says attackers took about $351.6 million from its hot wallets by compromising a backend system in its wallet infrastructure and using it to push fraudulent transfers through its own approval process — the exchange says the pattern is consistent with North Korean groups, but no group has been named and no government has attributed it

The cryptocurrency exchange Bitget disclosed on 25 September 2026 that it lost about $351.6 million in digital assets in an incident detected on 24 September. In the incident report posted by chief executive Gracy Chen and reported by SecurityWeek and The Hacker News, Bitget says the attacker compromised a critical backend system in its wallet infrastructure and abused it to get fraudulent transfers approved. The assets taken were ETH, XRP, BNB, AVAX, USDT and USDC across several blockchains, with XRP the largest loss on a single chain. Bitget says the theft was limited to hot wallets, that private keys were not compromised and that cold wallets were not affected; some blockchain foundations have frozen addresses linked to the attacker, and Mandiant and the blockchain security firm SlowMist are assisting the investigation. On attribution Bitget is careful, and this page follows it: Chen said that, “based on IP behavior patterns and on-chain analysis, the attack method in this incident is highly consistent with known patterns of North Korean hacker organizations.” That is the victim’s own assessment. No specific group has been named, and no law-enforcement, government or blockchain-analytics attribution had been published at the time of this edition. The loss is confirmed by the company that suffered it; the attribution is not confirmed by anyone else yet. The mechanism is the part every custodian, exchange and payments operator should take away. Bitget’s description is not of stolen keys or a broken smart contract but of an internal system that was trusted to describe transactions to the approval step: once the attacker controlled that system, the approval process did what it was designed to do and signed. Multi-party signing, hardware security modules and withdrawal approvals protect the key; they do not protect against a compromised system feeding them a false picture of what they are approving. The same pattern applies outside crypto wherever a payment-release step trusts an upstream system’s rendering of beneficiary, amount or destination — treasury platforms, wire-release queues and card-settlement jobs included. Root cause, dwell time and how the backend system was first reached have not been disclosed.

High Breach

Revolut’s forged government requests came from a hijacked Italian Interior Ministry certified-mail account — about 680 high-value customers exposed over five to six months, and the breach now has a second act: a smishing wave that lands inside genuine Revolut text threads and fakes the video identity check — and on 25 September a second notice: DriveWealth, the US broker that once carried Revolut customers’ US-stock trades, was breached through social engineering

Revolut has confirmed that fraudsters sent it fake government data requests, that its compliance process accepted them as genuine, and that customer data was released. The disclosure was first reported on 12 September 2026 and widely covered on 14 September. Revolut’s own description is that “an unauthorized third party utilized a legitimate government agency domain email to submit fraudulent requests for information”. Updated 17–18 September: SecurityWeek, citing Hudson Rock and other investigators, reports that the domain was genuine because the account was: infostealer malware had captured the login of an employee of Italy’s Ministry of the Interior on its certified-email (PEC) system, and Hudson Rock found around 300 compromised logins for that domain in infostealer logs. The forged requests were sent to Revolut Bank UAB in Lithuania and, per the same reporting, ran for five to six months. American Banker reports the Lithuanian entity appears to have been chosen because EU law obliges it to answer European Investigation Orders, and that about 680 customers were affected — described as high-net-worth and crypto “whales” — none of them in the US. Revolut itself still says only “a limited number of customers” and has not named the agency. An actor calling itself IAmNotAVillain posted a $3M ransom demand on 16 September; Revolut says it “has not received any direct contact or demand”. Earlier reporting had cited a 10,000 BTC demand. The same actor claims 147 GB taken from an Italian law-enforcement agency, which Italian police are investigating. The 680-customer, five-month and $3M figures come from researchers and the actor, not from Revolut. What was handed over is unusually complete: names, dates of birth, occupations, addresses, email addresses and phone numbers, passport and driver’s licence copies, identity-verification selfies, statements with IBANs, and transaction and crypto history. Revolut says its systems and customer funds were not affected and that it has notified law enforcement, data-protection authorities and financial regulators. The new detail removes the comfortable reading of this incident. The request did not come from a look-alike domain that a careful analyst could have spotted; it came from a real government mailbox whose credentials were sitting in infostealer logs, so every check that validates the sender’s domain, headers or reference format would have passed. Law-enforcement request desks, subpoena and court-order intake, emergency disclosure requests and regulator information demands all end at a small team authorised to release exactly this dossier, and most verify the request rather than the requester. The control that works is an out-of-band callback to a number the institution already holds for that authority, dual authorisation before any bulk identity or transaction export, and volume alerting on the request desk itself — a single requester drawing hundreds of high-value customer files over months is a pattern somebody should have seen. It is also a reporting event: under DORA the initial notification clock for a major ICT-related incident runs in hours, and US banking institutions sit under a 36-hour supervisory notification rule, both starting from classification. Updated 21 September: the follow-on has arrived. Malwarebytes, reported by Infosecurity Magazine, has tracked a smishing campaign against Revolut customers running since 14 September, two days after the disclosure. The texts warn that the recipient must confirm their identity or lose access, and because they arrive inside the existing Revolut SMS conversation on the phone they read as a continuation of genuine bank messages. The link opens a page that asks for camera access and imitates Revolut’s live-video identity check before asking for the password — a convincing liveness step followed by a credential screen, which Malwarebytes says is enough to take over an account outright. Several hundred accounts are thought to have been affected, with high-net-worth crypto users singled out. American Banker’s 18 September account, updated 20 September, confirms the vector: credentials for Italy’s PEC certified-mail system tied to the Interior Ministry domain, believed bought from infostealer logs, used to send requests over several months to Revolut Bank UAB in Lithuania; Revolut has notified data-protection authorities and financial regulators, and the OCC’s conditional approval of its US national-bank charter earlier this month is contingent on information-security compliance. Whether the smishing list is the stolen dossier or an opportunistic mailing is not established, but the targeting matches. For every other institution the lesson is the sequence: a breach of customer contact data is followed, within days, by a campaign that impersonates the victim firm’s own security process, and any bank whose app uses a video or selfie check has just watched that check be faked. Updated 25 September — a second, separate incident: The Register reports that DriveWealth, a US brokerage that provides execution and clearing services, has notified customers that a “sophisticated social engineering campaign” on 4–5 September exposed historic customer data, and that the people affected include Revolut customers who used to trade US stocks through DriveWealth. Revolut moved its UK, EEA and Australian customers off that arrangement between December 2023 and June 2025, so the exposed records are legacy data. Per the notices as quoted, the data includes names, email and postal addresses, phone numbers, employment information, country of citizenship, age, gender and partial DriveWealth account numbers; passwords, payment and card details and bank account information were not affected. DriveWealth warns the data “could potentially be used for identity fraud, impersonation, further social engineering, or unsolicited contact”; Revolut says its own systems were not compromised and customer funds and investments remain safe. Neither company has given a count, DriveWealth’s website carried no public notice at the time of writing, and only one outlet has reported it so far — treat the detail as provisional. It is a different company and a different vector from the forged-request breach, but for the same customers it means a second set of contact and identity details in circulation in one month, which is exactly the raw material the smishing wave above needs. It is also a reminder that customer data held by a former brokerage, custody or banking-as-a-service partner does not leave your risk register when the customers migrate: ask former partners what they still retain and on what deletion schedule.

Hilltop National Bank restores online and mobile banking twelve days after taking every system offline — business positive pay is still down, the backlog is being worked by hand, and the bank has still not said what happened or whether data left

Hilltop National Bank of Casper, Wyoming, a $1.14 billion institution with eight offices and about $988 million in deposits, has been operating without its core systems since 9 September 2026, American Banker reported on 15 September. The bank says its IT systems began degrading on 8 September; overnight on 9 September it confirmed a cybersecurity incident and took every system offline, closing all offices that day. Five offices reopened on 10 September with limited service, and by 15 September seven of eight were open, but online and mobile banking and the public website were still down, scheduled bill payments were suspended with the bank reimbursing late fees, debit cards were capped at $1,000 a day, and branches were handling only deposits, withdrawals and internal checks. President and chief executive Darren Cantlay acknowledged the incident in a video message to customers. The bank has notified the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency and the Federal Reserve Bank of Kansas City, which is what the 36-hour computer-security incident notification rule requires. What it has not done is name a cause: it has not called the incident ransomware, no extortion group had claimed it on a leak site as of the reporting, and there is no statement on whether customer data left its systems. Those gaps should be read as unknowns rather than reassurance. The shape of the event — every system down at once, a week-plus rebuild, services restored in order of how manual they can be made — is the shape a community bank should plan around whatever the cause turns out to be. Most institutions of this size run their core, online banking and card processing through a small number of providers, and the fallback when all of it is unavailable is a branch with a cash drawer, a card limit and a hand-written ledger. That is survivable for a day; it is a different problem in week two, when payroll, mortgage and loan payments start to miss. Updated 21–22 September: the bank’s own update site reports personal mobile banking restored on 21 September along with in-branch debit-card printing, then personal and business online banking that evening, and on 22 September business online banking with saved ACH and transfer templates and the business mobile app. Business positive pay — the check-fraud control that matches presented items against a business customer’s issued list — was the one service explicitly still offline. Transactions from the outage were processed manually and a backlog is still being worked; ATM balances were expected to lag until the backlog cleared. On 14 September the bank said it had “no evidence” of any impact to customers’ account balances, but across every update it has not named a cause, called the event ransomware, or said whether customer data was exposed. It also told customers that only information from its Facebook page and update site is valid, which is the right message for a bank whose customers are about to be targeted by recovery-themed fraud. For peers, the positive-pay gap is the detail to copy: business customers who lose positive pay lose their main defence against altered and counterfeit checks, so a restoration plan should say when that control returns and what the bank does with exception items until it does.

A data-centre cooling failure took 23 credit unions in 14 states offline through their shared core provider — not an attack, but the same outage a ransomware event on a core would produce

On 15 September 2026 a cooling-system failure at the data centre hosting the core-processing platform of Sharetec, a Fort Wayne, Indiana core provider to small credit unions, knocked 23 credit unions across 14 states offline, American Banker reported on 21 September. The institutions hold about $2.56 billion in combined assets and serve roughly 200,000 members. Member notices posted by the credit unions say Sharetec’s backup systems “did not have time to take over”. The facility is reported to be Ark Data Centers in Cedar Rapids, Iowa, an identification made from internet records rather than confirmed by the company. Iberville Federal Credit Union in Plaquemine, Louisiana, an $8.9 million institution with 2,122 members, closed on 18 September because of the outage. The article does not say when service was restored. Any adversary who reached that platform would have produced the same outage. American Banker also notes that the National Credit Union Administration, nine months earlier, dropped its own request for authority to examine third-party vendors, saying information-sharing agreements might suffice; the credit unions affected here are supervised, their core provider is not.

AWS says data held only in its Bahrain region cannot be recovered after drone strikes — the first permanent regional loss at a hyperscaler is a resilience test financial firms can no longer call hypothetical

Amazon Web Services has told customers that resources and data hosted exclusively in its Bahrain region, me-south-1, cannot be restored, and that one Availability Zone in its UAE region, me-central-1, is a total loss, Help Net Security reported on 17 September 2026 from AWS’s 15 September updates. The cause is physical rather than cyber: Iranian drone strikes that began in early March. AWS says two of its UAE facilities were directly struck; in Bahrain a nearby strike caused structural and power damage and fire suppression caused water damage, which AWS says exceeded what its redundancy design could withstand. A second Bahrain Availability Zone was disrupted in April, taking the region fully offline. AWS says most customers had already relocated using backups; restoration of the remaining UAE zones continues and it expects further Bahrain updates in early 2027. No specific financial institution has been reported as losing data. The lesson for regulated firms is nonetheless direct. Operational-resilience regimes such as DORA and the UK impact-tolerance rules assume a cloud region can fail; here one did, permanently, and the customers who kept their data were the ones holding copies outside it. A multi-zone design protected against a single facility failure, not against a regional event that took out more than one zone.

IDScan.net confirms the breach behind 153 million driver-licence scans — the identity-verification layer under remote account opening is the thing that was taken

IDScan.net, the Louisiana identity-verification vendor whose technology is used by banks, car-rental firms, retailers and hospitality businesses to check government IDs, has confirmed unauthorised access to its cloud platform. The intrusion was discovered around 1 September 2026 and disclosed on 4 September; BleepingComputer reported the confirmation on 10 September. Exposed material covers more than 153 million driver-licence scans, about 10 million identification cards, roughly 3 million travel documents and some 579,000 medical cards. Brian Krebs first surfaced the data on 1 September after it was advertised on a dark-web marketplace calling itself Nexus, and verified the listing by searching the database. This is a state change from the position this page carried on 10 September, when the vendor had published nothing and the breach was unconfirmed: the occurrence is now acknowledged, though the number of individuals affected has not been stated. It is the first regulator action on the breach, and a reason for institutions with Canadian customers to document what they have asked their verification provider and when.

High Breach

Trezor says a former fulfilment vendor still held customer records it had certified as deleted — roughly 80,689 US customers now exposed

Hardware-wallet maker Trezor disclosed on 5 September 2026 that a breach at its former fulfilment provider ShipMonk exposed a further 67,000 US customers, taking the reported total to about 80,689 once an earlier tranche of 13,689 is included. The records cover orders placed between November 2019 and August 2021 and contain names, email addresses, phone numbers, shipping addresses and order numbers. Trezor stated plainly that throughout the relationship it had repeatedly requested and received written assurance that the data had been deleted in line with its contract and data policy — and that the data was nonetheless still present in ShipMonk’s systems. The underlying compromise is attributed by blockchain security firm Holborn to the ShinyHunters extortion group, which reportedly reached ShipMonk through CVE-2026-72898, a critical SQL injection flaw in Metabase carrying a CVSS score of 10.0. ShipMonk has not publicly acknowledged the incident. The predictable consequence arrived on 11 September, when the exposed mailing list was used to phish 347,000 Trezor customers; the company reports that roughly 2,500 recipients clicked the malicious links, with no indication so far that recovery phrases or funds were taken as a result. That is the shape of a list breach: the loss event is not the exfiltration, it is the campaign that runs against the list weeks later, against people who have every reason to believe the sender knows them. Trezor is clear that its wallets themselves are unaffected; the exposure is that a list of confirmed crypto-hardware owners with home addresses and phone numbers is an unusually well-qualified target set for phishing, vishing, impersonation and, given the addresses, physical coercion. For banks, insurers, brokers and payment firms the transferable lesson has nothing to do with crypto. This is a fourth-party retention failure: a vendor kept data past a contractual deletion date, attested in writing that it had not, and the attesting firm carried the notification duty when the attestation proved wrong. Written deletion assurances are not evidence of deletion, and DORA, the FCA regime and most US supervisory expectations put the burden on the regulated firm regardless of who actually held the file.

Microsoft’s Digital Crimes Unit takes down EvilTokens, a device-code phishing service that compromised more than 12,000 Microsoft accounts at over 10,000 organisations — financial services among the named sectors, two arrests in London, and MFA was no obstacle

Microsoft’s Digital Crimes Unit, working with Health-ISAC, law enforcement and SpyCloud, disrupted EvilTokens on 22 September 2026. The phishing-as-a-service platform, run by an operator Microsoft tracks as Storm-2992, abused the OAuth device-code authentication flow: the victim is persuaded to enter a code on a genuine Microsoft sign-in page, and the attacker’s session receives the tokens, so multi-factor authentication is completed by the victim on the attacker’s behalf. Microsoft counts more than 12,000 compromised accounts at over 10,000 organisations; SpyCloud’s data shows 8,708 accounts across 6,585 corporate domains in 79 countries. Named sectors include wholesale distribution, construction, financial services, real estate, higher education and healthcare. The kit sold for $500 a month or $1,500 outright, and reporting says it used AI to write lures and triage stolen mailboxes. The Metropolitan Police arrested two men, aged 32 and 38, who have been released on bail. A takedown removes one service, not the technique: reporting notes clones are already appearing. For banks the two exposures are the finance and treasury mailbox, where a token-holding attacker can alter payment instructions from inside a real thread, and the privileged administrator. The control is to switch off the device-code flow wherever it is not needed.

F5 published advisory K000162605 on 22 September 2026 for CVE-2026-94127, a heap-based buffer overflow in BIG-IP Access Policy Manager that lets an unauthenticated remote attacker execute code on the BIG-IP system. It is rated CVSS 9.8 under v3.1 and 9.3 under v4.0, F5 says it is being exploited, and CISA added it to KEV on 22 September with a 25 September federal deadline. The exposure is narrower than the score suggests: the vulnerable setup has an APM access policy and an OAuth authorisation server profile on the same virtual server, the one hosting the address that receives the OAuth traffic. A BIG-IP used only for load balancing or for conventional APM remote access is not in that configuration. F5 has not disclosed who is exploiting it, how many systems are affected or when attacks began, and Rapid7 reports no confirmed public proof-of-concept. Fixes are engineering hotfixes for 21.1.0, 17.5.0–17.5.1 and 17.1.0–17.1.3, and F5 offers an iRule mitigation through a support case. BIG-IP is common at banks, insurers and payment processors, and APM in the OAuth role typically fronts customer or partner API authentication — exactly the token issuer a fraud operation would want to control.

A CVSS 10.0 Cisco ISE zero-day gives unauthenticated root on the box that decides who gets onto the network — exploited, in KEV, and Cisco warns attackers may erase the evidence

Cisco has confirmed active exploitation of CVE-2026-76460, an authentication bypass in Identity Services Engine and ISE-PIC rated CVSS 10.0. Cisco describes it as insufficient authentication control on an API endpoint: an unauthenticated remote attacker can get past the web management interface and reach root command execution. ISE and ISE-PIC 3.0 through 3.5 are affected; the fixes are 3.1 Patch 12, 3.2 Patch 11, 3.3 Patch 12, 3.4 Patch 7 and 3.5 Patch 4, and there is no workaround. CISA added the flaw to the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog on 16 September 2026 with a federal deadline of 19 September, which has passed. No actor has been named, and Cisco warns attackers may remove or hide evidence of exploitation. It is the third exploited ISE flaw since June 2025 and Cisco’s second exploited zero-day in a matter of days, after the Secure Email Gateway flaw below. ISE is the policy engine that decides which users and devices get onto the network and into which segment — wired and wireless port authentication, posture checks, guest access and often the authentication behind VPN and network-device administration. VulnCheck notes that root on ISE lets an attacker change network-access policy, pull stored credentials, delete logs and move laterally. In a regulated estate that means the controls separating card-data, payments and trading segments from the general network can be rewritten from a system trusted to write them. Cisco’s own guidance goes beyond patching: re-image affected nodes, restore from configuration backups, review access.log for suspicious usernames and cross-check network and firewall logs. As of 22 September the advisory is unchanged at its 16 September revision and Cisco has published no attribution or exposure count. Its hunting guidance is specific: run “show logging application ise-kong/access.log | include dummyuser” from the admin CLI and review ise/logs/apigateway/access.log.gz in support bundles, and the only mitigation short of patching is an infrastructure access list on management traffic. An Orkes Conductor flaw that runs attacker code through the workflow API is being exploited three months after its fix — the orchestration layer is the part of the estate nobody is tracking

Attackers are exploiting CVE-2026-58138, a pre-authentication remote-code-execution flaw in the Orkes Conductor workflow-orchestration platform, SecurityWeek and The Hacker News reported on 18 and 19 September 2026. It is rated CVSS 9.8 under v3.1 and 9.3 under v4.0. Conductor’s INLINE, LAMBDA, DO_WHILE and SWITCH tasks evaluate user-supplied JavaScript or Python inside GraalVM with unrestricted host access, so anyone who can reach the workflow API can run operating-system commands without logging in. Versions 3.21.21 through 3.30.1 are affected, and the fix, 3.30.2, has been available since June. Exploitation is not new: Previdian’s honeypots saw it from 24 July, a public proof-of-concept circulated in early August, Empirical Security confirmed in-the-wild use as recently as 21 August, and Fortinet blocked about 7,000 attempts between 2 and 9 September. It is not in CISA KEV as of the 21 September catalog and no actor has been named; Fortinet’s own outbreak alert of 15 September says its telemetry is observing active attack attempts. Orchestration engines are easy to miss in a financial institution’s inventory because they are often deployed by a product or data team rather than central IT, yet where they are used they sit in the middle of onboarding, payment and reconciliation flows and hold the credentials each step needs to call downstream systems. A three-month gap between a fix and continuing exploitation is the signature of software nobody is patching.

Cisco has confirmed active exploitation of CVE-2026-76461, a SQL-injection flaw in the email-parsing path of Cisco AsyncOS for Secure Email Gateway, physical and virtual, that carries a CVSS score of 9.8. An unauthenticated attacker sends a message containing the payload and gains command execution with root privileges on the underlying operating system. Every configuration is affected, Cisco states that no workaround exists, and the fixes are AsyncOS 15.5.5-014, 16.0.4-302 and 16.5.0-780; Cisco says Secure Email and Web Manager and Secure Web Appliance are not affected. Cisco published advisory cisco-sa-esa-inj-2bLVGmhX on 14 September, and CISA added the flaw to the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog the same day with a federal remediation deadline of 17 September, which has now passed. Cisco says every Secure Email Cloud device has already been upgraded to 16.5.0-780, so the exposure that remains is on-premises and virtual appliances. Because root access lets an attacker wipe indicators, hunt mail logs for SQL statements and check egress logs as well as patching; Cisco’s advisory, revised on 17 September to clarify handling of clustered appliances, gives a search of mail_logs for the string “COPY.*TO PROGRAM” as the indicator. It lands days after exploitation of Cisco Secure Firewall Management Center was confirmed. For a bank, an insurer or a payment firm the email gateway is not a commodity appliance. The device also holds directory credentials and TLS keys. Patch it, and then question what it saw: a gateway compromise is one of the few positions from which an attacker can undo the callback-verification story your payments controls depend on.
