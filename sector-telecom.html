Top Story

Attackers are taking over internet-exposed MikroTik routers through SSH with no credentials, and CERT Polska says it started on 2 September

CERT Polska published an advisory on 5 September 2026 describing six vulnerabilities in MikroTik RouterOS and, separately, confirming that two of them are already being chained in the wild for complete device takeover. The two that matter most are CVE-2026-67276, a signature-verification failure in which RouterOS does not compare the complete RSA public key when matching an SSH authentication request, and CVE-2026-86060, an argument-injection flaw in SSH login handling that escalates a session to administrator when a username contains characters the platform should have rejected. Both are rated CVSS 9.2. The remaining four cover a bandwidth-test service reachable without authentication that can restart the kernel (CVE-2026-67277, CVSS 8.8), acceptance of malformed RSA PKCS#1 v1.5 signatures enabling TLS impersonation (CVE-2026-67278), an SSH state-machine defect that enters the connection protocol after a client-requested rekey and permits unauthenticated file operations (CVE-2026-67279), and an uninitialised-pointer read in the WebFig /jsproxy path that discloses credentials without authentication (CVE-2026-67281). MikroTik shipped fixes on 3 September in RouterOS 6.49.21, 7.23.4, 7.24.2 and 7.25beta3. CERT Polska recorded exploitation from at least 2 September, sourced to 82.192.72.4 and 103.102.31.18, and reached the findings with AI-assisted binary and protocol analysis validated by hand on live devices.

This is the item on this page most likely to describe equipment you actually operate. RouterOS is not a niche enterprise product — it is the low-cost routing, PPPoE-termination and wireless-backhaul platform that a very large number of ISPs, WISPs, hosting providers and small carriers build their access networks on, and MikroTik boxes routinely sit at the customer edge with management interfaces facing the internet because that is how they are provisioned and supported. An unauthenticated path to administrator on a device in that position gives an attacker routing control, tunnel creation, DNS redirection, traffic interception and a residential-looking egress point in one step, and history says MikroTik compromises get monetised as proxy and botnet infrastructure rather than cleaned up quietly. The saving grace is that CERT Polska published usable indicators. Look for log lines reading login failure for user -2 from <ip> via ssh, for user <name> added by ssh:-2@<ip>, and for the presence of a privileged account named ops; any of the three should be treated as a compromise rather than a curiosity. Patch first, then hunt, because patching a router that already has an attacker-created admin account does not remove the attacker. Where an immediate upgrade is not possible, CERT Polska’s interim advice is to take SSH and the bandwidth-test server off any internet-facing interface until the update lands. No victim count and no attacker attribution have been published, and neither The Hacker News’s review of the warning nor CERT Polska’s own posts state how many devices have been taken over — so treat scale as unknown rather than small.

A critical NetScaler authentication bypass went from public proof-of-concept to live exploitation in about a day

CVE-2026-19490 is a critical authentication bypass in Citrix NetScaler ADC and Gateway, disclosed in a Citrix advisory on 19 August 2026. It affects appliances configured as an AAA virtual server or as a Gateway — SSL VPN, ICA Proxy, CVPN or RDP Proxy — with the precise impact varying by firmware and SAML Action configuration. SecurityWeek reported at disclosure that exploitation was expected; it arrived quickly. After a proof-of-concept appeared publicly, Ryan Dewhurst of Previdian recorded exploitation attempts from three countries on 3 September, and Belgium’s national cybersecurity centre issued its own warning the following day. Authentication bypass is the worst failure mode for a device in this role, because it strips the control everything behind the Gateway assumes is holding — MFA, conditional access, session identity — without generating a failed-login signal for anyone to notice. For carriers and ISPs, NetScaler typically fronts customer self-service portals, provisioning front ends and staff application access, so a bypass there is an identity problem before it is an appliance problem.

Critical Incident

Attackers are exploiting an unauthenticated SQL injection in Sangoma Switchvox to reach code execution on enterprise phone systems

CVE-2026-9586 is an unauthenticated SQL injection in Sangoma Switchvox, the VoIP and unified-communications platform used to run enterprise PBX estates, rated CVSS 9.3 and exploited in the wild. The defect sits in an XML-processing endpoint that concatenates an unauthenticated PhoneIP parameter straight into PostgreSQL queries with no parameterisation, so an attacker who can reach the interface can run arbitrary SQL and pivot from there to code execution. Horizon3 confirmed live exploitation and published indicators of compromise; CISA added the CVE to the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog on 2 September with a 5 September federal remediation date, and reporting on that KEV batch describes post-exploitation activity including reverse shells and credential harvesting. It matters more on this page than on a general IT one because Switchvox is telephony infrastructure — deployed by carriers, MVNOs, hosted-voice providers and the channel partners who resell them, and typically sitting close to SIP trunks and provisioning data rather than in a hardened data-centre tier. A database compromise on a PBX is not just a server; it is extension mappings, SIP registration credentials, call detail records and voicemail — the raw materials for toll fraud, for caller-ID spoofing that survives downstream trust checks, and for quiet call-metadata collection. The federal deadline has now passed. Treat any internet-reachable Switchvox as compromised until proven otherwise, and start with the SIP and administrative credentials it holds rather than with the patch alone.

High Threat Intel

A China-nexus group is living inside Cisco IOS XR routers and TACACS servers, turning carrier-class network gear into a listening post

Sygnia has published incident-response research documenting activity by Fire Ant, a China-nexus intrusion set whose operations the firm assesses as strongly overlapping with UNC3886 while noting differences in filenames, paths and implementation that stop short of conclusive attribution. What makes it matter for this sector is that it is not a CVE. The group moved off the VMware hypervisor estates it was known for and onto the network layer itself: Cisco IOS XR routers, TACACS authentication servers and Linux management hosts. On the routers a custom implant ran behind a fake system service on an alternating schedule to limit its exposure to observation, while a second backdoor, BridgeAgent, presented itself as a legitimate Zabbix monitoring agent and supplied tunnelling and persistence. The operators created GRE tunnel interfaces that left no entry in configuration history and suppressed the syslog messages that would have described them, took interactive shell access without logging, and manipulated file timestamps to frustrate forensics. The routers then became collection platforms, with packet captures written on the device and uploaded to external FTP servers — exposing internal topology, administrative sessions, authentication flows and routing relationships. Sygnia describes a “target behind the target” pattern, in which trusted infrastructure is used to reach the high-value networks that connect to it.

For a carrier or ISP this lands on exactly the assets that usually sit outside the security programme rather than inside it. IOS XR is the operating system of the service-provider core; TACACS decides who may touch it. Compromise both and an adversary holds traffic visibility, credential flow and the authorisation layer at once. The second half of the finding is worse than the first: because the actor suppressed logging, the telemetry an operator would reach for to answer “has this happened to us” is the same telemetry the actor was editing. Sygnia’s guidance is to treat routers, TACACS servers, hypervisors and jump hosts as first-class forensic and Tier-0 assets — export their telemetry to independently administered systems, validate logs against more than one source of evidence, keep out-of-band access that does not traverse the equipment being investigated, and limit the blast radius of trust between connected networks. Sygnia declined to name the victim’s sector or location and has published indicators of compromise, YARA rules and hunting guidance. There is no patch, because no vulnerability was named: this is a hunting exercise, and the IOS XR hardening release further down this page is the reason not to defer it.

Medium Threat Intel

An operator quietly took over 14,530 Dahua cameras in 35 days, concentrating on Russian and CIS telecom netblocks

Threat-intelligence firm Hunt.io found an exposed operator directory and reconstructed a campaign it calls CameraSwarm, in which a single actor compromised 14,530 Dahua IP cameras between 17 June and 22 July 2026. The toolkit was ordinary but methodical: internet-wide scanning with masscan against Dahua’s port 37777, an asyncio credential brute-forcing engine that reached 12,324 unique IP addresses, and a binary called p2pwn that chained older authentication bypasses — CVE-2021-33044 and CVE-2021-33045 — to plant persistent backdoor accounts on a further 1,923 devices. A cloud-relay technique using serial numbers and embedded SDK credentials reached 283 more cameras sitting behind NAT, and Hunt.io reports that 89.4% of live serial numbers it tested exposed an access channel without authentication. Results were exported in the XML format used by Dahua’s enterprise management software and exfiltrated through a Telegram pipeline. The telecom angle is in the targeting rather than the tooling: after early reconnaissance that touched Mexican and Vietnamese ISP ranges, the operator’s focus settled on Russian and CIS telecom netblocks, with Ukraine holding the largest share of compromised devices. That is a reminder that the surveillance estate hanging off an access network is part of the operator’s attack surface whether or not the operator owns it — NAT is not a control, cloud P2P relays route around perimeter filtering, and a camera fleet with default or reused credentials becomes durable infrastructure inside customer address space.

Critical Vulnerability

Cisco Nexus 9000 switches with Silicon One silicon expose two TCP ports in the default VRF that lead to unauthenticated root code execution

Cisco published a critical advisory on 2 September 2026 for CVE-2026-20212, a CVSS 9.8 remote code execution flaw in Nexus 9000 Series switches built on Silicon One ASICs. The advisory states that TCP ports 43210 and 43211 are reachable in the default Layer 3 virtual routing and forwarding instance, and that an unauthenticated remote attacker who can reach them can execute arbitrary code with root privileges on the switch. Affected hardware includes the N9K-C9804 and N9K-C9808 chassis and a set of fixed switches — N9324C-SE1U, N9348Y2C6D-SE1U, N9364E-SG2-O, N9364E-SG2-Q, N9396T12C-SE1, N9348Y12C-SE1, N9396Y12C-SE1 and N9336C-SE1. Cisco PSIRT says it is not aware of public exploit code or malicious use. For telecom and media operators the exposure follows deployment: these are high-density data-centre and AI-fabric switches, increasingly used in the compute estates that carriers build for network analytics, media processing and hosted services, and the vulnerable ports being open in the default VRF means the risk depends on how far that VRF reaches rather than on a feature someone opted into. Root on a switch is not a device compromise so much as a fabric compromise: traffic mirroring, VLAN manipulation and a persistent foothold under the visibility of most host-based tooling.

A memory-exhaustion flaw lets an unauthenticated attacker knock Cisco desk phones offline until someone reboots them by hand

CVE-2026-20281 is a high-severity denial-of-service vulnerability, rated CVSS 7.5, affecting Cisco Desk Phone 9800 Series, IP Phone 7800 and 8800 Series and Video Phone 8875 handsets running SIP Software. Informational Policy

France’s ANSSI and its G7 partners are pressing public and private organisations to start the post-quantum migration now

ANSSI, working through the G7 Cyber Expert Group, published a joint call to action on 3 September 2026 urging public and private organisations to accelerate the transition to post-quantum cryptography, with European guidance pointing at a 2026 start for transition planning. The reasoning is the familiar “harvest now, decrypt later” argument: traffic and stored data captured today can be decrypted once a cryptographically relevant quantum computer exists, so the migration clock is set by the sensitivity lifetime of the data rather than by the arrival date of the machine. That argument bites harder in telecom than in most sectors. Operators run some of the longest-lived cryptographic estates anywhere — subscriber authentication keys, SIM and eSIM provisioning material, roaming and interconnect security, backhaul and transport encryption, lawful-intercept infrastructure, and hardware in the field on refresh cycles measured in a decade. Very little of it can be re-keyed on a software release. The practical first step in the G7 material is not a technology purchase but an inventory: know where cryptography is used, which algorithms and key lengths, in what hardware, and how long each protected item must stay confidential. Nothing here is a mandate, and no telecom-specific deadline has been set, but building a cryptographic asset register is the item that governs how expensive everything after it becomes.

How to read the indicators. Exploitation and CISA KEV tags reflect status at generation time — “No known exploitation / Not in CISA KEV” means none was reported as of this edition, not that risk is absent. Suggested SLAs are starting points (Critical ≈ 7 days, High ≈ 30 days) to adapt to your own risk tolerance. Owner and status notes you enter are saved only in your local browser and are not transmitted anywhere.

Methodology. Compiled on-demand by searching authoritative security sources including CISA advisories and the KEV catalog, and reporting from BleepingComputer, the SANS Internet Storm Center, Dark Reading, The Register, The Hacker News and SecurityWeek, plus vendor and product advisories (Microsoft, Oracle, Cisco, Citrix, Progress, SimpleHelp, Fortinet) and Sysdig threat research. Default lookback: 7–30 days. Severity tiers are editorial judgments based on described impact; consult the linked source for official CVSS scores and affected versions. “Claimed” / “reported” items are unverified assertions; “confirmed” items have vendor or agency acknowledgment.
