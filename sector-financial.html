ConnectWise is restoring Cloud Backup in phases after Microsoft tied the compromise to China-aligned Storm-0940, which used the product’s own app identities to sign in to customers’ Microsoft 365 tenants — financial services is on its target list

Microsoft said on 21 October 2026 that it assesses Storm-0940, an actor it calls China-state aligned, was responsible for the ConnectWise supply-chain compromise. The attacker controlled several ConnectWise cloud application identities tied to Cloud Backup (formerly SkyKick) and used them to authenticate to customers’ Microsoft 365 tenants as the application, so user MFA never came into play. Microsoft says sign-in attempts reached more than 2,250 tenants — attempts, not confirmed compromises — and Huntress saw unauthorised sign-ins at 162 tenants it monitors. Storm-0940 drove the work with ARTEX, an open-source penetration-testing framework that can use any AI model. ConnectWise first detected the activity on 14 October; it suspended Cloud Backup and shut down SaaS Security and Cloud Management, which connect to it. Chief executive Manny Rivelo told Cybersecurity Dive on 22 October that service is being restored in phases and that customers should keep their connections disabled until ConnectWise gives them direct instructions. Microsoft lists financial services among Storm-0940’s targets; no financial victim has been named, and Microsoft does not attribute the earlier South Korean bank breaches to it.

Medium Supply Chain

Hardware-wallet maker Keystone says intruders stole the key its devices use to prove they are genuine — it will destroy 18,835 unsold wallets, and 25,964 buyers’ order records were exposed

Keystone said on 26 October 2026 that an attacker exploited a vulnerability in a third-party data-monitoring tool on its website, escalated privileges and took the device-authentication key its hardware wallets use to pass the company’s online genuineness check. The key is written in at manufacture and cannot be changed, so anyone holding a copy could build counterfeits that pass that check. Keystone says the key plays no part in recovery phrases, private keys or transaction signing, and that no counterfeit devices or thefts have been reported. It found and blocked the access on 20 October and first disclosed it on 24 October; it has not said when the attacker first got in. It will destroy 18,835 unsold devices carrying the old key and has paused website shipping until new-key devices are ready. Owners who received a device after 20 October should not use it until Keystone support confirms it is genuine; devices delivered earlier that pass the check can be used normally. Names, contact details, shipping addresses and order details for 25,964 website purchases from 2024 to 2026, held in Shopify, were also exposed, along with 12,873 unfinished checkouts; card, bank and ID data were not involved. Editorial assessment: after Ledger’s grey-market implant earlier this month, it is the second hardware-wallet supply-chain problem on this page in October, and firms that hold digital assets on hardware signers should know where each device came from and when it arrived.

CrowdStrike reported on 7 October 2026 that attacker directories it found exposed online show one operator using ARTEX, an open-source agentic penetration-testing tool developed in China, with several large language models to break into several South Korean financial organisations between late September and early October and take data. The FBI and Secret Service say the FortiBleed credential campaign against Fortinet firewalls and SSL VPNs is still running — no CVE, just leaked passwords, weak legacy hashes and sniffed logins, with ransomware following

The FBI and the US Secret Service published a joint advisory on 6 October 2026 warning that the campaign researchers call FortiBleed remains an active threat to internet-facing FortiGate firewalls and SSL VPN gateways. It has no CVE: in the agencies’ words it exploits reused or leaked credentials and legacy SHA-256 password storage. Researchers describe credential stuffing and spraying from leak dumps, captured password hashes sent to a GPU cracking cluster, and a Go-based tool, FortigateSniffer, that passively captures authentication traffic across 24 protocols. Some victims were locked out of their own devices after the attackers changed administrator passwords or deleted accounts, and the authorities say compromised FortiGates are being used as a way in for ransomware, with the advisory linking Payload ransomware. SOCRadar says it has confirmed at least 12 ransomware deployments from this access; the figure of more than 86,644 working device credentials across 194 countries dates from 19 June, when SOCRadar and Hudson Rock first documented the campaign, and is not a new count. The agencies say targets span all 16 US critical-infrastructure sectors, financial services among them. Researchers describe the operator as a Russian-speaking initial-access broker with overlaps to INC and Lynx ransomware; that is their assessment, not the agencies’. Editorial assessment: banks and credit unions that run FortiGate at branches or for remote access should treat this as a credential incident in progress rather than a patch job.

Critical Breach

Bitget’s investigators say the way in was two compromised third-party security appliances, one reached through a zero-day — Mandiant puts the hot-wallet loss at $351.6–387.5 million, and the North Korea link is still Bitget’s and blockchain analysts’ assessment, not a government attribution

The cryptocurrency exchange Bitget disclosed on 25 September 2026 that attackers had taken digital assets from its hot wallets. Two documents published since explain how. Mandiant’s status update of 28 September, released by Bitget, says the attackers gained unauthorised privileged access to two unnamed third-party security appliances, referred to as A and B, planted a web shell with a command-and-control connection on appliance B, and used that foothold to push malicious packages to the production server that runs wallet jobs. Mandiant dates the initial compromise to 24 September; the unauthorised transfers were detected at 02:31 (UTC+8) on 25 September and continued until about 05:23, and it estimates the loss at between $351.6 million and $387.5 million across several chains. Cold wallets were not affected and Mandiant found no evidence that private keys were compromised. On 1 October Bitget said its investigation had found malicious activity involving third-party security products, including a zero-day, and had recovered the custom tool used to start the withdrawals (The Hacker News). SlowMist’s report, as summarised by The Hacker News, puts the earliest malicious activity on 31 August, when a service on one of Product A’s nodes was hit by the zero-day and the attacker read an environment variable holding a database password; it describes similar activity on two more nodes on 23 and 25 September, access to Product B’s management platform with an employee’s identity on 25 September, and withdrawals across 11 blockchains. Mandiant’s and SlowMist’s start dates differ, and neither the appliance vendors nor the vulnerability has been named. About $1.1 million has been frozen by Circle, Tether and NEAR Intents. Correction: earlier editions of this page carried Bitget’s revised figure of about $390.06 million; this edition uses Mandiant’s range. On attribution this page stays with the evidence: Bitget’s chief executive, Gracy Chen, says IP behaviour and on-chain analysis are consistent with known North Korean groups; TRM Labs points to overlaps with TraderTraitor, and Elliptic links the funds to earlier North Korea-attributed thefts, including addresses tied to the 2025 Bybit theft. Mandiant’s published update makes no attribution, and no government has attributed the theft. The lesson has moved from the signing path to the security stack itself. NIST published “Transition to Post-Quantum Cryptography Standards” (NIST IR 8547) on 27 October 2026, Nextgov reported. Help Net Security reported on 28 October that quantum-vulnerable public-key algorithms are to be deprecated after 2030 and disallowed under NIST standards after 2035.
