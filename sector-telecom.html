Cisco confirms attackers are exploiting a CVSS 10.0 zero-day in Identity Services Engine — root on the system that decides who and what gets onto the network

CVE-2026-76460 is an authentication bypass in Cisco Identity Services Engine and ISE Passive Identity Connector, rated CVSS 10.0. Cisco describes insufficient authentication control on an API endpoint that lets an unauthenticated remote attacker bypass the web management interface and execute commands as root. Cisco says it is aware of active exploitation, warns that attackers may remove or hide evidence of it, and has not named an actor. CISA added the flaw to the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog on 16 September 2026 with a three-day federal deadline of 19 September, which has now passed. ISE and ISE-PIC 3.0 through 3.5 are affected; the fixes are 3.1 Patch 12, 3.2 Patch 11, 3.3 Patch 12, 3.4 Patch 7 and 3.5 Patch 4, and there is no workaround. It is the third actively exploited ISE flaw since June 2025, and Cisco’s second exploited zero-day in a matter of days after the Secure Email Gateway bug covered below. For an operator the weight comes from what ISE is: the RADIUS and TACACS+ policy engine behind 802.1X on corporate and NOC networks, device administration for routers and switches, and VPN posture — and many carriers also run it inside managed network and security services for business customers. VulnCheck’s assessment is that root on ISE lets an attacker change network-access policy, pull stored credentials, delete logs and move laterally; the authorisation layer becomes the attacker’s. No telecom victim has been named. Because Cisco itself warns of evidence tampering, a patch is not closure. Cisco’s remediation guidance is to re-image affected nodes and restore from configuration backups, review access.log for suspicious usernames and cross-check network and firewall logs held somewhere ISE cannot write to. Then rotate what ISE holds — RADIUS and TACACS+ shared secrets, directory join and bind accounts, certificates and administrator credentials — and compare authorisation policy against a known-good export.

High Incident

Telus tells customers that attackers were inside subscriber accounts for sixteen months — and the way in was credentials, not a vulnerability

Telus customer notifications, reported by SecurityWeek on 14 September 2026, say that compromised credentials were used to access consumer accounts between February 2025 and June 2026, and that the data obtained was then used to lure customers into switching providers and to make unauthorised service changes. The exposed fields named in the notices are names, account numbers, phone numbers, billing and email addresses, partial card numbers, and subscription and payment history. Two things Telus has not said matter as much as what it has: the number of affected customers is not disclosed, and neither is the origin of the credentials — whether they came from an internal breach or were harvested elsewhere and replayed. No software flaw has been named, and none is implied. What is confirmed is the shape of the intrusion and its duration. Sixteen months of account access that fed switching lures and unauthorised service changes is a detection failure on the customer-facing estate rather than on the network, and that estate is the one part of a carrier that is deliberately exposed to the whole internet and defended mostly by a password. The operational point for other operators is that the self-service portal is the control plane for the phone number itself: an attacker holding a subscriber session can attempt a port, change the contact address a confirmation is sent to, and alter a plan, which is the same sequence that precedes SIM-swap and account-takeover fraud downstream. Telus has forced credential resets on affected accounts, increased account monitoring, notified Vancouver Police and is offering identity-theft protection. The controls that generalise are credential-stuffing defences on the login path, mandatory multi-factor authentication on self-service rather than optional, a port-out lock or PIN that is not derivable from the account record, and alerting on the sequence of contact-detail change followed by a port or plan change — because it is the sequence, not any single request, that is the signal.

Portugal’s MEO says a “mass attack” congested and degraded its international network — the operator reports no intrusion and no data access

Altice Portugal’s MEO told the Lusa news agency on 15 September 2026 that it had been hit by “a mass attack that caused congestion and degradation of the international network”. The operator says all mitigation and recovery measures were immediately activated, that the situation was quickly controlled and service stability restored, and that the attack “did not result in any intrusion or access to data from MEO and its customers”. That is the whole of the confirmed record. MEO has not said what kind of attack it was, when it started, how long it lasted, how many customers were affected or who was behind it. The description — volume causing congestion on international capacity rather than a system compromise — is consistent with a volumetric denial-of-service attack, but MEO has not used that term and this page does not assume it. Downdetector also logged problem reports for Vodafone, NOS and Digi the same day; no link between those reports and the MEO event has been established. The operational lesson sits on the transit side. International capacity is where congestion is hardest to absorb locally, because the saturated links have a far end you do not control, and mitigation that engages only once traffic reaches your own scrubbing capacity is too late for a full upstream. The arrangements that decide how quickly an operator recovers from this kind of event are the ones agreed with transit providers in advance.

High Incident

Researchers say an intruder got into Thai broadband provider 3BB through an old FortiGate flaw, then ran MeshCentral as a hidden backdoor aimed at subscriber credentials

Hunt.io researchers, in analysis reported by The Hacker News on 14 September and SecurityWeek on 15 September 2026, say an attacker’s exposed file directory documents a compromise of 3BB, a Thai broadband provider. According to that analysis, initial access came through FortiGate SSL-VPN flaw CVE-2024-21762 — an old, KEV-listed bug — with exploits for older F5 BIG-IP flaws and Linux privilege-escalation bugs also in the toolkit. The intruder reached root, configured MeshCentral — a legitimate open-source remote-management platform — as a covert backdoor under a device group named “TH-3BB”, and targeted RADIUS subscriber credential databases; a cleanup script left the MeshCentral agent in place, and the activity was discovered around 3 June 2026. Treat this as researcher-reported: 3BB has not commented, no regulator statement has been reported, and subscriber impact has not been quantified. Two lessons hold regardless. The way in was an unpatched edge VPN appliance carrying a long-known, KEV-listed flaw. And the persistence needed no exploit at all: the tool is the implant. That second detail is worth carrying into your own environment, because a remote-management server is exactly the kind of asset that survives a hunt. It is signed, it is expected to talk to large numbers of hosts on odd ports, its agent runs as a service with administrative rights, and an unauthorised instance looks identical in process and network telemetry to an authorised one. Carrier and ISP estates are unusually exposed to this pattern because they already run several overlapping remote-management and provisioning systems — field-technician tooling, CPE and ACS management, NOC jump infrastructure, managed-service platforms for business customers — often inherited through acquisitions and rarely inventoried end to end. The control is an authoritative list of which remote-management platforms are sanctioned, which servers are authorised to run them, and which accounts are permitted to enrol agents, followed by a sweep for instances that appear on none of those lists.

A critical NetScaler authentication bypass went from public proof-of-concept to live exploitation in about a day

CVE-2026-19490 is a critical authentication bypass in Citrix NetScaler ADC and Gateway, disclosed in a Citrix advisory on 19 August 2026. It affects appliances configured as an AAA virtual server or as a Gateway — SSL VPN, ICA Proxy, CVPN or RDP Proxy — with the precise impact varying by firmware and SAML Action configuration. SecurityWeek reported at disclosure that exploitation was expected; it arrived quickly. After a proof-of-concept appeared publicly, Ryan Dewhurst of Previdian recorded exploitation attempts from three countries on 3 September, and Belgium’s national cybersecurity centre issued its own warning the following day; reporting around the KEV listing puts the documented total at 56 exploitation attempts since early September. Shadowserver counts more than 22,000 exposed NetScaler ADC appliances and close to 1,700 Gateway instances worldwide, though how many are patched is unknown. Authentication bypass is the worst failure mode for a device in this role, because it strips the control everything behind the Gateway assumes is holding — MFA, conditional access, session identity — without generating a failed-login signal for anyone to notice. For carriers and ISPs, NetScaler typically fronts customer self-service portals, provisioning front ends and staff application access, so a bypass there is an identity problem before it is an appliance problem.

High Campaign

Attackers are planting a Linux rootkit on F5 BIG-IP APM appliances that leaves no malicious file on disk

Reporting on 8 September 2026 describes intrusions in which attackers deploy a Linux rootkit on F5 BIG-IP Access Policy Manager devices. The rootkit hooks the loading of PHP files and injects a web shell directly into memory, so nothing malicious is written to the filesystem — a file-integrity check of the appliance comes back clean while the attacker retains interactive access. BIG-IP APM sits at the remote-access edge of a great many enterprise, carrier and government networks, terminating VPN and application-access sessions and holding the authentication decisions for the users behind them. That position is the whole point of the target selection: an implant there sees credentials and session traffic in the clear, and it survives the checks most teams would run. For carriers and service providers there is a second dimension. Appliances of this class are frequently operated on behalf of enterprise customers, so a compromise is a multi-tenant problem rather than a single-device one, and the tenant is unlikely to be able to inspect the box themselves. The detection approach has to shift accordingly: on-box antivirus and file hashing will not find a fileless implant, so look outward at what the appliance does — unexpected outbound connections, administrative sessions from unfamiliar sources, and changes in process behaviour — and treat vendor guidance on integrity verification as the authoritative check.

Attackers are exploiting an unauthenticated SQL injection in Sangoma Switchvox to reach code execution on enterprise phone systems

CVE-2026-9586 is an unauthenticated SQL injection in Sangoma Switchvox, the VoIP and unified-communications platform used to run enterprise PBX estates, rated CVSS 9.3 and exploited in the wild. The defect sits in an XML-processing endpoint that concatenates an unauthenticated PhoneIP parameter straight into PostgreSQL queries with no parameterisation, so an attacker who can reach the interface can run arbitrary SQL and pivot from there to code execution. Horizon3 confirmed live exploitation and published indicators of compromise; CISA added the CVE to the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog on 2 September with a 5 September federal remediation date, and reporting on that KEV batch describes post-exploitation activity including reverse shells and credential harvesting. It matters more on this page than on a general IT one because Switchvox is telephony infrastructure — deployed by carriers, MVNOs, hosted-voice providers and the channel partners who resell them, and typically sitting close to SIP trunks and provisioning data rather than in a hardened data-centre tier. A database compromise on a PBX is not just a server; it is extension mappings, SIP registration credentials, call detail records and voicemail — the raw materials for toll fraud, for caller-ID spoofing that survives downstream trust checks, and for quiet call-metadata collection. The federal deadline has now passed. Treat any internet-reachable Switchvox as compromised until proven otherwise, and start with the SIP and administrative credentials it holds rather than with the patch alone.

Red Heron scanned 1,386 self-hosted Gitea servers and got into 11 organisations — telecom among the sectors named

Acronis’s Threat Research Unit disclosed on 13 September 2026, with wider coverage on 14 September, that a group tracked as Red Heron, assessed as China-linked at moderate confidence, automated a public proof-of-concept for CVE-2026-60004 — a critical remote code execution flaw in the self-hosted Gitea code platform, disclosed in July 2026 and fixed in Gitea 1.27.1 — into a Python framework that registers accounts, exploits unpatched servers, steals repositories and cleans up after itself. The framework was pointed at 1,386 Gitea instances and produced confirmed compromises at 11 organisations in five countries — Canada, Argentina, Taiwan, the United States and Sri Lanka, per Acronis’s primary report — spanning defence, elections, energy, aerospace, government and telecommunications. At one Taiwanese victim the group reached root on a three-node Proxmox cluster and installed a custom Linux rootkit, SIXZUT, that patches fifteen kernel functions to hide itself. The telecom relevance is not that carriers run Gitea more than anyone else; it is that a self-hosted code platform inside an operator holds network automation, device configuration templates and the credentials that drive them, and it is the kind of internal service that gets stood up by an engineering team and then falls outside the patch programme. This is exploitation of unpatched instances of a two-month-old flaw, not a zero-day, which makes it a question about your asset inventory rather than about your patch speed. Confirm every Gitea instance you own is on a fixed release, then audit for accounts you did not create and repository clones you did not authorise — patching does not evict an attacker who already holds a valid account.

High Threat Intel

A China-nexus group is living inside Cisco IOS XR routers and TACACS servers, turning carrier-class network gear into a listening post

Sygnia has published incident-response research documenting activity by Fire Ant, a China-nexus intrusion set whose operations the firm assesses as strongly overlapping with UNC3886 while noting differences in filenames, paths and implementation that stop short of conclusive attribution. What makes it matter for this sector is that it is not a CVE. The group moved off the VMware hypervisor estates it was known for and onto the network layer itself: Cisco IOS XR routers, TACACS authentication servers and Linux management hosts. On the routers a custom implant ran behind a fake system service on an alternating schedule to limit its exposure to observation, while a second backdoor, BridgeAgent, presented itself as a legitimate Zabbix monitoring agent and supplied tunnelling and persistence. The operators created GRE tunnel interfaces that left no entry in configuration history and suppressed the syslog messages that would have described them, took interactive shell access without logging, and manipulated file timestamps to frustrate forensics. The routers then became collection platforms, with packet captures written on the device and uploaded to external FTP servers — exposing internal topology, administrative sessions, authentication flows and routing relationships. Sygnia describes a “target behind the target” pattern, in which trusted infrastructure is used to reach the high-value networks that connect to it.

For a carrier or ISP this lands on exactly the assets that usually sit outside the security programme rather than inside it. IOS XR is the operating system of the service-provider core; TACACS decides who may touch it. Compromise both and an adversary holds traffic visibility, credential flow and the authorisation layer at once. The second half of the finding is worse than the first: because the actor suppressed logging, the telemetry an operator would reach for to answer “has this happened to us” is the same telemetry the actor was editing. Sygnia’s guidance is to treat routers, TACACS servers, hypervisors and jump hosts as first-class forensic and Tier-0 assets — export their telemetry to independently administered systems, validate logs against more than one source of evidence, keep out-of-band access that does not traverse the equipment being investigated, and limit the blast radius of trust between connected networks. Sygnia declined to name the victim’s sector or location and has published indicators of compromise, YARA rules and hunting guidance. There is no patch, because no vulnerability was named: this is a hunting exercise, and the absence of a fix to apply is the reason to start it rather than defer it.

Microsoft researchers have detailed at least two initial-access-broker clusters, tracked as Storm-3032 and Storm-3121, running voice-phishing campaigns since May 2026. The reason this belongs on a telecom page is structural rather than sectoral. Carriers, ISPs and cable operators run some of the largest helpdesk and retail-channel workforces in any industry, with high turnover, shift coverage, outsourced tiers and a culture of resetting things quickly for colleagues and customers alike — which is exactly the ground a helpdesk-impersonation call is designed to work. The BYOD step is what defeats the usual controls: nothing touches a managed device, so endpoint detection never sees it, and the subsequent activity is a legitimately-issued token calling a legitimate API. The detections that matter are identity-side, not endpoint-side: new device registrations and passkey or MFA-method additions that follow a helpdesk contact, sign-ins from unmanaged devices, consent grants and unusual Graph API read volume against SharePoint and Exchange, and any authentication against look-alike sign-in domains. The corresponding process control is verification of the caller on the helpdesk side, not just verification of the user.
