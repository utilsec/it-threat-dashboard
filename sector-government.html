Top Story

IDScan confirms an attacker reached customer data on its cloud platform, the FBI is investigating — and the dataset advertised for sale is claimed to hold 153 million driving-licence scans

What is confirmed. IDScan.net, an identity-verification vendor whose document-scanning software sits at car-hire counters, hotel desks, retailers, financial institutions and a range of government-adjacent identity checkpoints, issued a breach notice dated 4 September and by 11 September had confirmed unauthorised access to customer data held on its cloud platform. The FBI is investigating. IDScan says it has locked the platform down and is offering free credit and identity monitoring to affected individuals. What is claimed. A dark-web marketplace known as “Nexus” advertised a searchable service offering more than 153 million driving-licence scans, roughly 10 million other identification cards, more than 3 million travel documents and 579,000 medical cards. That accounting comes from journalist Brian Krebs, working from the leaked dataset, and was not disclosed by IDScan; the company has not said how many customers or how many individuals are affected, and has not verified the scale. The distinction matters and should survive into whatever briefing you write: the intrusion is established, the number is the seller’s own advertisement of it.

Why a private breach is a public-sector problem. The documents involved are government-issued credentials held by a commercial intermediary. Very few agencies contract with an identity-verification vendor directly; they inherit the exposure through a contractor, a licensing partner, a payment processor or a benefits administrator that does, which is why an incident like this rarely shows up in an agency’s own supplier register. The operational consequence is narrow and specific. A great deal of citizen-facing identity proofing — benefits enrolment, licence renewal, records requests, tax account recovery, first-time account creation — still leans on the assumption that a clear photograph of a driving licence is hard for a stranger to obtain. If a bulk corpus of licence images is circulating, that assumption no longer carries weight on its own, and it fails quietly: the fraudulent submission looks better than the genuine one, because it is a clean scan rather than a phone photograph taken in a car park. This is also the week’s second driver-data incident, alongside Florida’s confirmed breach of its DAVID driver database through stolen law-enforcement credentials. Two independent routes to the same category of record in one week is the argument for treating licence images as compromised by default rather than waiting for a victim count.

High Data Breach

Florida confirms its driver database was reached with credentials stolen from a law-enforcement officer’s personal device — no software flaw involved

On 11 September Florida officials confirmed unauthorised access to DAVID, the state’s driver and vehicle information system used by law-enforcement and other authorised agencies. The route in was credentials stolen from a law-enforcement officer’s personal device rather than any vulnerability in the application; reporting by The Record links the intrusion to the ShinyHunters extortion group. The reported impact centres on driver-record data, and the state has not published a final affected-record count. The response so far is credential revocation, a review of multi-factor authentication covering law-enforcement access to driver data, and notification processes now under way. The control lesson sits with the account rather than the system: DAVID behaved exactly as designed for a session it had every reason to trust, which is why no patch closes this and no vulnerability scan would have found it. Any system where an authorised external user’s credential unlocks bulk citizen records — driver data, benefits, court files, tax accounts — needs that credential bound to a managed device and the session watched for query volume, because a lookup rate no human could sustain is usually the only signal the application itself can see. Coming in the same week as the IDScan disclosure, it is the second independent route to driver-licence data in seven days.

The ScreenConnect flaw now has a CVE, a patch and a three-day federal deadline — and a worm-like spread pattern that moves between connected machines

ConnectWise has patched CVE-2026-84869 (CVSS 9.9), a missing-authorisation and improper-privilege-management flaw in ScreenConnect that lets an attacker transfer and execute files across an active remote-support session without the host confirming it. CISA added the flaw to the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog on 12 September and, under Binding Operational Directive 26-04, gave federal civilian agencies three days to remediate. Exploitation has been running since around 20 August. The propagation pattern is what earns the attention: attackers abuse legitimate support sessions to move files, then use social engineering to get a victim to run a modified ScreenConnect client that detects active sessions on the machine and pushes VBScript payloads onto the systems connected to them. That is worm-like movement travelling along a trust relationship rather than a network path. For public-sector bodies the exposure usually arrives through a supplier, because ScreenConnect is heavily used by managed service providers and it is small agencies, districts, county offices and municipalities that outsource endpoint support — so the instance able to reach your machines may not be one you can patch, inventory or even see. Fixed in version 26.6.5; the interim mitigation is to disable the TransferFiles permission.

High Data Breach

Japan’s Digital Agency says a known VPN flaw exposed data on about 240,000 government staff and officials, two months after the intrusion was confirmed

Japan’s Digital Agency said in a notice dated 11 September, widely reported from 14 September, that a third party got in through a known vulnerability in a VPN serving its Government Solution Service (GSS) and reached data on about 240,000 government staff, public officials and people at associated businesses — names, email addresses, telephone numbers and some postal addresses. National identification numbers, bank details and pension numbers were not exposed. The flaw was medium-severity, already disclosed and not a zero-day. Large-scale file access from a maintenance worker’s account was detected on 25 June and the VPN route confirmed on 9 July, two months before the notice. Reporting counts the exposure slightly differently — about 246,000 record rows in one account, about 240,000 people in another — and the agency has not named the VPN product or CVE; no attribution has been made. No misuse has been detected to date. The agency suspended affected accounts, isolated the network, stood up a dedicated support line and issued phishing warnings to those affected. Two things are worth carrying away. First, the flaw was neither novel nor maximum severity, which is the ordinary case: remote-access gear does not need a headline vulnerability to produce a headline exposure, and this lands in the same week as actively exploited authentication bypasses in Citrix NetScaler and Cisco Secure FMC and urgent warnings on Check Point VPN. Second, the data is directory-grade rather than financial, and that is precisely why the agency’s first outbound action was a phishing warning — a verified list of roughly 240,000 public officials with working contact details is the raw material for targeted phishing and impersonation aimed at other government bodies, including yours.

High Ransomware

A Minnesota county paid a $128,000 ransom in January — and a different group hit it again three months later

Winona County, Minnesota has confirmed that it paid $128,539 to recover from a ransomware attack in January 2026, and that a second, separate ransomware attack struck the county in April. County Administrator Maureen Holte said roughly $50,000 of the payment came from insurance and about $78,000 from the county levy — that is, from local taxpayers — and described the decision as taken on advice, to restore services and protect residents’ personal information. For small counties and municipalities the transferable lesson is about the recovery period rather than the payment decision: the months after an incident are the months you are most likely to be hit again, and that is when hardening investment has to land.

A crew tracked as Red Heron automated a Gitea exploit, scanned 1,386 servers and compromised at least 11 organisations across government, defence and election bodies

Acronis Threat Research Unit researchers disclosed on 13 September that a threat actor they call Red Heron has been exploiting CVE-2026-60004, a critical remote code execution flaw in the self-hosted Gitea code forge, since 29 July. The flaw was disclosed in July, is fixed in Gitea 1.27.1 and has been in CISA’s Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog since 25 August (federal deadline 28 August), so confirmed victims were running instances that had not been updated. The actors wrapped a public proof-of-concept in a Python framework that registers an account, exploits the server, steals repositories and clears its traces, then ran it against 1,386 Gitea instances. Sources differ on the count — Acronis reports 11 targets in five countries, The Hacker News 13 organisations in six, adding Qatar — so the safe figure is at least 11 organisations, in the United States, Canada, Taiwan, Argentina and Sri Lanka, possibly also Qatar, across government, defence, election, public-safety, energy, aerospace and telecommunications. In one Taiwanese victim the operators obtained root on a three-node Proxmox cluster and installed a Linux rootkit tracked as SIXZUT that patches 15 kernel functions to stay hidden. Attribution to a China-linked operation is researcher-assessed at moderate confidence, on tooling and infrastructure overlap rather than any official finding. The detail that matters for public-sector estates is where Gitea tends to live: it is the lightweight forge a small team stands up on a spare virtual machine when the central developer platform is too slow to onboard, which means it frequently sits outside the asset register and outside the vulnerability-scanning scope.

Two details are worth carrying into other public-sector estates: the combination of vehicle registration, postcode and travel booking is a strong pattern-of-life data set, and secrets in client-side JavaScript is a failure mode that a code review catches and a perimeter scan does not.
