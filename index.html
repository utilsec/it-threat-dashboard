Top Story

Microsoft has taken down EvilTokens, a phishing service that compromised more than 12,000 Microsoft accounts at over 10,000 organisations — MFA did not stop it, because victims completed the sign-in themselves

Microsoft’s Digital Crimes Unit, working with Health-ISAC, law enforcement and SpyCloud, disrupted the EvilTokens phishing-as-a-service platform on 22 September. Microsoft tracks the operators as Storm-2992. Since February 2026 the service had compromised more than 12,000 Microsoft accounts at more than 10,000 organisations in 79 countries; SpyCloud separately verified 8,708 compromised accounts across 6,585 corporate domains, and 97.5% of victims were on enterprise domains. The United States was hit hardest, followed by Canada, Australia, the United Kingdom and Saudi Arabia. Named sectors are wholesale distribution, construction, financial services, real estate, higher education and healthcare. The technique needs no stolen password and no fake login page. EvilTokens abused Microsoft’s legitimate OAuth 2.0 device-authorization flow: the victim is sent a code and asked to enter it on a genuine Microsoft page, and by completing that sign-in, MFA included, they hand the attacker’s session a valid token. Subscribers paid US$500 a month or US$1,500 once, and the kit included AI features to write lures and sift compromised mailboxes for high-value targets. The Metropolitan Police arrested two men, aged 32 and 38, at addresses in Canary Wharf and Nine Elms; both are on bail. This leads the edition because it is the widest confirmed compromise on the page, across every sector, and because the disruption does not close the hole: a successor service, APToken, has already appeared, and tokens already issued to attackers can keep working until they are revoked. Four exploited edge and management flaws added to KEV on 22 September (Check Point twice, F5 and Arista) sit below it in Critical Advisories. They are serious, but each is a discrete patch-and-hunt job.

High Incident — Supply chain

Brevo’s own embedded scripts were rewritten at the CDN edge to serve ClickFix lures and a WordPress backdoor — Sansec estimates more than 100,000 sites were exposed

Email-marketing platform Brevo, formerly Sendinblue, has confirmed a two-stage compromise that ended with its own customer-embedded scripts serving malware. On 14 September, between about 16:07 and 20:30 UTC, attackers used a long-lived Cloudflare API key with full account permissions that had been hardcoded in Brevo’s application source code to deploy a Cloudflare Worker that rewrote responses at the CDN edge. The Worker altered brevo.com, sendinblue.com, the login and account hosts and sibforms.com form hosting. It also altered the forms, Conversations chat widget and SDK loader scripts that customers embed in their own sites, and it stripped Content-Security-Policy headers along the way. Ordinary visitors to those sites were shown a fake “Cloudflare, verify you are human” ClickFix page telling them to paste and run a command. For visitors logged in as WordPress administrators, the script tried to install a malicious plugin, “Web Media Optimizer”, as a persistent backdoor with a hardcoded authentication bypass. Sansec, which first reported the attack, estimates that more than 100,000 websites were likely exposed; Brevo has not published its own count. Brevo says the key was first misused in late August, but it found no injection into customer-facing pages before 14 September. Its origin servers were never modified, and because the change happened at the edge, “standard integrity checks did not detect the change”. The first stage, on 10 September, was separate. An improperly scoped SAML single sign-on path gave the attacker access to 138 Brevo customer accounts; contacts were exported from 43 of them and phishing was sent from six. It reached every organisation that pasted the vendor’s script tag into its site: retailers, charities, schools and law firms alike. Sansec’s write-up, last revised on 17 September, puts the exposure at 100,000 or more sites; Brevo has not published a customer count. The story led the previous two editions and moves here now that it is contained and a wider confirmed campaign has taken the lead. Brevo has revoked the key, removed the hardcoded credentials, deleted the attacker’s hostnames and purged its edge caches. It cannot clean the WordPress sites that received the plugin, or the computers of visitors who ran the command.

The FBI’s Internet Crime Complaint Center issued a public service announcement on 18 September on government-impersonation fraud: about 61,000 complaints between January 2025 and July 2026, with losses above US$1.6 billion, an average of roughly US$26,000 each. Fake criminal-charge threats dominate. Jury-duty variants accounted for about 11 per cent (6,833 complaints, about US$36 million); a professional-licence variant, in which callers tell doctors and lawyers their licence is expiring or has been misused, produced 3,322 complaints and more than US$37 million; and “international targeting”, where callers on video from mock government offices threaten foreign nationals and international students with passport cancellation or deportation, was under 3 per cent of complaints but about US$140 million, roughly a tenth of losses. The FBI’s message is simple: no law-enforcement agency will call and demand payment. The PSA number was not captured for this edition and should be checked on ic3.gov. It is on an enterprise page because the victims are employees and the losses arrive at work: a clinician or associate on the phone with a “licensing board”, an international hire told to move money to avoid deportation, and, in the professional-licence case, a pretext that leads naturally to the victim’s work credentials.

One extradition and two sentences in a week: alleged Black Axe leaders sent from South Africa to New Jersey, a Conti developer jailed for four years, and a LockerGoga and Nefilim developer given nearly 13 years in Zurich

Five alleged leaders of the Cape Town cell of Black Axe, Perry Osagiede, Franklyn Edosa Osagiede, Osariemen Eric Clement, Collins Owhofasa Otughwor and Musa Mudashiru, all Nigerian nationals arrested in South Africa in 2021, were extradited to the United States in mid-September and have made their first appearances in federal court in Trenton, New Jersey, CyberScoop reported. They are charged with wire-fraud and money-laundering conspiracy over romance and advance-fee scams against US victims running since at least 2011; FBI Newark and the Secret Service led the case, and no fraud total was given. Oleksii Lytvynenko, 44, a Ukrainian who built a malware loader for the Conti ransomware operation from September 2021, held stolen victim data and kept attacking after Conti’s 2022 shutdown, was sentenced on 11 September to four years in US prison for wire fraud, after arrest in Ireland in 2023, extradition in late 2025 and a guilty plea in June. And on 15 September the Zurich District Court sentenced a 52-year-old Ukrainian developer, held since October 2021, to twelve years and nine months for his role in LockerGoga, MegaCortex and Nefilim attacks, including those on Stadler Rail, Meier Tobler and Crealogix, covering more than 1,800 victims in 71 countries; the court found he was not the leader, the verdict can be appealed, and the alleged leader Volodymyr Tymoshchuk remains at large with a US$11 million US reward. None of this changes your exposure this week. It does show, alongside the NightmareStresser seizure, that the pipeline from arrest to sentence is running, and the Conti case in particular that a developer who never touched a victim network is being held to the loader he wrote.
