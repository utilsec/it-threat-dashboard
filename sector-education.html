Citrix patches a fourth critical NetScaler SAML flaw; campuses that fixed the exploited zero-days may need to upgrade again

Citrix bulletin CTX697191, public on 8 October, fixes CVE-2026-107406, a memory overflow rated CVSS v4.0 9.5 in NetScaler ADC and Gateway appliances configured as a SAML service provider or identity provider; Citrix says it may allow code execution. It is not reported exploited and was not in KEV as of the 15 October catalog. The catch is that builds which fixed the exploited trio — CVE-2026-88771 and -88772 (KEV 27 September) and the SAML denial-of-service flaw CVE-2026-88779 (KEV 4 October) — stay vulnerable on appliances acting as a SAML identity provider. On 12 October CISA urged customers to patch again and warned that 88779 is being used to force reboots so that code planted through 88771 runs. Mandiant names education among the sectors hit by the earlier zero-days; no school or university has been named. Campuses that federate sign-in through NetScaler should upgrade and hunt (editorial assessment).

ShinyHunters: the FBI announces another arrest, and its own breach is reported to trace to an unapplied Oracle PeopleSoft patch — the CVE-2026-35273 flaw the group used mainly against academic institutions in June and is again exploiting past WAF rules

Update, 9 October 2026: FBI Director Kash Patel announced another arrest in the ShinyHunters investigation, describing the group as the one believed responsible for the FBIjobs.gov incident. The New York Times and CBS News, citing unnamed sources, report that the suspect is a Canadian citizen arrested in Pennsylvania; no name or charges have been made public. The FBI says the group has breached more than 140 organisations and collected at least $70 million in extortion payments since last year. A 24-year-old was arrested in Amsterdam on 15 September with FBI support, and Reuters’ sources report a further arrest in Jordan on 29 September; this page names none of the suspects. 6 October: FBI Cyber Division Assistant Director Brett Leatherman said the jobs-portal breach came from a security failure on a platform run by a third-party organisation, after a contractor did not apply a patch issued specifically for it, and the FBI has removed the contractor. The FBI has not named the product or a CVE. Reuters, citing sources, reports that the platform was Oracle PeopleSoft, and The Hacker News ties the breach to CVE-2026-35273 and the WAF bypass Mandiant described; treat that link as reported, not confirmed. The campaign itself is confirmed and unchanged. On 25 September Google Threat Intelligence Group and Mandiant reported renewed mass exploitation by UNC6240, the group known as ShinyHunters, of the Environment Management Hub (PSEMHUB) in PeopleSoft PeopleTools. The flaw is an unauthenticated remote code execution bug that the group exploited as a zero-day between 27 May and 9 June 2026, predominantly against academic institutions; Oracle fixed it out of band on 10 June. The new trick is requesting /%50SEMHUB/ instead of /PSEMHUB/: a WAF rule that matches the literal path does not fire, while WebLogic decodes the path and serves the application anyway. Mandiant found web shells on dozens of systems in higher education, technology, IT services, healthcare, agriculture, transportation and government, along with Neo-reGeorg tunnels, a SIDEEYE backdoor hidden in a trojanised Light Alloy installer and the MeshAgent remote-management agent. No victims are named. CVE-2026-35273 has been in CISA’s KEV catalogue since 12 June 2026, due 15 June. The FBI case is the lesson for campuses: the exposure was a fix that existed and was never applied, on a platform someone else operated. Many universities run PeopleSoft Campus Solutions, HCM or Financials through a hosting or managed-service partner, and Mandiant’s hunting guidance points at HR, payroll and student-records tables (editorial assessment of the campus exposure).

High Campaign

The FBI and Secret Service say the FortiBleed campaign against FortiGate firewalls and SSL VPNs is still active — there is no CVE to patch, because it runs on leaked and reused credentials

On 6 October 2026 the FBI and the US Secret Service issued a joint advisory (JCSA-20261006-01) warning that attackers are still working through internet-facing Fortinet FortiGate firewalls and SSL VPN gateways with credentials obtained earlier. The agencies tie the campaign to reused or leaked passwords and legacy SHA-256 password storage; researchers describe password spraying and stuffing from leak dumps, offline cracking of extracted hashes and a Go-based sniffer that passively captures authentication traffic. Some owners were locked out after attackers created their own admin accounts and changed or deleted legitimate ones. Access is resold, and the FBI links it to ransomware intrusions, including Payload. The agencies say targets span all 16 US critical-infrastructure sectors. On the numbers: SOCRadar’s figure of more than 86,644 working device credentials across 194 countries dates from 19 June 2026, when SOCRadar and Hudson Rock first documented the campaign; it is not a new count. SOCRadar also says it has confirmed at least 12 ransomware deployments that began with this access. Descriptions of the operator as a Russian-speaking access broker with links to INC and Lynx are researchers’ assessments, not the agencies’. There is no FortiBleed CVE. FortiGate is a common district and campus firewall, and a VPN account that a contractor or former member of staff never lost is exactly the kind of credential this campaign uses (editorial assessment).

Medium Extortion

Slate Valley Unified in Vermont lost internet, Windows sign-in and its PowerSchool instance for about a week after a September attack — the district believes teacher data was taken, and a group calling itself Kairos is demanding a ransom

VTDigger reported on 2 October 2026 that Slate Valley Unified School District found out about the attack on 3 September, when teachers arriving for the day could not sign in to Windows. The district was without internet access and internal platforms, including its own PowerSchool grading system, for about a week until outside contractors restored them. Superintendent Brooke Olsen-Farrell called the attackers’ follow-up email an extortion attempt. The district believes personal information of current and former teachers was compromised and has hired a consultant to identify and notify each person affected. What is claimed, not confirmed: a group calling itself Kairos says it holds data on more than 1,500 employees, including Social Security numbers and home addresses, and wants hundreds of thousands of dollars; the district has not confirmed those figures. The board says it has not paid, and the FBI, which is investigating, advised against paying. Similar messages reached staff and community members, and the Vermont Agency of Education told recipients not to reply, click links or open attachments. This was the district’s own PowerSchool instance going offline, not a breach of the vendor. The pattern to note is the second wave: once systems are back, the extortion moves into staff and community inboxes (editorial assessment).

Medium Breach

Bromcom, whose school MIS is used by more than 5,000 UK schools, says an intruder took email addresses and sign-in records from a legacy single sign-on component it had left running — not passwords, and not the MIS itself

The Register reported on 6 October 2026 that Bromcom has notified customers of a personal data breach in legacy single sign-on registration functionality in its Communication Server environment. Bromcom identified the incident on 6 September after reports of SSO access problems, and a Bromcom account disclosed the unauthorised access on the EduGeek forum on 24 September. According to Bromcom, the data taken was limited to email addresses tied to SSO registrations, which provider each account used (Microsoft or Google), registration and last sign-in dates where recorded, and internal user and registration reference numbers. Bromcom says the component held no passwords or authentication tokens, that Microsoft and Google accounts could not be reached through it, and that it has found no evidence its management information system, which holds student, attendance and behaviour data, was compromised. The superseded component had stayed in production because an internal system still called it; it has now been withdrawn, and external forensic specialists are establishing the full scope. No actor has been named and no count of affected schools or users has been given. Bromcom’s customers include more than 5,000 schools and 390 multi-academy trusts. The data is modest, but a list of real staff and pupil email addresses with their identity provider is what a convincing “re-register your sign-in” phishing lure needs (editorial assessment).

High Breach

The Technical University of Denmark says attackers used compromised credentials to download data from its identity and access management system — up to 200,000 current and former staff, students, guests and partners going back to 2003, with national ID numbers for current users

The Technical University of Denmark (DTU) disclosed on 3 October 2026 that attackers used compromised credentials to get into DTUBasen, its identity and access management system, and download data. Up to 200,000 people may be affected — about 40,000 active and about 160,000 former users — covering anyone who has been an employee, student, guest or external partner since 2003. For current users the data includes Danish civil registration (CPR) numbers, names, home addresses and profile photos, work email, job title and office location, and next-of-kin names, relationships and phone numbers. For former users, DTU says home addresses, photos and next-of-kin details are deleted automatically six months after they leave, which narrows what it still held about them. DTU is notifying people through e-Boks, Denmark’s official digital mailbox; students without a CPR number are not notified directly. University Director Bjarke Bak Christensen called it a serious attack on the university. What is not known: DTU has not said whose credentials were used or how they were obtained, when the access took place, or whether MFA was in place, and no attacker or ransom demand has been reported. It is separate from the misuse of lookup access to the national CPR register that a Danish ministry disclosed on 5 October. It matters because an identity system is the one place a university keeps a record of everyone it has ever let in, and two decades of former users is the retention tail that turns a single credential compromise into a population-scale breach (editorial assessment).

High Incident

Elsevier’s Evolve, Sherpath, ClinicalPharmacology and Gold Standard Drug Database platforms redirected visitors to LAPSUS$-branded extortion pages — Elsevier confirms the redirect, says it found no sign data was taken, and nursing programmes felt it first

On 21 September 2026 visitors to several Elsevier platforms were sent to extortion “splash” pages on the domains lapsus[.]ar[.]io and lapsus[.]bz. The platforms named are Evolve and Sherpath — the courseware, assignment and simulation systems that nursing and health-science programmes use for coursework and exams — together with ClinicalPharmacology and the Gold Standard Drug Database (GSDD). DataBreaches.net, crediting Sorami Consulting with first reporting it, says production API token requests to api.gsdd.net/auth/AccessToken were redirected as well, so applications that authenticate to the drug-database API were pointed at the attacker’s page too. Elsevier has confirmed the core fact. Its statement says that on 21 September it “identified that visitors to select platforms were being redirected to a third-party page”, that its team resolved the issue and restored normal service, that this was “a narrowly scoped, limited-duration event involving the temporary redirection of traffic for certain web properties”, and that there is “no indication that core platforms, customer data, research content, or operational systems were compromised.” Read the rest with its sourcing in mind. The report that nursing and medical students were locked out of exams and simulation charting comes from a single researcher write-up describing public Reddit discussion; Elsevier has not described student impact, and there is no count of institutions or learners affected. How the redirect was achieved has not been disclosed. The LAPSUS$ name is on the pages; no investigator has attributed the event to the original LAPSUS$ group, and this page does not. As it stands this is an integrity and availability incident at one supplier, not a confirmed data breach, and no further detail from Elsevier had been published by 25 September. It matters because of where it landed. A single courseware vendor sits under a large share of nursing and allied-health programmes at once, so a few hours of hijacked traffic becomes a missed exam window in many institutions simultaneously, and a redirected login or token endpoint is exactly where a phishing page would sit if the next attacker were quieter.

Self-hosted Nextcloud servers running Collabora’s built-in CODE server were backdoored through an unauthenticated command injection — exploited since September, fixed in 26.4.303, and disabling the app is not enough

The Hacker News reported on 14 October 2026 that attackers have been exploiting CVE-2026-110447 in the Collabora Online Built-in CODE Server, the optional richdocumentscode app that runs a document editor inside Nextcloud. Content from the request path was passed into a bash command, so an unauthenticated request could run commands while the local document server was down, for example during a restart, an upgrade or a crash. The scale is unknown and no root access has been reported. The fix, 26.4.303, shipped on 11 September and needs Nextcloud 30 or later; disabling the app does not remove the bug, so upgrade or uninstall it. Standalone Collabora Online and richdocuments-only installs are not affected, and the flaw is not in KEV. Universities and research groups often run self-hosted Nextcloud as an in-house alternative to commercial file sharing (editorial assessment).
