Greenberg Traurig files breach notices after Silent Ransom Group got in through a corporate iPad and leaked the data when talks failed

Greenberg Traurig has submitted a draft breach notification to the California Attorney General, dated 9 September, confirming that Social Security numbers and other personal information were involved. It told Vermont’s Attorney General that 10 residents are affected. According to DataBreaches.net, Silent Ransom Group got in through a corporate iPad starting 13 August, and the firm detected the intrusion on 26 August. The firm let several negotiation deadlines pass, and the group released the data on 2 September. DataBreaches.net’s analysis of the leaked files counts roughly 126,000 unique people with Social Security numbers. Most of them come from spreadsheets of class-action member information, and the outlet says the figure may be an underestimate. The notices do not give a total, so treat 126,000 as reported, not official. The exposed data includes names, Social Security numbers, dates of birth, addresses and phone numbers. Some files also hold medical records, tax documents, passport copies and employee job titles and hourly rates. The firm says its systems were not compromised or breached, and that an unauthorised actor accessed “a limited number of documents”. The group also claimed it watched the firm’s IT team discover the breach, but DataBreaches.net could not verify that. The campaign described further down this page now has a named victim at a large firm, and a further week of checking has produced no new filing, no official count and no firm statement, so it stays the sector’s most recent confirmed breach. Two lessons stand out. First, the reported way in was a mobile device, so tablets and phones need the same management and monitoring as laptops. Second, class-action member lists hold a lot of third-party personal data, and firms often keep them in spreadsheets outside any matter system. The immediate checks: confirm every firm-issued iPad and phone is enrolled in device management and covered by conditional access, review 60 days of mobile sign-in and file-access logs, and find the class-action, settlement and other bulk personal-data spreadsheets sitting outside the document management system.

The Ministry of Justice apologises after court and probation staff looked at Southport victims’ files without authorisation — an insider-access breach inside the courts, now with the ICO

The UK Ministry of Justice said on 16 September that staff in HM Courts and Tribunals Service and HM Prison and Probation Service had accessed records relating to victims and survivors of the July 2024 Southport attack without authorisation. For a limited number of individuals the material included sensitive personal data the department assessed as “likely to pose a high risk to their rights and freedoms”; the MoJ says it has found no evidence the information was shared outside the organisations. The Information Commissioner’s Office has been notified, both agencies are investigating, and the Prime Minister has asked the Lord Chancellor to oversee the investigation. The department has not said how many staff were involved. In its own words: “We are appalled that this happened and recognise the distress it will have caused victims, survivors, and their families.” It follows earlier reports of similar unauthorised access to the same victims’ records at NHS and emergency-service bodies. This is not an attack: people with legitimate accounts opened files they had no business reason to open, on a case with intense public interest, and every access looked like a permitted user doing their job. For firms the read-across is direct. Matter files for a high-profile client or a case in the news attract curious staff, a document management system that gives every fee-earner read access to every matter cannot tell curiosity from need, and ethical screens are built for conflicts, not curiosity.

Forged legal requests sent from a genuine ministry mailbox pried passport and financial data out of Revolut for months — the sender check passed because the account was real

Revolut disclosed on 14 September that “an unauthorized third party utilized a legitimate government agency domain email to submit fraudulent requests for information”. Reporting on 17–18 September filled in how. According to SecurityWeek, citing infostealer researchers Hudson Rock, an infostealer compromised the email account of an employee of Italy’s Ministry of the Interior on its certified-mail (PEC) domain; Hudson Rock found around 300 compromised logins for that domain in infostealer logs. The account was used to send fraudulent government data requests to Revolut Bank UAB in Lithuania — which American Banker says was apparently chosen because EU law obliges it to answer European Investigation Orders — over roughly five to six months. About 680 high-net-worth customers are reported affected, none of them in the US, and the data included passport details, contact details and financial information. Those figures come from researchers and reporting, not from Revolut, which still says only “a limited number of customers” and has notified regulators and law enforcement. An actor calling itself “IAmNotAVillain” posted a $3 million ransom demand on 16 September; Revolut says it has received no direct contact or demand. The actor also claims 147 GB from an Italian law-enforcement agency, which Italian police are investigating. Update 20 September: American Banker’s updated report carries no new statement from Revolut or any regulator; it adds analysis that US banks lack standardised controls for verifying legal data requests and that the compliance teams answering them often sit outside security. Update 21 September: Infosecurity, citing Malwarebytes, reports a wave of SMS phishing against Revolut customers that began around 14 September, within days of the bank’s first acknowledgement. The pages ask for camera access and mimic Revolut’s video identity check before requesting the password; several hundred accounts are believed affected, with high-net-worth cryptocurrency holders singled out after blockchain analysis. Malwarebytes links the targeting to data from the same breach — that is the researcher’s assessment, not a Revolut statement. This belongs on a legal page because production orders, subpoenas and law-enforcement data requests pass through firms constantly — received for clients, advised on and answered. The lesson is that sender authentication proves only which mailbox sent a message, not that the person behind it had authority, and the pressure to comply quickly is exactly what the attacker relies on.

A hijacked Brevo CDN key turned embedded sign-up forms and chat widgets into a malware channel for more than four hours — check the firm website if it uses them

Email-marketing platform Brevo (formerly Sendinblue) has confirmed a two-stage compromise first reported by e-commerce security firm Sansec on 16 September. Attackers obtained a long-lived Cloudflare API key with full account permissions that had been hardcoded in Brevo’s application source code, and used it to deploy a Cloudflare Worker that rewrote content at the CDN edge on 14 September, from about 16:07 to 20:30 UTC. Affected assets included brevo.com, sendinblue.com, the sibforms.com form host, the login and account portals, and the forms, Conversations chat widget and SDK loader scripts that customers embed on their own websites. The Worker also stripped Content-Security-Policy headers. Brevo says that because it acted at the edge, origin servers and files were unmodified and standard integrity checks did not detect the change. Ordinary visitors to affected sites were shown a fake “Cloudflare, verify you are human” page telling them to paste and run a command — the ClickFix technique. Visitors with a live WordPress administrator session had the script try to install a malicious plugin called “Web Media Optimizer” as a persistent backdoor. Sansec estimates more than 100,000 sites were exposed. Earlier, on 10 September, attackers had abused an improperly scoped SAML single sign-on path to reach 138 Brevo customer accounts, exporting contacts from 43 and sending phishing from six. No law firm has been named. The exposure for a firm is its own website: if a marketing page, newsletter or client-alert sign-up embeds Brevo forms, chat or tracking scripts, it served this code to visitors in the window, and anyone logged in to its WordPress admin at the time may have left behind a backdoored site. Scripts that marketing added are easy to miss on an IT asset register.

Still the sector’s defining campaign: Silent Ransom Group works law firms by phone, and sometimes by walking through the front door

Carried forward, and now with a named victim: on 14 September DataBreaches.net reported that this group breached Greenberg Traurig and leaked the data after talks failed (see the Top Story). The firm has filed notices with state regulators. This is still the most likely way a firm actually gets hit. The group tracked as Silent Ransom Group, Luna Moth, Chatty Spider and UNC3753 has been active since 2022 and targeting law firms since spring 2023. An FBI FLASH alert issued in May 2026 and Google Mandiant’s June 2026 reporting on a targeted campaign against US law firms both describe the operation as still running; no update to either has appeared since. The tradecraft is almost entirely human. Operators call or email posing as IT support, talk a partner, paralegal or billing clerk into a remote-support session, and install a legitimate remote monitoring and management tool. Exfiltration runs over WinSCP or a hidden or renamed copy of Rclone, with data pushed to Google Drive, Microsoft OneDrive, external hard drives or USB storage. There is frequently no encryption stage at all — the leverage is the client material itself, which in a firm means privileged files that cannot be restored away. The outlier detail, and the one that breaks most legal threat models, is that operators have turned up in person, claiming they need to image a device or take a backup to deal with the effects of a phishing email. Halcyon’s research centre ranked law firms the fourth most-targeted industry in early 2026, attributing it to the sensitivity of client data, regulatory pressure to resolve incidents quickly and a perceived willingness to pay. Read this alongside the ScreenConnect card further down the page: the remote-support tooling this campaign relies on being opened for it is the same class of tool now being exploited in worm-like attacks, patched and KEV-listed. The FBI’s own recommendations are unglamorous and correct: verify visitor identity against ID, deploy phishing-resistant multi-factor authentication, restrict remote access and external-drive permissions on sensitive systems, and train staff to recognise the pretext.

Microsoft takes down EvilTokens, a device-code phishing service that compromised more than 12,000 Microsoft 365 accounts at over 10,000 organisations despite MFA — two arrests in London

Microsoft’s Digital Crimes Unit, working with Health-ISAC, law enforcement and SpyCloud, disrupted the EvilTokens phishing-as-a-service platform on 22 September. BleepingComputer reports that the service had compromised more than 12,000 Microsoft accounts at over 10,000 organisations. SpyCloud separately counted 8,708 compromised accounts across 6,585 corporate email domains in 79 countries. The Metropolitan Police arrested two men, aged 32 and 38, and released them on bail pending investigation. EvilTokens emerged in February 2026 and sold access for $500 a month or a one-time $1,500. It “specializes in device-code phishing, a technique that abuses the device-code authentication flow to obtain authentication tokens despite MFA protections”. The victim is sent to Microsoft’s real sign-in page and asked to enter a short code. That approves a session on the attacker’s device, the user completes MFA as normal, and the attacker receives the tokens. Nothing is spoofed, which is why it gets past users who have been trained to check the address bar. The sectors named are wholesale distribution, construction, financial services, real estate, higher education and healthcare. No law firm is named as a victim, and the takedown removes this operator, not the technique. It belongs here because firms are almost uniformly Microsoft 365 tenants, and real-estate and financial-services clients sit in the named sectors. It is also the third route this month to the same token, after the help-desk vishing and passkey-enrolment campaigns further down. The control that stops it is unusually simple: few firms have any legitimate need for the device-code flow.

CISA and NIST set out how cloud identity tokens should be protected — one-hour lifetimes and 90-day key rotation give firms concrete questions for every SaaS supplier

CISA and NIST have published NIST IR 8587, joint guidance on protecting the identity tokens and signing keys that cloud services use to authenticate users and applications. The recommendations are specific: access and identity tokens valid for no more than an hour, with expired tokens rejected outright; signing keys rotated every 90 days for high-impact systems and at least annually for others; keys held in hardware-backed or isolated storage, with signing done in isolated environments for high-impact systems; a mandatory audience claim, so a token issued for one service cannot be replayed against another; and tokens never written to logs. The report cites two precedents — the 2020 SAML-forgery supply-chain attack and the incident in which forged tokens exposed more than 60,000 government emails. It is voluntary and aimed at federal agencies, cloud providers and the organisations that buy cloud services. Firms are in the last group, and this page shows why it matters. Nearly every campaign on it this month — the Storm-3032 help-desk crews, the passkey-enrolment abuse, the Revolut mailbox takeover — ends with the attacker holding a stolen session token or a live account rather than a password. How long that token stays valid decides whether a phish becomes weeks of quiet access to a matter mailbox. Token lifetime is partly the firm’s own configuration and partly the supplier’s design, so the guidance is useful on both sides of the contract.

The EU Cyber Resilience Act’s 24-hour reporting duty is now live — for most firms the obligation is a client’s, and the first call will arrive well inside the clock

The incident-reporting provisions of the EU Cyber Resilience Act took effect on 11–12 September 2026. Manufacturers of products with digital elements placed on the EU market must now notify actively exploited vulnerabilities and serious security incidents within a 24-hour window. For the typical law firm the direct obligation is nil — a firm is not a manufacturer of products with digital elements — and the temptation is to file this as a client matter and move on. Two things argue against that. The first is the nature of a 24-hour clock: it is a fact-finding deadline as much as a filing deadline, and it runs from awareness, not from the completion of forensics. A client that discovers an exploited vulnerability in a connected product on a Friday evening will call counsel inside the first few hours, needing to know what triggers notification, what an initial notification has to contain when almost nothing is yet known, and how what is said at hour four constrains what can be said at week four. That is a view a firm wants settled before the call rather than during it. The second is that parts of this sector are in scope directly: consultancies with product arms, and the legal-technology businesses that firms own, incubate or hold stakes in, place software on the EU market in their own right. Third-order, and quietly useful: products the firm buys will begin carrying CRA obligations, which gives procurement a concrete question to ask suppliers about disclosure timelines instead of a general one about security posture. ENISA has confirmed the CRA Single Reporting Platform went live on 11 September, routing reports automatically to national CSIRTs; obligations for open-source software stewards follow from 11 December 2027.
