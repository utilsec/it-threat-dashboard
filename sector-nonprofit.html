Two Belgian sports federations confirm intrusions while a hacker claims more than 260,000 member records — the membership database is the one asset a volunteer-run federation cannot afford to lose

Two separate intrusions at Belgian sports federations came to light on 21 September, reported by The Record. The French-speaking table tennis federation (AFTT) and the Royal Belgian Table Tennis Federation discovered a breach on Thursday 18 September; a hacker using the handle “Venus1337” claims to hold about 1.7 GB covering more than 66,800 members and 17,400 user accounts — names, membership numbers, sanction records and club administrator accounts. Days earlier, the French-speaking Gymnastics Federation (FfG) confirmed unauthorised access to its systems; the same actor claims more than 200,000 gymnastics members’ names, addresses, phone numbers, coaching qualifications, judges’ licences and accident reports. What is confirmed and what is claimed need separating. Both federations acknowledge unauthorised access. AFTT says its IT department is investigating and has found no evidence that its own infrastructure was compromised; FfG says the access route “was neutralised”. The record counts, the archive size and the data types are the attacker’s assertions, and neither federation has confirmed them. No ransomware was deployed and no extortion demand has been reported. It leads this page because it is the sector’s own shape: a federation is a nonprofit whose entire value to its clubs is a membership register, usually held on a licensing platform chosen years ago, administered by volunteers at club level with accounts that are rarely reviewed, and containing data on children, officials and injuries that is far more sensitive than a mailing list. A membership register is also a ready-made target list for phishing every club secretary in the country.

A leaked cloud key let attackers rewrite Brevo’s own scripts for four hours — the sign-up forms and chat widgets on charity websites were the delivery route, and the hunt for what was planted is still yours

Brevo, the email-marketing platform formerly called Sendinblue and a common choice for supporter newsletters and sign-up forms, confirmed that attackers served malicious code through its content-delivery network on 14 September. According to Brevo and to e-commerce security firm Sansec, which disclosed the attack on 16 September, the attackers found a long-lived Cloudflare API key with full account permissions hardcoded in Brevo’s source code and used it to create a malicious Cloudflare Worker that rewrote responses at the network edge. For roughly four hours (Brevo gives 16:07–20:30 UTC), the sdk-loader.js and brevo-conversations.js scripts that websites embed pulled in code from attacker-controlled subdomains of sendibt1.com, across Brevo assets including sibforms.com, where its forms are hosted. Sansec puts the number of exposed sites above 100,000. Ordinary visitors were shown a fake “verify you are human” page telling them to paste and run a command — the ClickFix technique. Visitors logged in to WordPress as an administrator had the script try to install a plugin called “Web Media Optimizer” as a persistent backdoor. A linked incident on 10 September gave attackers access to 138 Brevo customer accounts through a mis-scoped single sign-on path. Status on 22 September: no new development was found in the week since disclosure — no attribution, no count of backdoored sites and no regulator action — and the 100,000+ figure remains Sansec’s estimate. The window is closed; what remains is checking whether anything landed in it. The lesson for this sector holds: a third-party script on your donation or sign-up page runs with your site’s authority, and the person most likely to browse it while logged in as administrator is your own web volunteer.

Ransomware hit the vendor-run payroll system behind 48 Singapore mosques and four madrasahs — staff bank details were in it, and salaries still went out

The Islamic Religious Council of Singapore (MUIS) has confirmed a ransomware incident affecting SmartHRMS, a human-resources and payroll system operated by the vendor Avelogic, after threat activity was detected on 30–31 August. Those affected are 48 mosques, four madrasahs, the Islamic Learning Hub and Management Office, and Mosque-Madrasah-Wakaf Shared Services. The system held staff names, contact details, salaries and bank account numbers, and the data in it was encrypted. MUIS said on 17 September that there is no evidence a large amount of data was taken from the system, that no ransom was paid, that a police report has been made, and that alternative payroll arrangements mean salaries were not disrupted; the Cyber Security Agency of Singapore is aware and has offered assistance. Avelogic’s own incident notice, last updated 14 September, did not name its customers, and no ransomware group has been identified or has claimed the attack. The shape is the familiar one for faith organisations: congregations rarely run their own HR systems, so a shared-services body or a sector vendor runs one for dozens of them, and one intrusion at the vendor reaches all of them at once. What MUIS evidently had ready is what most small organisations have never tested — a way to pay people when the payroll system is gone. What remains is that bank details were in the system, and “no evidence of large exfiltration” is not the same as none, so payment-diversion and impersonation fraud aimed at those staff is the realistic follow-on risk.

Confirmed — MUIS confirmed the ransomware incident and the affected entities; large-scale data theft not confirmed (MUIS: no evidence); no group named or claiming No CVE — the vendor has not disclosed how the attackers got in
No patch applies — vendor assurance, payroll continuity and staff fraud warnings Action: Find out which vendor holds your payroll and HR data, and whether that system is shared with sister congregations, a diocese, a federation or a shared-services body. Ask the vendor in writing how and how quickly it would tell you about an incident, and whether it keeps offline backups it has actually restored from. Write down how you would pay staff for one payroll cycle if the system disappeared tomorrow: last month’s figures held offline, a bank payment template, and who is authorised to approve it. If a provider holding your staff’s bank details reports an incident, warn staff directly that fraudsters may contact them using those details, and make it a rule that any change to an employee’s bank account is confirmed by a call to a number already on file, never by email.

A WordPress admin plugin was backdoored through its own update channel — sites that updated on 14 September may now have a hidden administrator

The developer of Admin Menu Editor Pro, a premium WordPress plugin, published an incident notice on 14 September after an attacker gained access to the server hosting adminmenueditor.com and pushed a malicious version 2.35 through the plugin’s normal update mechanism, roughly between 06:00 and 13:00 UTC. The replacement version 2.36, released later that day, was also compromised. The backdoor drops a web shell at includes/wp-user-consent.php, creates a /wp-content/object-cache/ directory and wp_ocache database options, and adds a hidden administrator account whose username begins “wp_”. The vendor counts about 230 customers affected and around 300 more possibly affected; BleepingComputer, citing the developer’s analysis of update-server logs, puts the number of sites that received a malicious build at about 1,500, since customers run several sites each. Version 2.34 is confirmed clean. Updated 19 September: a clean version 2.37, built from 2.34, has been released through a temporary download that checks your licence key, while adminmenueditor.com is offline (still showing a “temporarily offline” notice on 22 September) and its servers are rebuilt on new infrastructure; the investigation is ongoing, and sites still on 2.34 do not need to update. There is no CVE because nothing in the plugin’s code was the weakness — the vendor’s update channel was. It belongs on this page because a WordPress site built by an agency or a volunteer and left to update itself is a common arrangement for small organisations, and it is the site that carries the donation page and the supporter sign-up form. An automatic update that lands overnight is exactly the change nobody inside the organisation sees.

The Magento zero-day backdooring online stores now has a CVE, an emergency patch and a place in CISA’s KEV catalog

Sansec, the Dutch e-commerce forensics firm, reported on 5 September that a previously unknown flaw it names StyleSmuggler is being used to plant backdoors in Magento Open Source and Adobe Commerce stores. Sansec describes it as giving an unauthenticated attacker remote code execution with no user interaction, delivered in two stages: PHP code is smuggled in through the platform, then executed by triggering a built-in scheduled email job. The implant that follows disguises itself as a system process, re-establishes itself through cron, and reads session data. Sansec observed exploitation from 4 September. The position has since changed for the better: the flaw is now tracked as CVE-2026-75650 at CVSS 10.0, affecting all versions of Adobe Commerce and Magento Open Source, Adobe has shipped an out-of-band patch and confirmed exploitation in the wild against merchants, and CISA added it to the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog on 8 September. Attackers used it to deploy a Rust backdoor and a PHP web shell; the injection path abuses Magento’s template system through a “Payment Transaction Failed Reminder” email. The same Adobe release fixed over 170 vulnerabilities. CrowdSec telemetry published on 14 September counted 2,760 exploit signals between 9 and 13 September, peaking at 1,303 on 11 September, from some 500 unique attacking addresses; volume has since halved but still runs more than ten times the first day’s level, and there is still no published count of compromised stores. Nonprofits reach Magento by a predictable route — a charity shop moved online, merchandise, Christmas cards, event ticketing, membership fulfilment — usually built years ago by an agency that is no longer on retainer, which means nobody is watching for an advisory that has not been written yet. If your store takes card payments, the exposure is not only your own data.

Attackers are taking over MikroTik routers through internet-facing SSH — the cheapest capable box in the sector

CERT Polska, Poland’s national CERT, published confirmation on 5 September that vulnerabilities it disclosed in MikroTik RouterOS are being exploited in the wild, chained into an attack it calls MikroTrick. The chain gives an unauthenticated attacker administrative control of a router whose SSH service is reachable from the internet: a flaw in how RouterOS verified the public key presented during SSH authentication provides the entry point, and improper handling of disallowed characters in the username then escalates that session to full administrator. A third issue affects the bandwidth-test service. CERT Polska reports attacks observed since at least 2 September. Updated: CISA added CVE-2026-86060 (argument injection) to the KEV catalog on 10 September with a 13 September federal deadline, which has passed. CVE-2026-67277, the bandwidth-test memory-disclosure flaw, was added to KEV the same day with the same deadline; CVE-2026-67276, the signature-verification half of the chain, is not itself a KEV entry. MikroTik lists fixes in RouterOS 7.24.2, 7.23.4 and 6.49.21, with 7.24.3 and 7.23.6 also covering CVE-2026-67278 according to CERT Polska, and says the upgrade runs a compromise check that writes a critical log entry if the device has been tampered with. This matters to nonprofits not because MikroTik is glamorous but because of who owns one. It is the router that ends up in small charities, community organisations, faith groups, hospices and overseas field offices precisely because it does serious routing for very little money — frequently installed once by a volunteer, a trustee’s relative or a contractor who has long since moved on, and then never touched again. The exposure is not a misconfiguration you made; it is a device nobody has logged into for years with management left open to the internet. If the router falls, everything behind it is reachable and every DNS lookup on your network is the attacker’s to steer.

UK, US and Dutch agencies warn that Iranian spyware is being planted on dissidents’ and journalists’ computers through fake tech support and fake apps

The UK’s National Cyber Security Centre, the FBI and the Netherlands’ AIVD issued a joint alert on 15 September about CHOSEN BRICK, Windows spyware used by a Tehran-backed actor active since at least 2025 against dissidents and journalists. Delivery is social rather than technical: the operators approach targets on social media posing as a contact or as technical support, then persuade them to install what looks like Pictory, RunwayML, Norton, Telegram or Adobe Flash. Once installed, CHOSEN BRICK harvests contacts, email, social-media accounts and Telegram and WhatsApp data, takes screenshots, records through the microphone, deletes and downloads files, and can wipe the entire system. The agencies note that stolen data has appeared on pro-Iranian leak sites, which puts victims’ physical safety at risk. Separately, Group-IB reported on 17 September that the “Handala Hack” persona is tied to a Telegram-controlled backdoor it calls HEAVYGRAM, and attributes the activity to Void Manticore, a group linked to Iran’s Ministry of Intelligence, with targets including Iran International journalists and opposition groups; its tooling includes a utility that adds Microsoft Defender exclusions. For human-rights groups, diaspora organisations, exile media and the funders and NGOs that work alongside them, this is the threat model in plain terms: no exploit, just a believable message and an installer. The recommended mitigations are ordinary ones — phishing-resistant MFA, application allowlisting, automatic updates and endpoint monitoring — which is exactly why they are worth doing for the people most likely to be approached.

Critical Campaign

A nation-state zero-day chain aimed at NGOs has another user — a further China-linked group sent lures dressed as a US policy institute and a China-focused news site

Volexity disclosed on 9 September, with a detailed public write-up following on 15 September, a spear-phishing campaign that began on 1 September against multiple non-governmental organisations, attributed with moderate confidence to the China-linked actor tracked as UTA0560. The targeting is the point: NGOs were the intended victims, not collateral damage from something aimed elsewhere. Recipients were sent links pointing at a legitimate US university website, which the actor had compromised through a reflected cross-site scripting flaw and used purely as a redirect — so the domain in the email read as academic and trustworthy, and the university itself was infrastructure rather than target. Anyone who followed the link was driven through a chain of three zero-days: CVE-2026-85046 in Chrome’s V8 JavaScript engine and CVE-2026-87491 in its WebAssembly handling to compromise the browser, then CVE-2026-85880, a Windows kernel flaw in ALPC, to take full control of the machine. The payload is GRIMWEDGE, a JScript backdoor that runs in memory inside the Windows Installer process and supports reconnaissance, file and process management and command execution; updated 16 September: a second China-linked actor, tracked as JungleBamboo and associated with APT31, used the same exploit chain to install LONGTALE (also known as GemStone), a credential-stealing Chrome extension. Researchers also note a patch gap — fixes for the browser bugs existed in the open-source Chromium tree before they reached stable Chrome, and the campaign ran inside that window. All three flaws are now patched, and all three are in CISA’s KEV catalog. Updated 24 September: Volexity researchers Damien Cash and Tom Lancaster reported on 21 September that another China-linked cluster, which they track as UTA0565, used the same three exploits on 3–4 September, while the flaws were still zero-days. The exploits were served through a kit Volexity calls “BlueMoon” and installed a new backdoor, CLEANGULP, which can run shell commands, list processes, move files and run beacon object files. The phishing was in Chinese and English, and its content referred to the Hong Kong activist Chow Hang-tung. It led to lookalike sites impersonating the Center for American Progress (americanprgoress[.]top) and China Digital Times (chinadigitaltimes[.]top). The command-and-control domain, thecovnresation[.]com, imitates The Conversation. The named targets in this wave were Asian government entities. The civil-society and media brands were impersonated, and there is no report that those organisations were compromised.
