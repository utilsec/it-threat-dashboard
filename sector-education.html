High Breach

LMU Munich says an attacker reached a server holding student enrolment records — bank account details, health-insurance numbers and student-aid identifiers — and that it must assume the data was taken

Ludwig-Maximilians-Universität München published a data-protection notice on Saturday 19 September 2026 describing unauthorised access to a server that stores the information students supply when they enrol. The categories LMU lists are the ones that make this more than a routine university breach: names, dates and places of birth, gender, postal addresses, phone numbers and LMU email addresses; bank account details (IBAN and account-holder names); and, for some students, health-insurance numbers, BAföG student-aid identifiers, prior educational history and in some cases the reason for a leave of absence. The university’s own wording is that it “must currently assume that this data was in fact retrieved”. Examination records and academic results are held elsewhere and were not affected. LMU says it isolated the server as soon as the intrusion was found, brought in IT forensics specialists, informed the Bavarian state criminal police and the supervisory authorities, expanded security monitoring across other systems and is watching darknet forums for any sign of the data being published; it has seen none so far. Enrolment deadlines have been extended for students affected by the outage. The Record reports that the intrusion was detected the preceding Wednesday and that LMU has not yet determined when the access began or how long it lasted. What is not known matters as much as what is. LMU has not said how many people are affected or how much data was involved, no attacker has been named, and no ransom demand or leak-site listing has been reported. This page does not print a student count and treats the attribution as open. The lesson is structural rather than German. An enrolment system is where a university keeps the data it needs once and then keeps forever: banking details for fee refunds and stipends, insurance numbers for statutory checks, aid identifiers and the personal circumstances behind a leave of absence. It is often an older, internally facing application with broad read access for administrative staff and a long retention tail, and a single server compromise reaches all of it at once.

High Incident

Brevo’s own CDN served fake “verify you are human” pages and a WordPress backdoor for five hours — any school site embedding Brevo forms or chat was carrying it

Brevo, the email-marketing and newsletter platform formerly called Sendinblue, has published a post-mortem of a two-stage compromise. On 10 September attackers abused an improperly scoped SAML single sign-on path to reach 138 customer accounts; contacts were exported from 43 and six were used to send phishing. On 14 September, from about 16:07 to 20:30 UTC, they used a long-lived Cloudflare API key with full account permissions, hard-coded in Brevo’s application source, to deploy a Cloudflare Worker that rewrote responses at the CDN edge. It altered brevo.com, sendinblue.com, the sibforms.com form host and the SDK-loader and Conversations chat scripts customers embed on their own sites, and it stripped Content-Security-Policy headers. Visitors saw fake “Cloudflare, verify you are human” ClickFix prompts asking them to paste and run a command. Visitors logged in as WordPress administrators were pushed a malicious “Web Media Optimizer” plugin that acts as a persistent backdoor. Sansec, which first reported it, estimates more than 100,000 websites were exposed. Brevo says the key was first misused in late August but found no injection before 14 September, that origin servers were untouched, and that the key is revoked and caches purged. The education point is not a claim about how many schools use Brevo — there is no count — but about how school websites are built: sign-up forms, event registration and chat widgets are added by communications or admissions teams as third-party scripts, outside IT’s change process. For five hours that script ran with the full trust of your domain.

The PaperCut campaign has moved from expansion into remediation — maintenance releases have shipped and this week produced methodology reporting, not new victims

For most of the past fortnight this was the story that defined education’s risk picture, and it is worth being explicit about what has changed rather than quietly dropping it. The chained exploitation of CVE-2026-81578, an improper access-control flaw allowing an unauthenticated attacker to change system configuration, and CVE-2026-82078, unsafe dynamic class loading in the database connection utilities allowing arbitrary Java bytecode execution, reached more than 440 compromised PaperCut NG and MF instances across ten countries, with education named as the primary target vertical; BleepingComputer counted 395 confirmed organisations over the same campaign. Both CVEs went into CISA’s Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog on 31 August with a federal deadline of 14 September, now passed, and PaperCut’s first emergency build was bypassed before a working hotfix shipped on 1 September. Neither the 8–15 September window nor the week to 21 September produced new confirmed compromise figures. What it did produce was Dark Reading’s 11 September reporting describing the automated, AI-agent “swarm” methodology behind the campaign as an emerging attack pattern in its own right — full technical detail behind a paywall and not independently verifiable here, so it is reported as methodology analysis rather than as new exploitation. Meanwhile PaperCut has replaced the out-of-band emergency hotfixes with fully QA-tested maintenance releases, which is the normal end-state of an incident of this kind. Two things follow. The exploitation curve is no longer expanding, which is why this page’s threat level comes down this week. And an instance that was internet-facing before 1 September still needs to be hunted rather than ticked off, because patching does not evict an attacker who arrived first.

The critical Citrix NetScaler authentication bypass is now in KEV with 56 documented exploitation attempts — and NetScaler is the campus VPN and virtual-lab gateway

CVE-2026-19490 is a critical authentication bypass in NetScaler ADC and NetScaler Gateway that affects appliances configured as an AAA virtual server or as a Gateway — that is, SSL VPN, ICA Proxy, CVPN or RDP Proxy, which between them describe nearly every way a university publishes remote desktops, virtual labs and off-campus access. Citrix disclosed and patched it on 19 August without flagging exploitation. That changed at the start of this month. On 3 September, sensors operated by Previdian recorded requests matching the public proof-of-concept from three distinct source addresses geolocated to Australia, the United States and Germany, per researcher Ryan Dewhurst; Belgium’s national cybersecurity centre, NCC-BE, issued its own warning. The tally has since grown: CISA added CVE-2026-19490 to the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog on 10 September alongside Cisco Secure FMC and Fortinet, with a 12 September federal remediation deadline, and reporting on that action documents 56 exploitation attempts since early September. Shadowserver tracks more than 22,000 NetScaler ADC appliances and close to 1,700 Gateway instances exposed to the internet, though it cannot see which of those are patched or in a vulnerable configuration. For education the exposure profile is unusually bad in one specific way: a campus NetScaler is deliberately open to the whole world because students and staff connect from anywhere, it fronts single sign-on, and an authentication bypass in front of an SSO estate is an identity problem rather than a network problem. If your appliance was reachable and unpatched at any point since the proof-of-concept became public, the honest posture is to assume session and credential compromise and work backwards from there.

A joint alert from Japan’s National Police Agency and National Cybersecurity Office, the FBI, the US Defense Department’s Cyber Crime Center, Australia’s ACSC and Germany’s BND and BfV sets out the scale of the North Korean campaign tracked as WaterPlum, also known as Contagious Interview. Between December 2025 and July 2026 it infected at least 30,000 devices in more than 100 countries and drained more than 7,000 cryptocurrency wallets of about US$10.5–10.7 million (¥1.7 billion). The agencies attribute it to a North Korean munitions-industry body under the ruling party’s Central Committee. The method is recruitment. Operators pose as AI, crypto or NFT companies on social media, recruiting, gig and freelance platforms and run video interviews using AI face-swap and text-to-speech. Candidates are then asked to complete a coding test: a malicious npm package or VS Code project that installs BeaverTail, InvisibleFerret, OtterCookie or related malware. Stolen identity documents are also used to win real jobs. BleepingComputer adds that Japanese authorities have, for the first time, dismantled a North Korean IT-worker operation inside Japan, with evidence of “several hundred million yen” sent abroad. The stated targets are developers, web designers, engineers, IT staff and crypto specialists. The alert does not name students or universities, and this page does not claim that it does. The education relevance is the target profile. Research software engineers, IT staff and computing graduate students all fit it, and so does anyone on campus using a recruiting or freelance platform to find their next role. A coding test run on a university laptop, lab workstation or research server puts that device’s credentials, SSH keys and source code within reach.

High Threat Actor

A compromised US university website was the redirect in a China-linked Chrome and Windows zero-day chain — the institution was infrastructure, not the target

Volexity published research on 9 September 2026 on China-linked actors chaining browser and Windows zero-days, and one detail belongs on every education risk register: a US university’s public website was used as the redirector. The site had a reflected cross-site scripting flaw, which the attackers used to bounce targets from a trusted .edu address to an exploit chain combining CVE-2026-85046 (V8) and CVE-2026-87491 (WebAssembly) in Chrome with CVE-2026-85880, a Windows kernel ALPC elevation-of-privilege flaw fixed in September’s Patch Tuesday. Volexity tracks two separate users of the chain. UTA0560 targeted US non-governmental organisations with donation-themed phishing and deployed GRIMWEDGE, a JScript backdoor that runs in memory inside msiexec. JungleBamboo, also tracked as APT31 and Violet Typhoon, used the same chain to install LONGTALE, a credential-stealing browser extension. An earlier version of this card treated them as one actor; they are two. Volexity also describes a patch gap: both Chrome bugs were fixed in the open-source Chromium tree before stable Chrome shipped the fixes. The university has not been named, and nothing new has been published since. For the sector, this is a duty-of-care problem rather than a data-loss one. Universities run a sprawling public web estate: departmental and lab sites, conference microsites left running for years, student-society pages and delegated subdomains that nobody owns. Much of it sits outside central IT. A reflected XSS on one of those pages is a low-priority scanner finding and close to worthless to a criminal. An espionage actor values it because it borrows the institution’s reputation: a domain that mail filters trust and a target will click.

Fake “LastPass Authenticator” GitHub pages push an infostealer that loads a Microsoft-signed driver to kill 145 security products — a search-result trap built for students and staff installing software themselves

LastPass’s threat intelligence team and Delphos Labs published on 17 September 2026 an analysis of a campaign they call Rapuncel. Search-engine-ranked GitHub organisations and GitHub Pages sites impersonating at least 40 software brands — the example is a fake “LastPass-Authenticator” organisation — redirect visitors to attacker servers serving ZIP installers padded to 127–148 MB to evade scanning. The installer loads a kernel driver, Alinubx.sys, which is a renamed copy of a legitimate driver from a Chinese disk-encryption product, signed through Microsoft’s Windows Hardware Compatibility Publisher chain in March 2023 and presented as an “NVIDIA File System Filter Driver”. It carries a list of 145 antivirus and EDR process names and terminates them from kernel mode. The stealer then harvests saved passwords from more than 20 browsers, cryptocurrency wallets, Discord, Steam and Telegram sessions, Windows Credential Manager, and any file whose name matches password, seed or recovery patterns. LastPass says the signing certificate had not been revoked and the driver was not on Microsoft’s vulnerable-driver blocklist at the time of writing. No victim count is given. The education relevance is the delivery method. Students install software constantly on personal laptops and bring those laptops onto campus networks and into the institutional single sign-on; staff on unmanaged or lightly managed devices search for an authenticator app when IT tells them to enrol in MFA. A top-ranked GitHub page looks like the safe choice. And a stealer that pulls browser-saved passwords and session cookies is the front end of the account-takeover and session-theft campaigns already on this page.

Medium Advisory

The FBI puts government-impersonation fraud at $1.6 billion in 19 months — and a variant aimed at international students, with fake uniforms and mock offices on video calls, accounts for roughly a tenth of the losses

The FBI’s Internet Crime Complaint Center issued public service announcement PSA260917 on 17 September 2026 on scams in which callers pose as law-enforcement or government officials. Between January 2025 and July 2026 IC3 received nearly 61,000 complaints of this type with losses of more than $1.6 billion. The pattern is a threat of arrest, charges or other official consequence, followed by a demand for payment or for the victim to move money to “protect” it. One variant belongs on this page. IC3 counts 1,809 complaints in which the targets were foreign nationals, including international students, and attributes more than $140 million in losses to it — a small share of complaints and about a tenth of the money. In these cases the caller impersonates the student’s home-country police or a US diplomatic official, threatens extradition or cancellation of the home-country passport, and in some cases appears on video in a fake uniform in front of a set dressed as a government office. A second variant, 3,322 complaints and more than $37 million, tells the victim their professional licence is expiring or has been misused. The FBI’s advice is that no law-enforcement agency will call and demand payment. International students are exposed for practical reasons. They may be newly arrived, unfamiliar with how US agencies actually contact people, dependent on visa status, and reachable through phone numbers and email addresses that appear in university directories and on social media. The losses per case are life-changing for a student and their family, and a student who has been told not to contact anyone will often comply.

Medium Takedown

The FBI has seized NightmareStresser, a DDoS-for-hire service with more than half a million registered users — a disruption, not the end of cheap attack capacity

The FBI and Justice Department have seized the domains of NightmareStresser (nightmare-stresser[.]com and nightmarestresser[.]org) as part of Operation PowerOFF, the international effort against booter and stresser services that has run since December 2018. The service had been active since at least 2022 and is linked to hundreds of thousands of actual or attempted distributed denial-of-service attacks worldwide. It had more than 566,000 registered users by a 2023 count and ran 52 dedicated servers capable of attacks of up to 200 Gbps at both network and application layer. SecurityWeek calls it one of the longest-running booter services; no arrests were reported in the sources reviewed. It is the first visible disruption since the FBI published its disruption-first cyber strategy on 10 September. For schools and universities the reading is practical rather than celebratory. Booter services sell attack capacity to anyone with a payment method and a grievance, and taking one down removes a shop rather than the market; its customers move to the next one. What an education network needs to ride out an attack has not changed. It needs an upstream mitigation arrangement that is already contracted rather than negotiated mid-attack. It also needs a clear list of the services that must stay reachable: learning platforms, online assessment, authentication and the student portal.

High Phishing

Microsoft takes down EvilTokens, a device-code phishing service that took over more than 12,000 Microsoft accounts at 10,000-plus organisations — and higher education is on its target list

Microsoft’s Digital Crimes Unit, working with Health-ISAC, law enforcement and SpyCloud, announced on 22 September 2026 that it had disrupted EvilTokens, a phishing-as-a-service platform run by the actor it tracks as Storm-2992. Active since February 2026 and sold for $500 a month or $1,500 outright, it had compromised more than 12,000 Microsoft accounts at over 10,000 organisations; SpyCloud separately counts 8,708 accounts across 6,585 corporate domains in 79 countries. Two suspected administrators, aged 32 and 38, were arrested in the UK and released on bail. The technique is device-code phishing. The attacker starts a legitimate OAuth device-authorisation request, sends the resulting code in a lure, and the victim enters it on Microsoft’s real sign-in page. That hands the attacker valid tokens, MFA included, because the victim completed the MFA step themselves. Reporting says the service used AI to write lures and triage stolen mailboxes. The sectors named include higher education, alongside wholesale distribution, construction, financial services, real estate and healthcare. A clone called APToken has already appeared, so the disruption removes one operator, not the technique. Campuses are a good fit for it. Tenants are large and federated, device-code sign-in is often left on for lecture-theatre kit, shared displays and research tooling, and a student or staff member who is used to “enter this code” prompts has no reason to be suspicious of one. Pair it with the passkey-enrolment and helpdesk-vishing cards below: all three get around MFA rather than breaking it.

Medium Phishing

Attackers are phishing the passkey enrolment step to take over Microsoft 365 accounts — the cryptography holds, so the registration process is what gets attacked instead

Researchers described two related campaigns against Microsoft 365 and Microsoft cloud accounts on 13 September 2026. One is high-volume and financially motivated: more than a million scam emails aimed at diverting ACH and wire payments. The second is the interesting one. Rather than trying to defeat a passkey, the attacker persuades the user to enrol one — a passkey-themed message walks the victim through adding a new sign-in method, and the method they add belongs to the attacker. Nothing about the FIDO2 cryptography fails; the credential is genuine, the authentication is genuine, and the result is durable account access that survives a password reset and does not trip the alerts built around password compromise. It matters that this targets a control the industry has been recommending precisely because it is phishing-resistant. Resistance to credential phishing is not resistance to social engineering of the enrolment workflow. Education is a natural testing ground for it: universities and districts run some of the largest Microsoft tenants outside the Fortune 500, with populations that turn over annually, include students, adjuncts, contractors and seasonal staff, receive limited security training, and are served by self-service identity workflows designed for scale rather than scrutiny. Many institutions are actively rolling out passkeys this academic year, which means users are being told to expect exactly the message this campaign sends.

High Campaign

Microsoft documents helpdesk vishing that ends in Graph API data theft — the attack shaped for a district or campus service desk

Microsoft researchers, reported by Dark Reading on 10 September 2026, detailed at least two initial-access-broker clusters — tracked as Storm-3032 and Storm-3121 — running voice-phishing campaigns since May 2026. The pattern is worth reading slowly because every step of it is designed around a help desk. The caller impersonates internal IT, directs the target to a personal device rather than a managed one, and sends them to a convincing fake Microsoft sign-in page on a lookalike domain (the example cited is of the form “company.add-passkey.com”). Credentials and session tokens are harvested there, and the attacker then abuses the Microsoft Graph API to enumerate and exfiltrate data from SharePoint, OneDrive and Exchange in small, periodic transfers rather than one visible bulk download. Access is reportedly sold on to extortion groups including ShinyHunters. Microsoft says it has not yet tied these campaigns to a confirmed corporate breach, so treat the outcome as documented technique rather than a named victim. Education is a soft fit for this by design. District and university service desks carry very high call volume at the start of term, they reset passwords for a population that genuinely does forget them, they are frequently staffed by students or part-time technicians, and BYOD is not an exception but the norm for both staff and students. The BYOD step is the clever part: routing the victim to an unmanaged personal device sidesteps conditional-access device compliance and endpoint controls entirely, and the Graph API traffic that follows looks like ordinary Microsoft 365 usage.

It is not the same flaw as CVE-2026-91843 on the Check Point management card further down, which is still not known to be exploited. CVE-2026-94127 (CVSS 9.8) is an unauthenticated heap overflow in F5 BIG-IP APM, exploitable only where an access policy and an OAuth authorisation server profile share a virtual server; F5 confirms exploitation (advisory K000162605). CVE-2026-93952 (CVSS 10.0) is in on-premises Arista VeloCloud Orchestrator, reachable when certificate-based Edge-to-Orchestrator authentication is configured; Arista says it is being actively exploited and has published indicators. WordPress released version 7.1.2 on 22 September 2026 to fix CVE-2026-87902 (CVSS 9.2), an unauthenticated path traversal in core that allows local PHP file inclusion and, in some configurations, remote code execution. It was found by researcher Robert Ressl. The fix is backported to older branches back to 4.7. Code execution needs two things: an active theme with a top-level directory whose name begins with page-, and a readable PHP file on the server an attacker can include — for example pearcmd.php with PHP’s register_argc_argv setting on. The Hacker News notes that this is the default in the official PHP Docker image and in default cPanel PHP builds below 8.5. According to Patchstack, the first malicious requests arrived at 17:44 UTC on 22 September, less than five hours after the release. By 23 September traffic had risen tenfold and moved from reconnaissance to writing files such as wp-pear-rce-flag.php and poc87902.php in /tmp and /var/tmp and running shell commands. Patchstack is the only source for the exploitation data, no count of compromised sites has been published, and the flaw is not reported as added to CISA’s catalogue. It is also a different bug from the earlier Click2Shell fix in 7.1.1, so a site that stopped at 7.1.1 is still exposed. This is the broadest-reach item on this page. A district or university rarely runs one WordPress site. It runs dozens, spread across schools, departments, admissions, alumni, athletics and research groups, and many sit on shared cPanel hosting or containers that match the preconditions. The EU Cyber Resilience Act’s 24-hour reporting duty is now live — and for schools and universities it is a procurement lever rather than a compliance burden

The incident-reporting provisions of the EU Cyber Resilience Act took effect in the week of 11 September 2026. Manufacturers of products with digital elements sold into the EU market must now notify serious security incidents and actively exploited vulnerabilities within a 24-hour window. Educational institutions are overwhelmingly buyers rather than manufacturers, so the obligation itself lands on suppliers — learning platforms, student information systems, interactive classroom displays, campus access control and door systems, network and audio-visual equipment, laboratory instrumentation with embedded software, and the long tail of edtech. That is the point worth extracting. For the first time a substantial part of the sector’s supply chain has a legal deadline to tell someone when a product they sold is being exploited, which is a materially better position than the current norm, where institutions learn about a product vulnerability from a news article or from an incident. The practical move is contractual: the regulation obliges vendors to notify authorities, not automatically to notify you on the same clock, so the notification path to the customer belongs in the contract. Two caveats. The scope is EU-market products, so a purely domestic US supplier may be out of scope entirely — worth establishing rather than assuming. And institutions are not always only buyers: a university spin-out, a research group selling instrumentation, or a department distributing connected hardware into the EU may itself be a manufacturer under the Act.
