Attackers are now exploiting the critical NetScaler authentication bypass — CVE-2026-19490 turns a Gateway or AAA virtual server into unauthenticated access, and roughly 22,000 appliances are still reachable from the internet

Citrix patched CVE-2026-19490 in mid-August and published its advisory on 19 August 2026. The flaw lets an unauthenticated remote attacker bypass authentication when a NetScaler appliance is configured as an AAA virtual server or as a Gateway — SSL VPN, ICA Proxy, CVPN or RDP Proxy — with the exact exposure depending on firmware version and whether a SAML Action is configured. For three weeks that remained a patching problem. It stopped being one on 3 September, when vulnerability-intelligence firm Previdian reported that its NetScaler sensors had picked up exploitation attempts from three distinct IP addresses in Australia, the United States and Germany. A credible proof of concept had been published online shortly before, which is the usual sequence: advisory, PoC, opportunistic scanning, then targeted use. Shadowserver tracks more than 22,000 internet-exposed NetScaler ADC appliances and close to 1,700 Gateway instances, though that count includes patched systems and honeypots and is not a vulnerable-population figure. The reason this sits at the top of the page rather than in the advisory list is what a NetScaler Gateway is: the front door to remote access for a very large share of enterprises, sitting outside the perimeter, terminating sessions for staff and third parties, and holding the session material that makes lateral movement easy. Authentication-bypass bugs in this product line have repeatedly become an initial-access vector for ransomware affiliates within weeks of a proof of concept landing. Belgium’s national CSIRT has already told organisations to prioritise the patch. Note also what patching alone does not do — if a session was hijacked before you upgraded, the fix does not evict the attacker.

Critical Incident — Unpatched zero-day

An unpatched Magento and Adobe Commerce zero-day nicknamed StyleSmuggler is backdooring stores now — no CVE, no advisory and no vendor patch

Dutch e-commerce security firm Sansec published an advisory on 5 September reporting active exploitation of a previously unknown flaw in Magento Open Source and Adobe Commerce, which it named StyleSmuggler. Attacks were observed on 4 and 5 September. The described chain is two-stage: the attacker injects PHP into files that Magento itself generates, then triggers execution through the platform’s Payment Transaction Failed Reminder email function, giving unauthenticated remote code execution and a persistent backdoor. Sansec reports current releases affected, including Magento Open Source 2.4.7, 2.4.8 and 2.4.9, and says instances with the August 2026 updates applied were still compromised. As of this edition Adobe has not issued a CVE identifier, an advisory, a patch or an official workaround; its next scheduled security release is 8 September. That combination — confirmed exploitation, no vendor fix, no CVE to track against — is the worst state a widely deployed application can be in, and it is why this sits second on the radar despite affecting a narrower estate than the NetScaler bypass. Anyone whose organisation runs a storefront, including one owned by marketing or a subsidiary, is in scope. Treat unofficial community patches as risk-managed stopgaps, validated in a test environment, not as vendor-supported fixes.

Critical Incident — Active exploitation

CERT Polska says two MikroTik RouterOS SSH flaws are being chained for unauthenticated administrative takeover — attacks seen since 2 September

CERT Polska issued a warning on 5 September that MikroTik RouterOS vulnerabilities are being exploited in the wild. Two of them combine into what the team calls MikroTrick. CVE-2026-67276, CVSS 9.2, is an SSH authentication bypass caused by improper RSA public-key verification, allowing access without the private key. CVE-2026-86060, also CVSS 9.2, is an SSH privilege escalation reached through a crafted username beginning with a disallowed character, which yields full administrative privileges. Chained against a device with SSH reachable from the internet, the result is complete takeover with no credentials. A third issue, CVE-2026-67277 at CVSS 8.8, leaks kernel memory or restarts the device through the bandwidth-test service, and three further flaws affect the SSH client, X.509 certificate handling and the WebFig interface. MikroTik has published fixes in 7.25beta3, 7.24.2, 7.23.4 and 6.49.21. CERT Polska observed exploitation from at least 2 September, naming 82.192.72.4 as the primary source and 103.102.31.18 as making further attempts, and lists concrete log indicators: SSH login failures for a user named “-2”, creation of an account called “ops”, and unknown users or scripts appearing in the configuration. RouterOS turns up in enterprise networks more often than most inventories admit — branch offices, labs, temporary sites and equipment inherited through acquisition.

Critical Incident — Supply chain

The Shai-Hulud npm worm is still self-propagating and now scans 469 separate credential locations, up from 189

The self-replicating npm worm known as Shai-Hulud remains live and remains the broadest single exposure in the developer ecosystem. The current wave began at 09:00 UTC on 4 August 2026, when a compromised maintainer identity was used to publish malicious versions of widely used packages including keyv, cacheable-request, cache-manager, flat-cache and file-entry-cache. Wiz counted more than 400 distinct packages; Singapore’s CSA put the figure above 1,300 affected versions representing roughly two billion monthly downloads; JFrog later tracked the campaign across 1,700-plus versions. There is no CVE and no patch because nothing was flawed — packages were replaced. The payload runs from a preinstall hook under the Bun runtime, harvests npm tokens, GitHub PATs, cloud keys, Kubernetes secrets and AI service keys, then uses any writable publishing token to push infected releases of every package that identity can reach. It plants execution hooks in repository files such as .vscode/tasks.json and AI assistant hook configurations, and injects workflows to steal GitHub Actions secrets. Command-and-control addresses are read from an Ethereum smart contract, so domain takedowns achieve little. On 3 September GitGuardian reported the credential-scanning logic had grown from 189 paths to 469, now covering .env files, shell history, package-manager configs, CLI caches, CI/CD systems, IDE settings and AI tool configuration. The operators are not breaking trust relationships; they are collecting the credentials already lying around build environments.

High Incident — Mass campaign

More than 5,400 hacked websites are serving ClickFix lures whose payloads live on a public blockchain, making takedown effectively impossible

Netskope reported that over 5,400 compromised websites, mostly WordPress and PrestaShop installations, have been injected with scripts that fetch their payloads from the BNB Smart Chain testnet using the EtherHiding technique. Because the testnet is free to use and behaves like a production chain, the attackers get resilient, unremovable payload hosting with no infrastructure to seize. Visitors see a fake CAPTCHA that instructs them to open the Windows Run dialog and paste a PowerShell command — the ClickFix pattern — which pulls the malware down. Netskope tracked the campaign back to spring 2026, with roughly 400 infected sites contacting BNB Smart Chain endpoints daily by August and a peak of 536. The payload has since evolved from a straightforward downloader into a WebRTC data-channel stager that constructs its own answer rather than completing a normal handshake, establishing a covert encrypted channel for remote code execution that does not look like web traffic. Two defensive implications follow. First, ClickFix defeats every control that assumes the user has to be tricked into opening a file — the user is the execution engine, so the countermeasures are user education and restricting the Run dialog and PowerShell for standard users. Second, the payload channel is neither HTTP nor DNS, so egress monitoring that only watches those two will not see the second stage.

High Research — Cloud identity

Datadog tracked a month-long password-spraying campaign against AWS root user accounts at more than 150 organisations

Datadog Security Research published findings on 31 August describing a campaign that ran from 24 July to 23 August 2026 and attempted to authenticate as the AWS root user across more than 150 organisations, with no clear industry or geographic focus. The technique was deliberately quiet: a small set of common or previously exposed passwords tried against many accounts, which keeps each account below lockout thresholds. The median organisation saw two failed attempts; some saw as many as eight. Requests came from geographically dispersed IP addresses across multiple autonomous systems using proxy, residential-proxy or hosting infrastructure, with spoofed user agents impersonating Microsoft Edge on Chrome 85 and Firefox 120. Datadog identified no successful authentications. That is the useful part of this report rather than a disappointment: it shows attackers systematically working through root email addresses they have harvested or guessed, and it means the detection is available to you before the compromise rather than after. Root is the one identity in an AWS account that cannot be constrained by IAM policy, so a single success is a total loss of that account.

The stopgap funding bill extends the Cybersecurity Information Sharing Act of 2015 into early December — the fourth short-term rescue of a law nobody wants to renew permanently

A continuing resolution passed by the House and previously approved by the Senate extends the Cybersecurity Information Sharing Act of 2015 through the stopgap funding period into early December 2026. The law is what gives companies liability protection when they share cyber threat indicators with government agencies and with each other, and without it many corporate legal departments will not approve participation in information-sharing arrangements at all. Its recent history is a study in how fragile that foundation is: it briefly lapsed during last autumn’s shutdown, lapsed again earlier before being extended to 30 September, and is now carried again on the back of a funding bill rather than reauthorised on its own terms. Industry associations continue to press for a permanent solution, while Senator Rand Paul has blocked bipartisan reauthorisation efforts pending free-speech provisions he wants included. Federal News Network notes that the Treasury Department’s new GOLD EAGLE cybersecurity clearinghouse depends on these protections. For an enterprise, the practical exposure is that the legal basis for your ISAC participation and your threat-intelligence sharing agreements has a three-month expiry date attached to a budget negotiation.

Policy development — not tied to a vulnerability or active exploitation Not applicable
Legal and governance matter, not a patchable issue Action: Ask your legal team whether any of your threat-sharing arrangements — ISAC or ISAO membership, sector working groups, bilateral agreements with peers, submissions to government partners — rely on CISA 2015 liability protection, and what the position would be during a lapse. Agree in advance what you would keep sharing and what you would pause, so the answer is not improvised in December. If sharing genuinely matters to your defensive posture, that is worth saying to your trade association while the reauthorisation debate is live.

France’s CNIL fines a private hospital €500,000 — and half the penalty is about who was never told, not how the attacker got in

On 3 September 2026 the CNIL fined Hôpital Privé de la Loire €500,000 following a breach in which an attacker accessed the hospital’s computerised patient record system in summer 2025, exposing data on 524,867 patients and 202,246 designated trusted third parties. The regulator found violations of GDPR Article 32 on security and Article 34 on communicating a breach to data subjects. The security failings named are notable for how ordinary they are: authentication without VPN or multi-factor protection, access controls that let a single user’s credentials reach every patient record, no real-time monitoring for suspicious activity, and consequently no detection of an extraction that ran over several days. The Article 34 finding is the one worth carrying into your own planning — the hospital notified affected patients but did not notify the 202,246 trusted third parties whose data was also involved, which the CNIL held deprived those individuals of information they needed to understand the incident. Breach notification is routinely scoped to the obvious population of data subjects, and secondary populations such as emergency contacts, guarantors, referees, next of kin and beneficiaries are exactly the ones a hurried notification exercise leaves out.

Regulatory enforcement following a 2025 intrusion; no current exploitation activity Not applicable
Compliance and control-design matter, not a patchable issue Action: Two concrete tests. First, on access control: pick your most sensitive record system and check whether one ordinary user account can retrieve the entire population, and whether anything alerts when a single account reads records at bulk volume — that combination is what turned one compromised credential into half a million records here. Second, on notification: list every category of person whose data sits in that system, not just the primary data subjects, and confirm your incident-response plan can identify and contact the secondary categories. Verify MFA is enforced on all remote access to clinical and equivalent record systems, including vendor and contractor paths.

CISA still expects to finalise the CIRCIA incident-reporting rule this month — 72 hours for incidents, 24 hours for ransom payments

CISA has targeted September 2026 for publication of the final rule implementing the Cyber Incident Reporting for Critical Infrastructure Act of 2022. As proposed, covered entities in critical-infrastructure sectors would have to report substantial cyber incidents to CISA within 72 hours and any ransom payment within 24 hours. The rule has slipped repeatedly: the statutory deadline was October 2025, CISA’s own prior internal target was May 2026, and the agency held town-hall consultations in June 2026 with more than 1,200 participants before settling on the current timeline. Final thresholds for who counts as a covered entity — based on the sector and size criteria set out in the April 2024 proposed rule — remain pending, which is precisely the detail most organisations need in order to know whether they are in scope. Carried here as still-current rather than new: the September target was set in July and had not been superseded as of this edition. The reason to prepare regardless of the publication date is that a 72-hour clock is short if nobody has decided in advance who declares an incident reportable, who writes the report and who signs it. Organisations already reporting under NIS2, DORA, SEC disclosure rules or state regulations should be mapping CIRCIA against those obligations now rather than building a separate process.

Pending regulation — not tied to a vulnerability or active exploitation Not applicable
Compliance readiness, not a patchable issue Action: Do the parts that do not depend on the final text: define who has authority to declare an incident reportable, who drafts and approves a regulatory notification, and how you would assemble the facts within 72 hours of determining a substantial incident. Build one incident-reporting matrix covering every regime you are subject to rather than a separate runbook per regulator. If you may be a covered entity, watch for the final rule’s covered-entity thresholds and compliance date as soon as it publishes.
