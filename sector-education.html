Top Story

PaperCut print-server flaws move from probing to hands-on intrusions — and no product sits deeper in school and campus estates

PaperCut disclosed a zero-day in its NG and MF print-management products on 27 August, and by the end of the month the activity around it had changed character. WatchTowr’s Jake Knott described attackers who are no longer running exploratory probes but working interactively on compromised servers — installing remote-access tooling, attempting to pivot into the surrounding network, and in some cases restricting further access so rival crews cannot use the same foothold. Two flaws are involved, CVE-2026-82078 and CVE-2026-81578, chained to bypass authentication and reach remote code execution; CISA added both to the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog on 31 August with a 14 September federal deadline. Shadowserver counts more than 1,000 PaperCut instances reachable from the internet. PaperCut shipped emergency patches, then had to ship a second round after the first fix was bypassed, and a consolidated release covering both issues was still in development as of 30 August. This leads the education page for a specific reason: print management is one of the few categories where schools, colleges and universities are the core market rather than an afterthought. PaperCut servers are near-universal in K-12 districts and higher-ed IT, they authenticate against directory services to enforce per-student quotas and charge accounts, they hold user directories and job metadata, and they are routinely stood up by desktop or print teams rather than by security. That combination — domain-joined, credential-adjacent, widely exposed, and rarely owned by anyone whose job is patching — is exactly the profile attackers are working right now, in the first weeks of term when print volume and helpdesk load are at their annual peak.

Read these carefully. Leak-site claims are sometimes inflated, occasionally recycled from an earlier intrusion, and very occasionally fabricated. What the cluster does tell you is directional rather than specific: mid-tier districts and small public colleges — the ones with a two- or three-person IT team and no dedicated security staff — are being worked steadily by mid-tier ransomware affiliates, and the posting rate did not slow when term started. Breachsense also notes large volumes of institutional credentials for these domains circulating in infostealer logs and combo lists, several hundred per institution with plaintext passwords, which is the ordinary way these intrusions begin.

Citrix NetScaler authentication bypass went from advisory to live exploitation on 3 September, and campus remote access is the classic deployment

CVE-2026-19490 is a critical authentication bypass in NetScaler appliances configured as AAA virtual servers or as Gateways — that is, in the SSL VPN, ICA Proxy, CVPN and RDP Proxy roles. Citrix published its advisory (CTX696939) in August. Exploitation began after a proof-of-concept became public: researcher Ryan Dewhurst reported that on 3 September one of his NetScaler sensors logged requests matching the PoC from three distinct source addresses geolocated to Australia, the United States and Germany. Shadowserver sees more than 22,000 NetScaler ADC appliances and close to 1,700 Gateway instances exposed, patch state unknown. CISA and Belgium’s national cybersecurity centre have both urged immediate patching. For education this is a familiar shape and a bad one. NetScaler Gateway is the standard front door for staff VPN, published desktops and virtual labs at a large number of universities and larger districts, and it is precisely the sort of appliance that gets a change-freeze exemption in September because taking it down locks everyone out during the first weeks of term. An authentication bypass on that device is pre-authentication access to whatever sits behind it, and historically NetScaler compromises have been followed by session-token theft that survives a later patch. Patching alone is not sufficient here.

High Active Exploitation

440,000 blocked exploit attempts against two WordPress form plugins — and departmental sites are where education runs WordPress

Wordfence reported blocking more than 440,000 exploitation attempts against two unauthenticated arbitrary-file-upload flaws. CVE-2026-14894 (CVSS 9.8) in Super Forms – Drag & Drop Form Builder has drawn over 250,000 attempts since 14 July, peaking on 18 August with more than 40,000 requests in a day; it is fixed in 6.3.314. CVE-2026-32475 in Elementor Pro has drawn over 190,000 attempts beginning 19 August — the same day the fix shipped in 4.2.2. The Elementor technique submits a form file-upload field as an array whose first element is empty and whose second carries a PHP payload with a .php filename, which slips past validation; the resulting webshell lands in /wp-content/uploads/elementor/forms/ under a random name. Super Forms is exploited through base64-encoded payloads to /wp-admin/admin-ajax.php. Elementor Pro alone has over six million active installs. Education is unusually exposed to this class of bug, not because central IT runs WordPress badly but because it often does not run it at all: admissions microsites, department and lab pages, conference sites, student-organisation sites and faculty pages accumulate outside the managed estate, on hosting nobody has inventoried, with a plugin set nobody patches. Any of those with a published form containing a file-upload field is a candidate for remote code execution on a domain that carries your institution’s name and, quite often, its SSO cookie scope.

Two accounts of this incident went on the record in mid-August and, as of 4 September, neither has been reconciled publicly. Second, a day-one “no evidence data was accessed” statement is a snapshot of incomplete forensics, not a finding, and it reads as a reversal when a state agency corrects it a day later. Write holding statements that survive that.

High Threat Intel

The Shai-Hulud worm now sweeps 469 credential locations, and research computing is full of all of them

GitGuardian documented a new variant of the Shai-Hulud credential-stealing worm that scans 469 distinct locations across developer environments, CI/CD tooling, cloud configuration files and AI-tool configs — up from 189 paths in earlier builds. The design goal the researchers describe is worth restating plainly: the worm is not trying to break trust relationships, it is trying to collect the credentials that already make those relationships work. It prioritises package-publishing tokens, because a stolen publishing credential lets the operator push a poisoned package into a registry that downstream consumers already trust, and the infection propagates forward through supply chains rather than sideways through networks. Universities are an awkward fit for the standard advice here. A research group’s pipeline typically has cloud credentials with real spending authority, registry tokens for internally published packages, long-lived SSH keys to HPC clusters, and increasingly API keys for hosted models — often in a repository owned by a postdoc who will graduate, on a runner nobody in central IT administers, with secrets in a dotfile because that is what worked. Central IT usually has no inventory of these environments and no mandate over them. The practical consequence is that a single compromised student laptop or a single malicious dependency in a lab’s requirements file can hand an attacker credentials that reach institutional cloud accounts and shared compute.

High Threat Intel

A 46-country phishing campaign that installs legitimate remote-management software names education among its top target industries

ANY.RUN tracked a phishing operation that does not deliver malware in the usual sense: it persuades the recipient to install a genuine remote monitoring and management agent, which then gives the operator hands-on control of the endpoint using signed, allowlisted software. The analysts linked 601 cases across 46 countries; the United States accounts for about 45% of observed activity, and education is listed alongside technology, government, banking and manufacturing among the sectors being hit. The lures are localised — Canada Revenue Agency tax forms in the original Canadian cluster, then fake shipping and UPS notices, Adobe PDF prompts, Social Security Administration themes and invoices elsewhere — and payloads arrive in password-protected archives to defeat mail scanning. Infrastructure rotates fast: pages are hosted on Vercel, GitHub Pages and Netlify, with delivery from compromised sites, Amazon S3, Cloudflare R2, DigitalOcean Spaces, Dropbox and GoFile, and 94% of observed hosts appeared for a single day. That last detail is why blocklists will not carry you here. The education problem is specific: schools and universities legitimately run several RMM tools at once — one for the Windows fleet, one for Macs, one a managed service provider brought in, one a department bought — so a new agent appearing on a machine does not look anomalous to anyone. Business-office and bursar staff, who get invoice and shipping mail all day, are the natural landing point.

High Threat Intel

Carried forward: DOJ charges 17 in an IRGC-linked campaign to steal research from American universities

The Justice Department charged 17 people over a coordinated operation that prosecutors attribute to an organisation linked to Iran’s Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps, alleging a sustained effort to steal research from American universities, companies and government agencies. It remains on this page in September because the tradecraft it describes is not historical and is not patchable. Campaigns of this type run on credential phishing aimed at named faculty — messages that reference a real paper, a real conference or a real collaboration, sent to an academic whose research interests, co-authors and email address are all published by the institution itself. The credential is then used to walk out through the library proxy and download journal content and datasets at volume, which is why these intrusions are often first noticed by a publisher’s abuse team rather than by campus security. Two structural facts make universities hard to defend here. Openness is the product: directories, syllabi, grant abstracts and preprints are meant to be public, and the same information is perfect targeting material. And faculty accounts sit outside the normal endpoint estate more often than any other user population, on personal machines, at conferences, and on collaborator networks abroad. An indictment does not change any of that; it just documents what the payoff looks like.

PaperCut NG/MF: two chained flaws in CISA KEV with a 14 September deadline, and the first hotfix was bypassed

The technical detail behind the top story. CVE-2026-82078 and CVE-2026-81578 affect PaperCut NG and MF and are used together: the pair allows an unauthenticated attacker to bypass authentication and achieve remote code execution on the print server. PaperCut disclosed the initial zero-day on 27 August and shipped emergency patches; a second patch became necessary when the first fix was bypassed, and as of 30 August a consolidated official release addressing both issues was still in development. Shadowserver counts more than 1,000 PaperCut instances exposed to the internet. Two operational points matter more than the CVE numbers. First, verify which build you are actually running — an estate patched in the first 48 hours may be sitting on the bypassed hotfix and believe itself covered. Second, the exposure count is the real finding: a print server has no business being internet-reachable, and every one of those thousand-plus instances represents an organisation that either intended it (for remote or mobile printing) or never noticed. In education, the second case is common, because PaperCut is frequently deployed per-site or per-school rather than centrally, and the servers outlive the staff who built them. Treat this as an inventory exercise first and a patching exercise second.

Seven KEV additions on 2 September, and the education-relevant ones are the phone system and the artifact repository

CISA added seven actively exploited vulnerabilities to the KEV catalog on 2 September, with remediation deadlines of 5 and 16 September. Alongside the two SonicWall SMA1000 flaws covered separately, the batch includes CVE-2026-9586, an unauthenticated SQL injection in Sangoma Switchvox rated 9.3; CVE-2026-82329, an improper-authentication flaw in JFrog Artifactory rated 9.8; CVE-2026-48710, a request-smuggling issue in Starlette rated 6.5 and chained with CVE-2026-42271; CVE-2026-49869, a maximum-severity command injection in Kestra OSS; and CVE-2026-59822, an improper-authentication flaw in Berri LiteLLM rated 8.8. Two deserve attention on a campus or district estate. Switchvox is a VoIP phone system: the flaw sits in the /pa HTTP endpoint, which parses XML phone notifications and concatenates the PhoneIP field straight into an unparameterised SQL query, giving unauthenticated remote code execution; it is fixed in 8.4.0.2, released 14 July, and roughly 4,000 exposed devices are visible on Shodan, mostly in the United States. Schools run phone systems and rarely patch them on a security cadence. Artifactory matters wherever a research computing or central development group runs a self-managed instance — the flaw lets an unauthenticated attacker on the network forge administrative tokens, which is a supply-chain problem, not just a server problem, because it allows replacement of trusted artifacts. JFrog patched on 28 August across the 7.111.21, 7.117.28, 7.125.20, 7.133.29, 7.146.38 and 7.161.20 branches; cloud instances were already protected.

Chrome V8 zero-day exploited in the wild — the sixth this year, on the browser that is the whole endpoint in most classrooms

Google patched CVE-2026-85046, a type-confusion flaw in the V8 JavaScript and WebAssembly engine, and acknowledged that an exploit exists in the wild. Type confusion means the engine misinterprets one object type as another, corrupting memory in a way that a crafted HTML page with malicious JavaScript can steer toward code execution inside the renderer sandbox. Fixed builds are 152.0.7977.82/.83 on Windows and macOS and 152.0.7977.82 on Linux; the release also closed nine other high-severity issues across crash reporting, networking and graphics. Google withheld technical detail to buy patching time, and credited the report to Salvatore Gulizia. This is the sixth actively exploited Chrome bug fixed since January 2026. The education angle is scale rather than novelty. In K-12 in particular the browser is not one application among many — it is the entire student computing environment, and a managed Chromebook fleet numbering in the thousands per district updates on a channel and a reboot cadence that IT controls only loosely, because the device belongs to a student who may not restart it for weeks. Verify enforcement rather than assuming it: set the minimum version policy in your admin console, check the reported version distribution across the fleet, and identify the tail of devices that has not taken the update. On the staff side, the same check applies to Chrome and Chromium-based Edge on managed laptops.

High Advisory

Gitea remote code execution added to KEV — the self-hosted code platform departments stand up without telling anyone

CISA added CVE-2026-60004 to the KEV catalog on 25 August with a 28 August federal deadline, after exploitation was observed in the wild. The flaw lets an attacker who already has repository write access send a malicious patch to the diffpatch API endpoint, planting an executable Git hook that runs shell commands as the Gitea service account. It is fixed in Gitea 1.27.1, released in late July 2026. Attribution and objectives are unclear, and there were no prior public exploitation reports. A separate Gitea flaw, CVE-2026-20896, was exploited in early July but has not been added to KEV. The write-access precondition is a real constraint, and it is also why this belongs on an education page rather than being dismissed. Self-hosted Gitea is popular in exactly the settings where write access is handed out liberally: a research group’s server, a computer-science department’s teaching instance where every student in a course gets a repository, a lab VM someone spun up to avoid a licence cost. On a teaching instance, “an account with repository write access” describes an entire cohort. The service account then gives shell access to a host that frequently sits on a research network with visibility into cluster storage and other lab systems. Find these instances first; the patch is trivial once you know where they are.

Education Department guidance treats student surveys and mental-health screenings as requiring written parental consent

The Education Department’s Student Privacy Policy Office issued a Dear Colleague letter in late August setting out its interpretation of the Protection of Pupil Rights Amendment and FERPA. The core position is that mental-health screenings and student surveys must be treated as required activities subject to parental notification and written consent, even when the district presents them as optional, on the reasoning that students commonly feel obliged to participate when an authority figure administers a survey. Under the guidance, districts must give parents an advance opportunity to review survey content and obtain written consent; a simple opt-out procedure is stated not to satisfy the requirement. The letter also addresses parental access to student information, including material some districts have treated as restricted. This page takes no position on the policy; the reason it appears here is that the operational burden falls largely on IT and data teams. Guidance of this kind converts a workflow question into a records question: which platform collects the survey, where the responses are stored, whether the SIS can record and evidence consent per student per instrument, how a parent request to review content is served and logged, and what the retention and deletion rules are for responses collected under an older process. Screening and social-emotional-learning tools are usually third-party SaaS, so the answer often sits in a vendor’s configuration rather than yours. Read the letter and inventory the tools before the first survey of the term goes out.
